On a boat to Crete, an English mine owner asks a Greek whether he has ever married. The Greek lists his wife, his children and his house, and finishes with two words that a stress clinic would later borrow for the title of its book.

[[story]]
The scene is from the 1964 film *Zorba the Greek*. Basil, a bookish English mine owner played by Alan Bates, asks Anthony Quinn's Zorba whether he is married. Zorba's answer runs through wife, children, house, everything, and ends with the full catastrophe. The line is not in Nikos Kazantzakis's 1946 novel, where Zorba calls marriage the Great Folly and says he fell headlong into the ditch, like everyone before him. The screenplay folded two scenes from the book into the one on the boat. The film's director and screenwriter, Michael Cacoyannis, said in a 2011 interview that he chose the non-idiomatic phrase on purpose, for humor and impact.

Jon Kabat-Zinn took the phrase as the title of his 1990 book, which grew out of the clinic at the University of Massachusetts. His editor on the first edition worried that the word catastrophe might repel readers from the start. The phrase kept coming back to him, he has said, and he kept it. Elsewhere the phrase mostly stayed a joke about marriage and family life, in titles such as a 1990 comic novel and a 1996 song by John Mellencamp.

Wikipedia summarises what he made of it as a redefinition: an affirmation of life's variety and chaos, to be embraced and not lamented. He reads Zorba's answer as a deep appreciation of life and its dilemmas. Among the attitudes the book builds on in Part I are acceptance and letting go.

A small example from the chapters on pain shows the idea at work. Kabat-Zinn suggests seeing a headache as just a headache rather than as "my headache." The pain is not wished away, and it does not collect a story about the person who has it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Name the whole situation, hard parts included.** Zorba lists his wife, children and house without complaint, and Kabat-Zinn reads that listing as a yes to life. A shop owner in a crowded week could say aloud what the week contains, tiring parts and all, before trying to fix any of it. Seeing the whole picture makes it easier to choose which job to take first and to stop blaming herself for the rest.$$

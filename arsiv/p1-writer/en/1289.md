Nineteen hours into a 24-hour race in San Diego, David Goggins stopped running. He had covered the distance he came for with five hours still on the clock, and almost nothing in his training suggested he should have got that far.

[[story]]
In 2005 Goggins entered a 24-hour race in San Diego called the San Diego One Day. Before he could apply for Badwater 135, a 135-mile race through Death Valley, he needed a 100-mile race on his record, and he signed up within a day of learning that. He was a former Navy SEAL, but according to a profile in *Trail Runner* his training came to about twenty minutes of running every other day, and he had never raced a marathon.

The goal was one hundred miles. He ran 101, roughly 163 kilometres, in about nineteen hours. Nineteen hours of running on a body used to twenty-minute sessions. Five hours were left on the clock, and the record he needed was his.

Several bones in his feet broke during the race. Afterwards his kidneys ached and he passed blood. Asking a lot of his body was nothing new: he had already lost 106 pounds in three months to become eligible for SEAL training.

He later finished Badwater too. In *Can't Hurt Me* he reads moments like these through what he calls the 40% Rule. When your mind tells you that you are done, you are only about 40 percent done, and most people quit with most of their capacity untouched. The first wave of pain and tiredness that makes them stop he calls the governor, the part of an engine that caps its speed.

##The wish to stop and the real limit may sit in different places.##

A caution belongs here. The 40 percent is not a laboratory figure. It is Goggins's own name and estimate, drawn from his experience, and nobody should turn it into a training plan. It rests on his experience, not on measurement. What stays useful is the narrower claim that the feeling of "enough" can arrive before the body's actual limit.

[[lessons]]
$$**Check the urge to stop with one more try.** When your mind says "enough", it reports how you feel at that moment and says little about how much you have left. A student who wants to shut the book after a long evening of revision can read one more page and then ask again. Often the tiredness is still there, yet the task is still doable. That small test lets you tell "I want to stop" from "I have to stop", and you say the second only after you have tried.$$

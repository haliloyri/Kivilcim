[[story]]
Visitors to Petrified Forest National Park in Arizona kept taking pieces of petrified wood home. The park's most obvious fix was a warning sign telling visitors that many people had already taken wood from the park. The aim was to show how serious the problem was.

Robert Cialdini and his colleagues tested signs like that in the field. According to *Influence*, they placed pieces of petrified wood, secretly marked, along visitor paths and counted how many disappeared under each sign. Counting marked pieces meant nobody had to guess how many visitors had taken wood. The count did not depend on what anyone said, only on what happened to the wood. A piece lifted from a path left its mark behind.

Where the sign said many visitors took wood, with a picture of several people picking pieces up, the number of vanished pieces rose to nearly three times the level on a path with no sign. The warning condemned the theft and made it look ordinary at the same time. Everyone passing by read the same message: lots of people do this.

A plain sign that simply asked visitors not to remove the wood did better, and fewer pieces went missing. It was the same park, the same wood and the same paths. Only the words on the sign had changed.

Cialdini calls the effect negative social proof. In his reading, a message can do two different jobs: it can show what other people actually do, or it can say what should be done. The sign did the first, and the simple request did the second, which got the park what it was after. This was one field test in one park, and the book does not claim the same result for every situation.

[[lessons]]
$$**Say the behaviour you want, and leave the common bad behaviour out.** A café owner who puts up "most customers leave their mugs on the counter" may make leaving mugs look normal. A sign reading "Please return your mug to the tray" tells people exactly what to do. When you explain a rule, even a widespread problem is better left unquantified, and a clear request lowers the chance that your reminder works against you. The next notice you write can name the action you want, in one line, and leave the headcount out of it.$$

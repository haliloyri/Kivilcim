A patient comes in with headaches and a head full of fog. The neurologist does not reach for a prescription pad first. He asks about bread. It sounds like a strange place to begin, but he has a reason.

[[story]]
Neurologist David Perlmutter opens *Grain Brain* with an unexpected suspect: the gluten in bread, pasta and cake. These are some of the most ordinary foods on the table, the morning roll, the lunchtime pasta, the evening pastry. Gluten is a protein found in wheat, barley and rye. In coeliac disease, where gluten sets the immune system against the person's own gut, it is a known problem. Perlmutter goes further than that.

He argues that gluten sensitivity does not have to stay in the gut. It can show up in the nervous system, he says, and it can happen in people who do not have coeliac disease. His proposed mechanism is inflammation. Inflammation is the body's response to injury or to something foreign, and short bursts of it help while long ones damage tissue. Perlmutter's suggestion is that inflammation driven by gluten can reach the brain.

The method he recommends is plain: take gluten out of your diet for a set period, then watch for changes in headaches, mood, energy or focus. ##The test needs a kitchen, not a laboratory.##

A distinction matters here. That coeliac disease can affect the nervous system is accepted in medicine. The claim that gluten harms the brain in people without coeliac disease is debated, and it should be read as Perlmutter's own view. Anyone thinking about a big change in diet does well to talk to a doctor first.

The practical idea stands apart from the argument. If you suspect that something affects you, you can remove it for a defined time and write down what happens. The writing matters, because memory tends to blur such changes, and one tired week can easily end up blamed on a food.

[[lessons]]
$$**Test a food with a trial instead of a hunch.** When you suspect that a food is affecting you, a short and clearly defined trial is far more reliable than guessing. Someone who feels foggy every afternoon could write down what they eat and how they feel for two weeks, then change one thing only. That way the answer comes from your own notes rather than from a friend's opinion, and you have something concrete to bring to your doctor.$$

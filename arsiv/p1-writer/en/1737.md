Each afternoon a teenage girl came home from school and went looking for something to throw away. She had been trying tidying methods since childhood and her home still would not stay tidy. She was asking a question that pointed the wrong way, and for years she did not see it.

[[story]]
In *The Life-Changing Magic of Tidying Up*, Marie Kondo describes being fascinated by tidying from childhood. She moved from one method to the next, always hoping this time the house would stay in order. As a teenager she understood tidying as discarding, and after school each day she searched through belongings for things to throw out.

The pace wore her down. By her own account, she had a breakdown at around fifteen, lost consciousness, and on waking heard a voice telling her to look at her things more carefully.

In that moment she saw what she had been doing wrong. For years she had thought only about what to get rid of. She had never asked which belongings she wanted to keep. Everything she owned had looked like a candidate for the bin.

##She switched from asking what to throw away to asking what to keep.##

That question was wrong in a quiet way, because someone hunting for things to discard looks at each object for flaws. A person who asks what to keep looks at the same object for value. The first approach tires you out, and the second gives you something to choose.

Her method grew from this. According to the book, you gather every item in a category, for example all your clothes, into one pile on the floor. Then you pick each one up in your hands and ask whether it sparks joy. What does stays, and the rest goes.

Kondo recommends tackling it by category rather than room by room, in one concentrated push. She suggests beginning with clothes and finishing one category before starting the next. Tidying became less an act of removal and more a choice about what to live with.

[[lessons]]
$$**Choose what stays, rather than hunting for what can go.** A person clearing a kitchen cupboard who only asks "can I throw this out?" may find everything suspect and end up exhausted. Kondo's way of holding each item and asking whether to keep it moves attention from flaws to value, and that makes each choice simpler to see. You can ask the same of a task list or a calendar, and decide first what you would choose again today.$$

Some adult children keep waiting for their parent to turn around one day and approve of them. Lindsay Gibson has a name for that waiting, and a different suggestion for what to do with the time.

[[story]]
In *Adult Children of Emotionally Immature Parents*, Gibson calls it a healing fantasy. Many adult children go on believing that the parent will eventually change and give the validation and love they never got. The belief can last for years, and as long as it does, the person's attention stays on the parent.

The book describes another way children cope: building a dream world. A child pictures a better future, and the picture helps them get through the present. Carried into adulthood, the same kind of dream can set up unrealistic expectations, and Gibson gives the example of a marriage imagined as effortless.

Her first step for healing points somewhere else. It is to connect with your authentic self and your true feelings. This step does not depend on the parent changing, so a person can take it today.

The next step concerns the relationship itself. She suggests aiming to relate to the parent instead of being embedded in the relationship, and she recommends entering each interaction with a clear goal. A goal of that kind has to be something you can reach on your own, whatever the parent does.

She backs it with an exercise. You reflect on a past bad conversation and identify the goal you could have brought to it. Replaying the scene that way moves the question from what the parent should have done to what you wanted.

The book sorts these parents into four types: emotional, driven, passive and rejecting. Each puts a child's needs second in a different way, so seeing which type a relative resembles helps in judging what can reasonably be expected from them.

Understanding why the parent behaves the way they do also plays a part. According to Gibson, that understanding can guide how much you engage, which leaves the decision about the depth of contact with you.

[[lessons]]
$$**Go into a hard conversation with a goal of your own.** A conversation entered in hope that the other person will change usually ends in disappointment, because the hoped-for response does not arrive. A man who phones his father every week wanting approval and hangs up empty-handed could decide this time to end the call without an argument, which gives him a target he controls. If that goal works, he can plan the next call on his own terms in the same way.$$

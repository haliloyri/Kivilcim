[[story]]
At Stanford's Bing Nursery School in the late 1960s and early 1970s, Walter Mischel's team led preschool children into a small room that *The Marshmallow Test* calls the Surprise Room. On the table was a treat, a marshmallow or a cookie, a little pretzel or a mint. The child could eat it now, or wait for the researcher to return and get a bigger reward.

A bell sat within reach. Ringing it brought the researcher back and ended the wait, and the children who dwelt on the treat's tempting qualities rang it soon. The researcher watched through a one-way window.

Others found ways to wait. They turned their backs on the treat, covered their eyes, sang quietly to themselves, played with their toes, explored their noses and ears, invented games, pushed the bell away, repeated the rule aloud, or fell asleep. One child sang a line about home in Redwood City. Covering the treat made waiting easier too.

Mischel then varied the setup. A life-sized photo of the treat roughly doubled the average wait, and so did telling children to picture the real treat as a photo. When they were told the picture was real, the effect reversed. Children asked to think of the treat as just a picture waited almost 18 minutes. Mischel separates the treat's hot qualities, like its taste, which push toward eating, from cool ones, like being white and soft, which help waiting.

He used the same idea himself when he quit smoking: whenever he craved a cigarette, he pictured a cancer patient receiving radiation. His reading of the scenes is that waiting depends on where attention goes, and that children can learn strategies for it.

Later, larger studies found a smaller link between waiting and later outcomes once family background was taken into account. The link is weaker than the first reports suggested.

[[lessons]]
$$**Move the temptation out of sight instead of staring it down.** Someone who wants to stop snacking at their desk can put the biscuit tin in a cupboard in another room, rather than resisting it each time it catches their eye. Each trip to the cupboard is a pause the open tin never gave. What is out of sight stops being a standing invitation, so there is less to resist in the first place. Arranging what you see once lets you spend your effort on the work, not on the same pull again and again.$$

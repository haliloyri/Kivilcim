[[open]]
A dot circles a clock face, electrodes sit on a scalp, and a volunteer flexes a wrist whenever the urge arrives and says where the dot was. One book draws a hard conclusion from that setup. The scientist who built it was more careful.

[[story]]
The dot on Benjamin Libet's clock went around once every 2.56 seconds. In his 1983 study, volunteers flexed a wrist whenever they felt like it and reported the clock position when they first felt the urge. Scalp electrodes recorded the readiness potential, a slow negative shift in voltage.

The readiness potential began about 550 milliseconds before the flex. The reported urge came about 200 milliseconds before it, a gap of roughly 350. Six volunteers took part, forty trials each. The paper appeared in the journal Brain, by Libet, Gleason, Wright and Pearl.

Yuval Noah Harari, in the chapter "The Time Bomb in the Laboratory" of Homo Deus, calls free will a factual claim that conflicts with science, the elephant in the laboratory. Brain processes, he says, are deterministic, random or a mix of the two, and none of that is free. Acting on a desire is not the same as choosing it.

Brain scanners, he writes, can predict people's desires and decisions well before they are aware of them, with signals appearing from a few hundred milliseconds to a few seconds ahead. A commentator sums it up this way: a scientist watching a scanner can predict which of two switches a person will press before the person knows they chose. Commentators say he cites Libet's experiment for this.

Libet himself was more cautious. He proposed a free won't: a veto in the last 150 milliseconds or so before the movement.

Later work muddied the picture. The wrist-flex task was trivial, the samples were small, and reports of when the urge arrived were never checked against an independent measure. A 2008 fMRI study by Soon and colleagues predicted a two-way choice only modestly above chance, at around 60 percent. In 2012 Schurger showed that the averaged readiness potential can arise from spontaneous noise in the brain, and a 2021 review says the classic reading of it as a decision signal is no longer the field's default. Harari goes on to say that drugs, genetic engineering or brain stimulation could create or erase feelings such as love or fear.

[[lessons]]
$$**Hold the reasons for your own choices a little loosely.** Feeling that you decided does not tell you everything about where the decision started, and an explanation added afterwards can feel just as convincing. Someone who fires off a sharp message to a coworker and is sure they chose the words calmly could read it again an hour later and see more honestly what was going on in them when they wrote it. That does not mean throwing away their reasons, only treating them as one part of the story. It might also change how long they wait before sending the next one.$$

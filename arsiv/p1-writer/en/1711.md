[[story]]
J. L. Collins wrote the letters behind *The Simple Path to Wealth* to his daughter, about money and investing she was not yet ready to hear. When he came to explain what wealth is for, he reached for a parable and left the charts out.

Two boys grow up as friends and then part. One becomes a humble monk. The other becomes a rich and powerful minister to the king. Years later the minister, in his fine robes, looks at the thin, shabby monk and feels sorry for him.

He wants to help, so he offers a tip: learn to cater to the king and you would not have to live on rice and beans. The monk answers with the same advice turned around. If the minister learned to live on rice and beans, he would not have to cater to the king.

Each man sees a lack in the other's life. For the minister it is the rice and beans on the monk's plate, for the monk it is the minister's service to the king. Collins reads the exchange as a point about freedom, and one summary spells it out: the ability to live on little frees you from needing a boss, a market or other people's expectations. He calls the freedom he means "F-you money", enough savings that you can say no to a job or a boss.

Debt works against it. Summaries report that Collins sees debt as the biggest obstacle to building wealth and likens carrying it to being covered in leeches. His own plan is short: live on less than you earn, stay out of debt, put the surplus into low-cost broad index funds, with Vanguard's Total Stock Market Index Fund, VTSAX, as his main pick, and hold them even through crashes.

The plan is plain because the letters were. They were written for someone who did not want a finance course, and what they offered her was an option, the one the monk already had.

[[lessons]]
$$**Build the ability to live on less before you need to refuse something.** A shop assistant who deliberately keeps her spending flat as her pay rises can turn down a manager's demand for unpaid overtime without panic, while a colleague who turned every raise into a new monthly instalment has to say yes. That difference was settled months earlier, in ordinary purchases, and not on the day of the request. Before a larger purchase, you can ask whether its monthly cost would later leave you unable to refuse some job or favour, and let that answer weigh in.$$

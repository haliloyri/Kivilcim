John Kotter puts a clock on the patience of people living through a big change: roughly six to eighteen months. If they have seen nothing by then, he says, many stop following. The sixth step of his eight-step model exists to beat that clock.

[[story]]
In *Leading Change*, John Kotter walks through eight stages of an organizational transformation, and the sixth, generating short-term wins, gets particular weight. His starting point is how long the work takes. A real transformation takes years, and an effort with no near-term goals to meet and celebrate risks losing its momentum.

People will not keep marching down a long road without proof that it leads somewhere. Most of them want compelling evidence, within about six to eighteen months, that the journey is producing results. Without it, many give up quietly. Some join the group that was resisting from the start.

Kotter sets three conditions for a win. It has to be visible, so that large numbers of people can see it with their own eyes. It has to be unambiguous, with no room to argue about whether it happened. And it has to be clearly related to the change effort, so nobody can credit it to luck or to some other cause.

He does not treat wins as a gift that arrives on its own. In successful transformations, managers actively look for clear performance improvements, write them into the yearly planning system as goals, reach those goals, and then reward the people involved with recognition, promotions and even money. Failing to plan for wins systematically is one of the errors he lists as sinking change efforts.

One win does several jobs at once. People who made sacrifices get evidence that the sacrifice was worth it. Cynics and self-serving resisters lose some of the ground they stand on. The team sees, for the first time in a real result, whether the vision and strategy hold up, and can fine-tune them, while the bosses above have a reason to stay on board.

Kotter also names declaring victory too soon as a separate error. A win a few months in shows that the road is working. It does not show that the road has ended.

[[lessons]]
$$**Build one visible result into a long project.** People usually come to believe in a long effort because of a result they can see, and a well-argued plan rarely does it on its own. A baker moving all the shops to a new ordering system could show everyone one shop finishing its morning deliveries without a gap in the first week, and the other shops would have a reason to join. Planning that first result in advance lets you point at progress instead of defending it with words.$$

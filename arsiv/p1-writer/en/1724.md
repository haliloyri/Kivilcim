Soon after Darwin Smith became chief executive of Kimberly-Clark, his doctors told him he had less than a year to live. He spent the years that followed selling the mills that made up most of his company, including the one that gave it its name.

[[story]]
Smith took over as CEO in 1971, and he had little time to settle in. Shortly afterward doctors diagnosed him with nose and throat cancer and told him he had less than a year left. Jim Collins tells his story in *Good to Great*.

Smith had come to the top job from the company's legal department. Of himself, Collins quotes him saying:

> "I never stopped trying to become qualified for the job."

Kimberly-Clark was then a traditional paper company, and its paper mills made up most of its business. One of them stood in Kimberly, Wisconsin, and had given the company its name. Smith decided to sell all the traditional mills, that one included. Since the mills were most of the business, he was selling most of the company.

The company turned toward consumer paper products instead, the Kleenex and Huggies side of the house. A company sold off most of what it was and rebuilt itself around the part that remained, the part sold in boxes and packs rather than in rolls from a mill.

Collins presents the decision as the work of a quiet, unshowy man making a hard, clear choice. It meant giving up what the company had always been, and the mill that carried its name went with it.

In the years that followed, Kimberly-Clark went up against Scott Paper, its main rival in consumer paper products, and came out ahead. Collins reports that in 1995 a merger left Kimberly-Clark owning Scott Paper outright.

Smith ran the company for about twenty years, which makes the doctors' one year look very short. By Collins's figures, from 1971 to 1991 the company's cumulative stock returns were 4.1 times those of the general market.

##The company sold the mill that gave it its name and went on to beat its old rival.##

[[lessons]]
$$**Decide what you can do best, and be ready to let go of the rest.** A baker who has sold bread and cakes for years may notice that customers come mostly for the cakes, yet finds it hard to drop the bread because the shop began with it. Smith selling the mill with the company's name on it shows that a business being yours and a business moving you forward are separate questions. Once you keep them apart, you can put your time and money where you are strongest, and you can explain what you dropped with a clear reason instead of regret.$$

Amy Edmondson was counting medication errors in hospital units, and the units that looked best had the longest lists. That was the reverse of what she had predicted. Her numbers seemed to be pointing the wrong way.

[[story]]
In *The Fearless Organization*, Edmondson goes back to an early study of hospital work. She was examining medication errors and expected that better teams would make fewer of them. The data said otherwise: the units with better teamwork and leadership reported more errors.

She did not stop at the puzzling result but changed the question. Perhaps the difference was not in making errors but in talking about them. In the units with more reports, people felt safe enough to say that something had gone wrong, so a wrong dose could be named out loud.

In other units, staff feared blame. The errors there were hidden, and the records showed few of them, so the reports looked tidy. Nobody could learn from a mistake that never came to the surface, and the same slip could happen again another day.

##A spotless record can mean that people have decided to stay quiet.##

Edmondson came to call the shared belief that you will not be punished for speaking up psychological safety. She reports that when she later looked at more objective, independent measures of errors, the psychologically safe units did not make more of them. The higher the safety, the fewer the errors.

So the paradox was a measuring problem. A low count of reported errors can mean a team is excellent, and it can equally mean errors are being hidden. In places where one hidden slip can hurt a patient, the difference is not minor.

The book carries the idea beyond hospitals to teams in companies. For Edmondson, judging a team by how few problems it shows is a trap, and a more telling sign is how safely problems come up.

[[lessons]]
$$**Do not read a quiet error log as proof of a flawless team.** In a workshop where an apprentice sets up a machine wrongly and says nothing for fear of the foreman's temper, the foreman sees a clean daily log and only learns of the problem when a bad part reaches a customer. In Edmondson's units, the long lists belonged to teams that spoke freely, while the clean ones did not show the same safety. When you review a team, you can ask how easily a problem would be said aloud, and not only how many problems appear.$$

A child who starts school this year will be midway through a working life by the middle of the century, and nobody can say which jobs will exist by then. So what should that child spend all those school days learning? One answer on offer sounds less like a school subject than like a way of coping.

[[story]]
In *21 Lessons for the 21st Century*, Yuval Noah Harari begins with an admission: nobody knows what the job market will look like in 2050. A child who sits down at a school desk now will be mid-career by then, and a good part of what that child memorises at that desk may be irrelevant long before. Schools still keep pouring in facts.

That puts an awkward question to every classroom. If the facts may expire and the jobs are unknown, then lessons built around handing over facts and one trade's skills are aimed at a target nobody can see.

Harari wants the weight to move from technical skills to general-purpose life skills. He points to four that many educators already recommend: critical thinking, communication, collaboration and creativity. None of them belongs to a single trade, so they can travel with a person from one job to the next. Then he names the one he values most.

It is the ability to deal with change, to learn something new, and to keep your mental balance in situations you have never met. He puts that steadiness at the top of the list, above any technical skill. A person can know a tool perfectly and still lose their footing on the day the tool disappears.

The need does not stop at the school gate. To keep up with the world of 2050, Harari writes, people will have to invent new ideas and products, but above all they will have to reinvent themselves.

##Reinvent yourself, and then do it again.##

So his answer to what school should teach has no subject name. It is the ability a grown person will need once more each time the work changes shape, and in his picture that happens more than once. The child with the memorised facts and the adult with a lost job end up needing the same thing.

[[lessons]]
$$**Practise how you learn, not only what you learn.** When a skill is likely to change, the habit of picking up the next one stays useful after the skill itself is gone. A bookkeeper who has just learned a new accounting program can watch how she gets through the awkward first days: whom she asks, which small tasks she starts with, what she writes down. Knowing that routine at all lets her treat the next unfamiliar tool as a problem she has solved before, so she can start sooner instead of waiting until she feels fully ready for it.$$

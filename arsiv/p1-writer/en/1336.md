Joe Navarro spent about twenty-five years as an FBI counterintelligence agent, and he came away trusting one part of a person's body least: the face. The part he trusted most is somewhere people rarely look.

[[story]]
In *What Every BODY Is Saying*, published in 2008, Navarro lays out what he learned from those years. His starting point is that you can manage your words on purpose, but you cannot easily manage your first reaction to a threat. That reaction comes from the limbic system, which he nicknames the "honest brain." It acts before the thinking part of the mind has weighed in.

The reactions come in three kinds: freeze, flight and fight. A freeze shows as a sudden stillness. Flight shows as a wish to leave, and the feet may turn toward the exit or start to shuffle. The shoulders can rise toward the ears and the neck seems to shrink, a move Navarro calls the turtle effect. A hand may travel to the neck or face, a self-soothing gesture he calls a pacifying behavior.

##Navarro argues the feet are the most honest part of the body, because they are the part nobody is watching.##

The face is the opposite case. People have practiced controlling their faces since childhood, while nobody practices their feet. Navarro's advice is therefore to look beyond the face, at the shoulders, hands and feet, when someone is talking. A threat does not have to be large. A hard question, a turn to speak in a full room, or one wrong sentence can set the alarm off, and the body shows it before any words arrive.

This is Navarro's own framework, not a measured finding. The picture of the brain as a clean split between feeling and reason is also a simplification. Studies of nonverbal cues find them weak at detecting lies, and Navarro himself says to avoid judging from any single signal and to compare against the person's normal behavior.

So feet turned toward a door prove nothing. They give you a reason to ask.

[[lessons]]
$$**Treat a change in someone's body as a reason to ask a question.** Suppose a colleague's hand goes to his neck every time one particular question comes up in a meeting. It would be wrong to conclude that he is lying, but it makes sense to return to that question gently and open it up again. That way the observation produces a better conversation, not a verdict, and the other person does not feel cornered. Your wording can sound like an invitation, for example "is there more to this one?"$$

A lawyer who had just won the Republican nomination in Chicago offered each of the three men he beat a seat in his cabinet. All three believed they were better qualified for the presidency than he was. Lincoln knew that and invited them anyway, and they came.

[[story]]
William Seward of New York arrived at the 1860 Republican convention in Chicago as the man most delegates expected to be nominated. Salmon Chase of Ohio and Edward Bates of Missouri wanted the job too. Lincoln's record looked thin beside theirs: he had served one term in Congress and had lost his 1858 Senate race. Ballot by ballot, delegates drifted toward him anyway, and on the third he had the nomination.

In *Team of Rivals*, Doris Kearns Goodwin shows that the surprise came after the election. Lincoln invited all three defeated men into his cabinet: Seward as secretary of state, Chase at the Treasury, Bates as attorney general. These were men who believed they belonged in his chair. Nobody at that table could have expected it to be comfortable.

The friction came quickly. On April 1, 1861, about a month into the administration, Seward sent Lincoln a memorandum. It said the government had no clear policy, proposed uniting the country by confronting foreign powers, and implied that Seward himself could carry that policy out. Lincoln did not dismiss him. He made plain that the president would set policy, and he kept Seward in office.

##Lincoln kept Seward in office and told him who decided.##

Another seat in the cabinet rested on an old slight. Years earlier, as a lawyer in a Cincinnati case, Edwin Stanton had treated Lincoln with contempt. In January 1862 Lincoln made him secretary of war.

Chase held on to his presidential ambitions while running the Treasury and left the department in 1864. After years of working alongside him, Lincoln appointed him chief justice of the Supreme Court before that year was out. Seward, who had first seen himself as the real head of the government, became one of Lincoln's closest friends.

[[lessons]]
$$**Bring in the person who disagrees, and say who decides.** A small restaurant owner might hire a former partner who knows the kitchen better than she does. The arrangement only holds if she says up front who gives the final approval to the menu. Objections then get made in the open, and nobody has to test every week where authority sits, so the two of them can argue about food and not about who is in charge.$$

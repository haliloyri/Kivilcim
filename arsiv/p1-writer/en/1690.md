Your shoulders tighten at the start of a meeting, and only later do you work out that you were nervous. Somewhere in between you probably told yourself that nervous was the wrong thing to be. A book on emotional intelligence starts its first skill right there, with the body and with that second thought.

[[story]]
In *Emotional Intelligence 2.0*, Travis Bradberry and Jean Greaves treat the body as the first place a feeling shows up. Their advice is a small exercise. When you are calm, recall an event in which you felt something strong, then notice what your body did at the time: whether your muscles tightened, how your breathing changed, whether your heart sped up.

The payoff comes later. If you know a given feeling shows up in your shoulders or your breath, you can recognize it in the moment, while it is still easy to act on. Body signals differ from person to person, so the book does not hand you one map. Each reader builds a personal one.

This belongs to self-awareness, the first of the book's four EQ skills. The authors define it as perceiving your emotions accurately as they happen and understanding how you tend to behave across situations. A share of the book's 66 strategies is devoted to it, and one of them concerns judgment.

The rule is to stop labelling emotions as good or bad. Judging a feeling, the authors say, puts a second feeling on top of it. Anger followed by guilt about being angry is the kind of pile-up they mean, and shame or pride can do the same job. You end up with two emotions where there was one, and the first one is harder to see.

##A third strategy asks you to lean into discomfort.## Rather than brush off a feeling you dislike, you turn toward it. The authors say that ignoring even a small emotion wastes a chance to use it, and that avoiding the "bad" ones means missing what they are trying to tell you. They also tell readers not to be afraid of their emotional mistakes, because a mistake carries information.

[[lessons]]
$$**Name a feeling before you rate it.** Calling a feeling good or bad adds a second feeling on top of it and makes the first harder to read. After a presentation, someone who feels uneasy can say "I'm anxious" and check how their shoulders sit, instead of telling themselves that anxiety is the wrong thing to feel. That small pause lets you see the feeling at its real size and decide what to do about it, rather than reacting to the layer of judgment sitting on top of it.$$

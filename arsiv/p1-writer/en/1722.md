In the room, a director was showing a half-finished film to experienced colleagues. The viewers did not hold back, yet not one of them had the power to give an order. According to Ed Catmull, that was exactly what made the meeting that rescued Pixar's films work.

[[story]]
At Pixar, a director brought the unfinished version of a film in front of a group called the Braintrust. The group was made up of experienced colleagues, peers rather than bosses. It said plainly what was not working, but it did not order a fix.

In *Creativity, Inc.* Ed Catmull sums up the arrangement this way: the Braintrust has no authority. The director does not have to follow any specific suggestion. Responsibility stays with the director, and the group only says what it sees.

Naming the problem rather than the solution is a rule chosen on purpose. The solution is the director's job, and a person who has been inside a film for a long time can lose perspective. Peers on the outside help him see it again.

That arrangement lowers the fear of authority. The director takes the notes not as a gate to approval but as a shared way of seeing. Even when the criticism is sharp, he does not have to carry it as an order, because the decision is still his.

Troubled projects such as Toy Story 2 were taken apart and rebuilt at this table. The film began as a direct-to-video sequel and was judged not good enough. John Lasseter took over as director and reconceived the story, and people such as Andrew Stanton, Lee Unkrich and Joe Ranft joined the rescue.

In Catmull's view the early versions of Pixar's films are not good, and that is normal. He compares a new film to an ugly baby that needs protecting and time to grow. The Braintrust exists not to pass judgment on that baby but to see together where it is stuck. The meeting is not an approval gate. It is a shared way of seeing that accepts the film is still being made, and the director leaves it taking the film back up, not letting it go.

##What made the meeting strong was that nobody could give anybody an order.## That is why the director could hear the criticism without going on the defensive.

[[lessons]]
$$**Name the problem and leave the fix to its owner.** If you read a friend's small-business flyer and say "I can't tell what you sell in the second paragraph" without telling them how to repair it, the writer listens without getting defensive and uses the note for their own work. Because the decision stays with them, they do not feel they must carry the criticism like an order. What you say is then both honest and usable, and the two of you can look at the same place in the next draft.$$

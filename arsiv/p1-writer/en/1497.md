One sentence, put to 198,000 employees. It named no task, no skill, no department. Gallup lined the answers up against the results of the business units those people worked in, then asked the same sentence of far more people in far bigger companies, and the pattern split in two.

[[story]]
The sentence was short: at work, do you have the opportunity to do what you do best every day? It comes from *Now, Discover Your Strengths* by Marcus Buckingham and Donald O. Clifton. Gallup put it to 198,000 employees, spread over 7,939 business units in 36 companies, and set each answer beside the results of the unit that person worked in. Turnover, productivity and customer satisfaction scores sat on one side, the single answer on the other.

People who strongly agreed were 50 percent more likely to work in business units with lower turnover. They were 38 percent more likely to work in more productive units and 44 percent more likely to work in units with higher customer satisfaction scores. These are associations, not proven causes. Still, units that raised their number of strongly agreeing employees saw comparable increases in productivity, customer loyalty and retention.

Then came the wider count. Across a broader database of more than 1.7 million employees in 101 companies in 63 countries, ##only 20 percent of employees in the large organizations surveyed strongly agreed that their strengths were in play every day.## Four in five did not give the top answer. Next to the first 198,000, this was a much bigger pool and a much thinner result.

The share thins out with time. The longer an employee stays and the higher the person climbs, the less likely they are to strongly agree. The article also says eight out of ten employees feel somewhat miscast in their role, but it does not clearly name where that figure comes from, so it is best held loosely.

Gallup sketches what a target could look like. Moving the strongly-agree share to 40 percent would double it, two employees in five instead of one. Sixty percent, a tripling, it calls not too aggressive for the greatest organizations.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask Gallup's question about your own week.** The answer is information about how tasks are handed out, more than a verdict on you. Suppose a bookkeeper looks at Friday's task list and marks the days that left room for what she does best, with a pencil and no commentary. If no day qualifies, she has something specific to bring to her manager when she asks to swap one task with a colleague. If most days qualify, she knows what to protect when new work arrives, and she can say so before the next round of tasks is handed out.$$

[[open]]
Two roommates in San Francisco, short on rent, noticed that conference visitors had nowhere to stay. Instead of looking for a way to make money right away, they asked a short question, and the order of the questions that followed is the story.

[[story]]
In the fall of 2007, Joe Gebbia and Brian Chesky were roommates in San Francisco with no jobs, little money and rent to pay. Visitors in town for a conference could not find hotel rooms, because the city's hotels were full. The roommates started with a question rather than a plan: why couldn't they house those visitors for a night or two? A second followed, why not in their own place?

They rented out three inflatable mattresses to three strangers, with a modest breakfast and some sightseeing tips thrown in.

Warren Berger says the "what if" questions came soon after. ##What would it take to offer more than a mattress?## They built their own website instead of posting on Craigslist. Then they asked whether the experience could be repeated in every major city.

There was no money for ads. They launched in Denver during the 2008 Democratic National Convention and pitched the story to news producers, which got them on CNN. Over the next year they kept asking what was working, and the answers led to online payments and expansion beyond the United States. Gebbia said that being both travelers and hosts helped them see the opportunity.

Berger sees a basic, logical progression of three open questions behind many innovation breakthroughs. Why challenges the premise of the work, and it is the question companies ask least. What if starts the search for solutions with no constraints. How turns ideas into practical steps. He sets these against the old closed questions, such as how many, how much and how fast, which assume you should keep doing what you already do. As a companion to why he adds why not, which presses on assumptions. His own example of a why is asking why a business unit exists at all instead of how to raise its margins. He points to Robin Chase in the same vein: she challenged the idea that cars could not be shared, and Zipcar followed. Berger argues that the questions we ask often matter more than the answers, because facts only help when you know which follow-up questions to ask.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask why before you ask how.** In Berger's sequence the how comes last, because practical steps only pay off after the premise has been questioned and ideas have been tried without limits. A bakery that keeps its orders in a paper ledger can ask how to write them down faster, and all that does is speed up the ledger. Someone who asks why the ledger exists, and what if the customer wrote the order themselves, may find the ledger is unnecessary. The order of the questions decides how much the answer is allowed to change.$$

[[open]]
A statesman loses his post after a military disaster and picks up a paintbrush. Alex Soojung-Kim Pang uses that choice as his clearest case of a rest that looks a lot like work.

[[story]]
Churchill left the Admiralty in 1915, after the Gallipoli campaign, dismissed from the post of First Lord. That was the year he started to paint. Pang reports it in *Rest* as a form of rest, and it becomes his main example of an idea he calls deep play.

Deep play, as Pang defines it, is an activity that is rewarding on its own and takes on additional layers of meaning and personal significance over time. He lists four features, and an activity qualifies when it has at least one of them. It is mentally absorbing. It offers a new context in which to use some of the same skills used at work. It offers some of the satisfaction of work, with different and clearer rewards. Or it gives a living connection to the player's past.

Churchill later wrote about his painting in an essay, *Painting as a Pastime*. In it he says that the tired parts of the mind can be rested and strengthened, not merely by rest, but by using other parts. A new field of interest, he adds, must be illuminated.

In the book deep play sits beside walking, napping and sleep as forms of what Pang calls active rest. Rest, in his argument, is a partner of work and a skill that can be learned, so resting well takes some craft.

Painting fits those features. Someone holding a brush in front of a blank surface has to look closely, decide and correct, and the result sits on the canvas where anyone can see how far it has come. The political burden of the day stays behind for a while, because the mind has something else to do.

Pang treats Churchill's painting as work done in a different part of the mind rather than as spare time filled in. His examples come from the lives of well-known people, so they show what deep play looks like without testing whether it works for everyone.

[[lessons]]
$$**Rest in something that absorbs you completely.** A passive break can leave a tired mind free to keep turning over the same worry, while a different activity that needs full attention interrupts that loop. An accountant who spends her days with numbers and builds model ships in the evening uses her precision in a new setting and ends the evening with something she can hold. Over time such an activity gathers meaning of its own and gives a recovery that the working hours cannot.$$

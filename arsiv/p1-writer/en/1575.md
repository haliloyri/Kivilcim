[[story]]
In the chapter "Pride and Prejudice ... and Other Blind Spots" of *Mistakes Were Made (But Not by Me)*, Carol Tavris and Elliot Aronson picture a pyramid, and Tavris describes its top with two students. Both hold the same mild view of cheating: it is neither honorable nor terrible. The test matters for getting into graduate school, and when both freeze on an essay question, a neighbor's answers are visible.

On impulse one student copies and the other looks away. Each then builds a reason for what he or she did. Over time their beliefs about cheating move far apart, as if they had started at the top of a pyramid, close together, and slid down opposite sides.

Tavris says Aronson built the image from an experiment by Judson Mills with children, published in the *Journal of Personality* in 1958, in which attitudes diverged sharply between those who cheated and those who did not. The mechanism is the explaining. Each step is justified, and the justification makes the next step easier to take. The farther down each person goes, the harder it becomes to see the path the other one took.

The book offers a grown-up case from Watergate. Jeb Magruder, a White House aide, watched Bob Haldeman chew out an assistant over a small lapse and found a way to excuse it. The authors present that kind of small excuse as an early step toward the prison term Magruder served later.

Their example from politics is a trip. Politicians accept a small favor from a lobbyist and justify it, then accept larger ones, and the trip Tom DeLay took to St Andrews is the one the book names. The line that sums it up: "Pride, when followed by self-justification, paves the road to Scotland."

[[lessons]]
$$**Notice the reason you give yourself right after a small choice.** The reason you invent in the first minutes decides how easy the next similar choice will be. A freelancer who skips a small step on an invoice "just this once" and thinks "nobody checks these anyway" has also made the next skipped step easier. By saying the reason out loud or writing it down, she can ask whether she would accept it from a colleague. If she would not, she can undo the small choice while it is still small, which costs far less than undoing a habit.$$

A new cola beat both Coca-Cola and Pepsi in taste tests, and it was pulled from sale 79 days after launch. One number shows what it cost the company: more than 1,500 calls a day on the hotline.

[[story]]
Chapter thirty-four of *Yes!* is titled "What can you gain from loss?" and says people react more to what they might lose than to what they might gain. Summaries of the chapter name New Coke as its example, and the events below follow the public record.

Coca-Cola's share of the U.S. cola market had slid from about 60 percent after the Second World War to under 24 percent by 1983, largely to Pepsi. Blind taste tests showed that people preferred the sweeter Pepsi, and survey results were what finally persuaded management to change the formula. Chief executive Roberto Goizueta insisted on the word "New!" on the label and put it as "New Coke or no Coke." The drink launched on 23 April 1985. In taste tests, surveys and focus groups it overwhelmingly beat both regular Coke and Pepsi, and surveys found three quarters of respondents would buy it again.

Still, roughly 10 to 12 percent of testers had said they felt angry and alienated by the idea of the change. The company did not give that small group much weight. After launch, Coca-Cola received more than 40,000 calls and letters. Its 1-800 hotline, which had taken about 400 calls a day, now took more than 1,500. Fans of the old drink organised as Old Cola Drinkers of America, and a class action was filed, which a judge quickly dismissed.

On 11 July 1985 the original formula came back as Coca-Cola Classic, 79 days after the launch. The hotline took 31,600 calls in the two days after the announcement. Donald Keough, the company's president, later said that consumer research could not measure the deep and abiding emotional attachment to the original. By the end of the year Classic outsold both New Coke and Pepsi.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you change something, count what people will feel they lose.** New Coke won the taste tests, yet the voices of those losing the old drink were louder. A café owner who wants to move the sugar bowls from the counter could ask regulars what they will miss about the old arrangement. Even if she is sure the move is better, the answer shows her what to protect and gives her the first thing to say when she explains the change.$$

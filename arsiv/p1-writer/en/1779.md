[[story]]
When Bob Iger became Disney's CEO, a few days after Michael Eisner left at the end of September 2005, his first priority was reviving Disney Animation, which had spent over a billion dollars on films in the previous decade and lost nearly $400 million. Market research showed that Pixar had overtaken Disney as the brand mothers with children under twelve called good for their family. Iger tells what he did about it in *The Ride of a Lifetime*.

He laid out four options to the board, and the last was to buy Pixar outright. The room nearly exploded. The board nonetheless let him explore the idea.

On a call about the video iPod, Steve Jobs told him that buying Pixar was not the craziest idea in the world. At Apple, Jobs wrote Pros and Cons on a whiteboard. The Cons list was longer: Disney's culture would destroy Pixar, and Iger's board would never allow it. Jobs still judged a few big Pros to matter more than many small Cons.

Iger then visited Pixar. John Lasseter previewed films, Ed Catmull showed him the technology, and Iger came away convinced that the deal would be transformative. He committed to protecting Pixar's culture and offered Lasseter and Catmull the leadership of Disney Animation. The agreement spelled out specific terms protecting that culture, along with a branding arrangement for Disney and Pixar.

The deal was all stock, worth $7.4 billion, at 2.3 Disney shares for each Pixar share. After a meeting in January 2006 with Jobs, Lasseter and Catmull, the board approved it 9 to 2, though Eisner had lobbied against it. On the day of the announcement, Jobs told Iger privately that his pancreatic cancer had returned and offered him a way out of the deal. Iger went ahead.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask what must not change before you change anything.** A new owner of a neighbourhood bakery who first asks the staff which habits make the bread sell, and keeps those, learns what the shop depends on before touching the schedule. She can then change the ordering system or the till without losing the thing customers come for. The question also tells the staff that she sees them as the shop's value and not as something to tidy up.$$

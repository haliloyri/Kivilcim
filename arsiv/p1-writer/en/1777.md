Three hundred pairs of running shoes sat in a basement, waiting for a man with a day job to take them to the next track meet. The company that owned them was a name he had made up in Japan.

[[story]]
In 1962 Phil Knight, 24, pitched the Japanese shoemaker Onitsuka in Kobe as the representative of Blue Ribbon Sports of Portland, Oregon. The company did not exist yet. Onitsuka agreed to let him represent its shoes in America. Knight tells the story in *Shoe Dog*.

In 1964, when the shoes arrived, he sent two pairs to Bill Bowerman, his old University of Oregon track coach, who became his partner. The first order was 300 pairs, stored in Knight's basement. He sold them from the trunk of his green Plymouth Valiant at track meets, and a second order of 300 sold out within a few months. Sales for the first year came to about $8,000.

Sales kept doubling, and that was the trouble. The cash from each sale went straight into the next, larger shipment, so the company was always short. Banks wanted a new company to turn a profit quickly and to keep its sales within its cash balance, and Blue Ribbon was doing the opposite. Late shipments from Onitsuka made it harder still, because they shortened the time to sell before loans came due.

In 1968 sales were set to double for the fifth year in a row, and Knight still could not afford a salary from the company. He left his accounting job at Price Waterhouse, which had taken six days a week, for a teaching post at Portland State that paid $700 a month. In 1969, with sales projected to double again to about $300,000, he paid himself $18,000 and left the classroom to work on the company full time.

The company was renamed Nike in 1971, and the cash problem did not go away. One fix was to give retailers a 7 percent discount for orders placed six months ahead, which brought money in early. In 1975 Bank of California dropped Nike and closed its credit line, citing its focus on growth over equity. Nissho, another Japanese company that financed it, demanded a full audit, which showed that a loan had gone into building a factory. Nissho then took over Nike's debt to the bank.

[[lessons]]
$$**Count the days between paying and getting paid.** A caterer who accepts a much bigger order has to buy ingredients and pay staff weeks before the client settles the bill. If she looks only at the profit on the invoice, she can be broke in the middle of a successful month. Writing down when each cost leaves her account and when each payment arrives shows her how much she has to cover, so she can ask for a deposit or take a smaller order. A growing business tends to hide this gap behind rising sales, which is why it helps to write it down before the order is accepted.$$

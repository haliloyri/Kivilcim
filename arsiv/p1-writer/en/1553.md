In some meetings every head nods and every decision passes. Lencioni says the real opinions then go behind the scenes, as veiled remarks and back-channel comments, and that the nodding is exactly what makes the meeting dull and the decisions weak.

[[story]]
In *The Five Dysfunctions of a Team*, Patrick Lencioni names the second dysfunction fear of conflict. Its outward sign is a quiet, pleasant meeting that he calls artificial harmony: nobody argues, nobody pushes back, and the agenda moves along on time.

The cost shows up elsewhere. According to Lencioni, teams that avoid open disagreement have boring meetings, and the disagreements they do have move into veiled discussions and back-channel comments. People nod in the room and say what they think afterward to whoever will listen. The decision on paper has fewer believers than it seems.

He separates this from fighting. The conflict he wants is about ideas: a plan, an assumption, a way of doing the work. It is not about personalities. A team that argues this way, he says, settles issues faster, because nothing is left to be raised in a side conversation later. The debate can be passionate and unfiltered, as long as it stays on the issue and ends with the team still intact.

##A team that argues in the room has fewer arguments waiting for it afterward.##

Lencioni gives the leader a job, which he calls mining for conflict. When a team goes quiet on a topic that clearly deserves a debate, the leader pulls the disagreement out of hiding and resists the urge to rescue everyone from the discomfort. He also suggests reminding people, in the middle of a heated exchange, that what they are doing is healthy and that they are not in danger.

All of this rests on trust, the first layer of his pyramid. People argue honestly only with colleagues whose reaction they do not fear. Where that is missing, the quiet meeting is the safe choice, and the real conversation takes place in the corridor.

[[lessons]]
$$**Invite the disagreement into the room.** Asking a group whether anyone has concerns usually produces silence, because saying no is the easy answer. A project lead can instead ask each person to name the weakest part of the plan. The concern that would have turned up in a private message after the meeting now gets said while the whole group can respond to it. Try this at your next planning session and notice which objections surface early, while changing course is still cheap, and see whether the plan changes as a result.$$

Behind a bicycle shop in Dayton, two men set small model wings inside a wooden box and blew air across them. They were not aircraft engineers. They repaired bicycles, and they suspected that the tables everyone trusted were wrong.

[[story]]
In 1901, Wilbur and Orville Wright came back to Dayton from Kitty Hawk disappointed. Their glider, a frame of fabric-covered wood lifted only by the steady Kitty Hawk wind, had not produced the lift their calculations promised. They had to find out what had gone wrong.

The glider flew in the steady wind of Kitty Hawk, a long way from the shop where they sold bicycles. It was meant to lift more than it did.

Their calculations rested on published air-pressure tables from earlier experimenters, including Otto Lilienthal. According to David McCullough's *The Wright Brothers*, Wilbur concluded the trouble might lie in the tables themselves, as well as in their glider design. Doubting the field's most respected data took nerve. One coefficient in the tables dated from the eighteenth century, and no one had measured it again.

Wilbur was thirty-four and Orville thirty. They made their living selling and repairing bicycles. In their shop they built a small wooden wind tunnel, with a fan driven by a motor in the shop pushing air through it.

They cut small wing models from metal, shaped them, and tested about two hundred wing shapes one at a time. They recorded their own lift and drag results, because they did not want to trust someone else's numbers again.

Those measurements went into the design of the 1902 glider, which flew far better than the one before. In December 1903, powered flight followed at Kitty Hawk.

##The Wrights tested whether the table could be wrong, using their own measurements.## The result came from data they had checked in their own shop. A small box behind a bicycle shop rewrote what people knew about flight.

[[lessons]]
$$**When a number everyone trusts disagrees with your own results, test it yourself.** A baker may notice that loaves stay raw when she follows the recipe book's baking time. Noting her own oven's temperature every day for a week would show whether the fault lies with the recipe or the oven. She keeps the book and ends up with the figure that fits her own conditions. The same habit works for any benchmark at work that nobody has checked on a real case.$$

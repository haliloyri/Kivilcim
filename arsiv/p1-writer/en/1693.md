Worsley raised the sextant, and the clouds, as usual, had closed over the sun. Behind him sat five men in an open boat 22 and a half feet long, with a deck of sledge runners and canvas, 800 miles of Southern Ocean ahead. Twenty-two more men were waiting for whatever he managed to calculate.

[[story]]
In Alfred Lansing's *Endurance*, Ernest Shackleton's ship is trapped by pack ice in the Weddell Sea in January 1915 and drifts with it for months. In November 1915 the ice crushes the ship and it sinks. The 28 men make camp on the ice, and when the floe breaks up they take to three lifeboats and land on Elephant Island in April 1916.

Elephant Island was uninhabited, and it lay far from any shipping lane. Nobody knew where the expedition was, which meant nobody was coming. Waiting there meant staying unrescued, and Shackleton decided that help had to be fetched.

The nearest help was South Georgia, about 800 nautical miles across the Southern Ocean. He chose the strongest boat, the 22-and-a-half-foot James Caird, and the carpenter, Harry McNish, built it up with a deck made from sledge runners, box lids and canvas. Five men went with Shackleton: Frank Worsley, Tom Crean, McNish, Timothy McCarthy and John Vincent. The other 22 stayed behind under Frank Wild, sheltering under upturned boats.

Worsley had the hardest job. To fix the boat's position he needed the sun in his sextant, and the sky seldom gave it to him. Each time the clouds opened he took a sighting, and from the few he managed to get he worked out the course. Ice kept forming on the boat, and the men had to chip it off so the load would not capsize her.

After about 16 days the boat reached South Georgia, but on the uninhabited side of the island, not the side with the whaling stations. Shackleton, Worsley and Crean crossed the unmapped, mountainous interior on foot in about 36 hours, with screws driven into their boots for grip and a length of rope between them. They came down to the whaling station at Stromness.

The rescue did not follow at once. Ice turned back the first three attempts to reach Elephant Island. ##The 22 men were taken off on August 30, 1916, on the fourth try, and every one of the 28 survived.##

[[lessons]]
$$**Count the cost of waiting next to the cost of acting.** A shop owner whose supplier has gone quiet may feel that waiting for a reply is the safe option. Meanwhile the shelves empty a little more each day, and the cost of waiting grows where nobody is looking. Looking for a new supplier carries its own risk, but putting both options side by side lets her decide how long to wait instead of leaving the outcome to someone else's silence. From then on she acts on a choice she made herself, not on hope.$$

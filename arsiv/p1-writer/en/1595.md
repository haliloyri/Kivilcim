Two boxes of the same kind of food sit on a supermarket shelf. One says LOW FAT in big letters. That phrase has a history, and it starts with a government booklet telling people what to eat.

[[story]]
In 1977 the US government published dietary goals for the public, and the first message was to eat less fat. Food manufacturers adapted quickly. Shelves filled with low-fat products, and the claim was printed large on the packaging.

Neurologist David Perlmutter argues in *Grain Brain* that the change came with a price. When fat came out of a product, he says, refined carbohydrate and sugar went in. One thing was reduced, and something else was added to the plate without much notice.

Refined carbohydrates are foods like white flour and sugar, stripped of their fibre. Eating them makes blood sugar climb fast, and the body releases insulin to bring it back down. If that loop repeats often, cells can start responding to insulin less well, which is called insulin resistance.

Perlmutter says the brain is largely made of fat and that cholesterol is one of the building blocks of brain cells. The real threat, in his view, is blood sugar rising again and again. ##For him the trouble was not what left the plate but what took its place.##

He also touches on Alzheimer's. Some researchers call the disease "type 3 diabetes", and he thinks that deserves to be taken seriously. Types 1 and 2 are blood-sugar diseases, and the name "type 3" suggests that Alzheimer's may be linked to the brain not using insulin well enough. The cause of Alzheimer's is not known for certain.

Honesty requires a note here. These are Perlmutter's views and they are disputed. Many health bodies still recommend limiting saturated fat. The book is best read as one specialist's argument against that advice, not as a settled finding, and checking labels is useful whichever side of the argument you land on.

[[lessons]]
$$**Check what replaced the thing you cut.** When you reduce one thing, the rest of the plate changes too, and we rarely notice. Someone who picks a snack marked "light" may not realise that the fat went down while the sugar went up, unless they compare the sugar line on the two labels. Looking at that line lets you base the choice on what is in the packet instead of what the packaging promises, and once it becomes a habit it changes how you shop.$$

Two chocolates sat on a table, each with a small price sign. The researchers took one cent off both prices and left the gap between them exactly where it was. Most people standing at the table promptly changed their minds about which one to take.

[[story]]
In Dan Ariely's *Predictably Irrational*, researchers set up a table on a university campus with two chocolates on it. One was a Lindt truffle, the other a plain Hershey's Kiss. Each customer could take only one. Ariely ran the study with two colleagues, Kristina Shampanier and Nina Mazar.

Ariely's starting point is that when we buy something we usually weigh an upside against a downside. What happens when the price is zero?

First came ordinary prices. The truffle cost 15 cents and the Kiss cost 1 cent. At those prices, 73 percent of buyers chose the truffle and 27 percent the Kiss. People thought the better chocolate was worth the 14 cents extra.

Then the researchers cut both prices by one cent. The truffle was now 14 cents, and the Kiss was free. The gap between the two was still exactly 14 cents. The truffle was still the better chocolate, and it still cost the same amount more. By the logic of standard economics nothing should have changed, because a 14-cent gap is a 14-cent gap.

##The choice flipped: 69 percent of buyers took the free Kiss.##

Ariely's explanation is that people are afraid of loss by nature, and with a free item there is no visible possibility of losing anything. To him zero is not just another price but a source of irrational excitement. The downside of the free option drops out of sight.

He finds the same pattern in online shopping. Amazon offered free shipping in several European countries. In France it charged a very small fee, about one franc, and orders did not rise. When that fee dropped to zero, French orders rose like the others.

[[lessons]]
$$**Work out what "free" costs you yourself.** Someone who accepts a free wardrobe from a neighbour may not count the hours of carrying it or the space it will take up in the flat. If he asks whether he would still take it at a small price, he starts to see the real cost of the job. That question does not have to talk him out of the wardrobe. It only lets him know why he is taking it, and it puts the time he is giving up in plain view.$$

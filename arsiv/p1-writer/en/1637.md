A research group was asked to work out what America's heroin-addicted soldiers would cost once they came home from Vietnam. The answer it brought back now sits at the center of a book about addiction, and it is not the answer the government feared.

[[story]]
In the chapter "From Vietnam to Rat Park", Gabor Maté starts from a number. After coming home, he says, American soldiers who had been addicted to heroin in Vietnam went into remission at a rate of 95 percent, a figure he calls unheard of among narcotics addicts treated in the United States. It is his main example in *In the Realm of Hungry Ghosts* against the idea that a drug alone causes addiction.

The figure traces back to Lee Robins and her research group. The US government asked them to measure what returning addicts would cost the Veterans Administration, since cheap, strong heroin had been easy for GIs to get in Vietnam. Opium had spread through Southeast Asia, and in September 1971 the group sampled about 470 men on their way home.

A 2017 review of Robins's studies reports that about a third of the soldiers used heroin in Vietnam and about one in five showed signs of dependence. Those are wartime numbers. A year after the men got home, about one in ten of the sample reported using an opiate, and 7 percent of those reported being addicted again. Definitions shift a little from study to study.

Back in the United States, most of those who recovered did so without formal treatment. Robins's team put it down to leaving the war and returning to a stable home. The team also said the effect of setting needed more study, including how long the remission lasts.

A 1979 survey of veterans seeking heroin treatment points the same way. Vietnam veterans had often started during service, and they usually named relief from fear and the tension of war as what the drug had done for them. Maté takes this picture as support for the claim that a substance alone does not make an addict, and he looks for addiction's roots in the surroundings.

The chapter's title already points to its second example, Bruce Alexander's rats in Rat Park. Not every soldier quit. The 95 percent applies to men who had been addicted, and it is the book's figure, resting on a study whose own authors asked for further research.

[[lessons]]
$$**Check what the setting asks of a habit.** A habit that does a job in a hard place may loosen when the place changes, so it pays to look at the setting before judging the person. A nurse who is short with colleagues on understaffed night shifts and notices it fade on day shifts has learned something about the night shifts, not about her character. That lets her stop blaming herself and name which single condition she could ask to have changed, and it gives her something concrete and fair to take to her manager.$$

A student who paid for food by returning empty Coke bottles sat down in the class on campus that looked the least useful: calligraphy. Nobody had told him what it could be good for. About ten years later he found it inside a computer.

[[story]]
In Walter Isaacson's *Steve Jobs*, Jobs enrolls at Reed College in Portland and quits his classes after about six months. His working-class parents' savings are going into his tuition, and he cannot see the value in it. He had no idea what he wanted to do with his life, and no idea how college would help him find out.

He did not leave the campus. He stayed roughly another 18 months and dropped in on classes that interested him. He slept on the floors of friends' rooms, returned empty Coke bottles for the five-cent deposits to buy food, and on Sunday nights walked about seven miles to a Hare Krishna temple for one good meal a week.

One of the things that caught his interest was a calligraphy class. Reed's calligraphy instruction was among the best in the country. The posters around campus and the labels on every drawer were beautifully hand-lettered. Jobs found the lettering beautiful and subtle in a way science could not capture, and it fascinated him.

In that class he learned about serif and sans serif typefaces. He learned how the space between different letter combinations can be varied, and what makes great typography great. None of it had any hope of a practical use in his life at the time.

##About ten years later, when he was designing the first Macintosh, it all came back to him.##

Jobs put it into the computer. He later said that without that single class the Mac would never have had multiple typefaces or proportionally spaced fonts. He added that since Windows copied the Mac, no personal computer might have had them. That is Jobs's own account, and the biography does not present it as the only cause: what he saw at Xerox PARC in 1979 also shaped the Mac's screen.

[[lessons]]
$$**Make room for learning that has no use yet.** An accountant might sign up for a photography course that has nothing to do with her work. The course may be useless to her job for years, but the day her family's small shop needs product photos, she knows what to look at. There is no guarantee of that. It simply keeps open the chance that what you learn out of curiosity turns out to matter later, and the learning is worth having in the meantime.$$

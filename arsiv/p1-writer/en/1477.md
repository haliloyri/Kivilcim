A company backed by Google has a stated aim of solving death. A passage in the book where it appears says that modern medicine has not lengthened our natural life span by a single year. Both statements sit in the same pages.

[[story]]
Calico, short for California Life Company, was announced on 18 September 2013, with Arthur Levinson, a former Genentech chief and Apple chairman, as chief executive. Google's own wording spoke of the challenge of aging and associated diseases, and Time put the question on its cover that month: Can Google solve death? Yuval Noah Harari cites the company in Homo Deus as an early step, and he gives it the aim it was credited with in the press: to solve death.

He reaches Calico through an argument about priests and engineers. In the section "The Last Days of Death" he starts from the UN's right to life. If life is a right, death becomes a violation of it, and Harari puts that as death being a crime against humanity.

Religions tolerated death because they sanctified something beyond earthly life. He asks the reader to picture Christianity, Islam or Hinduism in a world with no death, heaven, hell or reincarnation.

For modern people, he writes, death is a technical problem that we can and should solve. The experts on it change too. Engineers take the place of priests, and a few geeks in a lab can do the job. Harari names the effort the Gilgamesh Project, after the Mesopotamian hero. Its aims include nanobot immune systems that clear blocked arteries, fight bacteria and cancer, and slow or stop aging. In this view death is a technical failure, like a clogged artery, and no longer a verdict.

In the same section he writes that modern medicine has not extended our natural life span by a single year. Its real achievement is preventing premature death.

His forecast is blunt, and it is a political one. Equality is out and immortality is in, and he expects the struggle for eternal youth to become the fiercest political battle yet. In a talk around the book's release he guessed that it would take centuries rather than decades. A review reports that Google cofounder Sergey Brin was interested to learn he would probably die before humanity solves death. A reviewer objects that calling death only a technical problem presents a contested view as settled.

[[lessons]]
$$**The name you give a problem decides who you hand it to.** Call death a technical problem and it goes to engineers rather than priests, and the method follows the name. A family that calls grandpa's forgetfulness a scheduling problem and buys him a calendar may have skipped the doctor because of the label they chose. So it pays to notice which name you are using for a problem and to ask where that name sends you and who gets left out. You can also try a different word for it and see whether the route changes.$$

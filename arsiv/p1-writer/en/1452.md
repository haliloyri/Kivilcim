One line from the Gospel of Matthew runs through nearly the whole of Joseph Murphy's book, and he reads it as an instruction to yourself rather than a blessing. It explains, he says, what happens to a person who says "I can't".

[[story]]
Over and over in *The Power of Your Subconscious Mind*, Joseph Murphy returns to the same line from the Gospel of Matthew: "Be it done unto you as you believe." He treats it as the rule the whole book depends on. He was a minister, and the book reads like a sermon with exercises attached.

Murphy keeps coming back to it because it removes an excuse. He calls it the law of belief. A belief, in his account, is just a thought you have accepted as true. There is nothing mysterious about it, he says, and no belief is a fixed fact about you. It is a thought, and a thought can be replaced. But once a thought hardens into a conviction, the subconscious works it out and the person lives accordingly.

That is why a sentence like "I can't" is more than a description to him. It looks more like planting the result before you begin. Someone who believes they will fail has, in his words, sown failure. The reverse holds too.

As an example he tells of a relative of his who had tuberculosis. The man's son handed him a piece of wood and called it a healing cross. According to Murphy the man recovered overnight, and nobody told him the truth.

It is worth saying plainly what this is. It is Murphy's own account, and the book offers no medical evidence beside it. Nobody should take it as proof that illness gives way to belief. What it shows is the point Murphy wants to make: what a person believes steers what they do next.

His way out begins with changing the belief. The book gives prayer-like exercises and repeated positive statements for it, and leaves the reader to try them and watch what changes. Murphy does not promise a result, he presents the method as an invitation.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you say "I can't", look at what the sentence makes you do.** A belief is a thought you have accepted, so it quietly decides what you try and what you never touch. Someone who wants to learn the guitar but keeps saying "I have no ear" may never buy the first set of strings, and the unbought strings are the belief at work. If you treat Murphy's rule as a hypothesis and watch one sentence for a few days, you can see which small attempts it keeps you from making and take one of them on purpose.$$

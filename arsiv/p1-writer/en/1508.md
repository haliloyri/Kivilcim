No matter how capable or dedicated a head of human resources is, a change team she leads without strong line leadership will never gain the power it needs. Kotter says so flatly, and he names the habit that keeps producing such teams.

[[story]]
In the work behind *Leading Change*, John Kotter calls the second common mistake in big transformations not creating a powerful enough guiding coalition. Major renewal programmes often start with just one or two people. In successful efforts the coalition grows over time, but unless some minimum mass is reached early, he says, nothing much worthwhile happens.

It goes far beyond the head of the organisation being an active supporter. In successful transformations the chairman, president or division general manager, plus another 5 or 15 or 50 people, come together and develop a shared commitment. The group never includes all the most senior executives, because some people will not buy in at first. In the most successful cases, though, it is pretty powerful in titles, information and expertise, reputations and relationships.

Size matters as well. A guiding team may consist of only three to five people in the first year. In big companies it needs to grow to the 20 to 50 range before much progress is made in the later phases. Senior managers form the core, with sometimes board members, a representative of a key customer or even a powerful union leader. Because some members sit outside senior management, the coalition works outside the normal hierarchy. That can be awkward, but Kotter calls it necessary: if the hierarchy worked well, there would be no need for a major transformation.

##Getting the right group together takes someone who keeps people in the room until they share a picture of the problem and some trust.##

Off-site retreats of two or three days are one popular way, and Kotter saw groups of 5 to 35 executives attend a series of them over months.

A common failure is to expect the team to be led by a staff executive from human resources, quality or strategic planning instead of a key line manager. Efforts without a powerful enough coalition can make apparent progress for a while, but sooner or later the opposition gathers itself and stops the change.

The numbers are Kotter's observations, not research findings.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before announcing a change, gather a small group that can carry it.** The group needs titles, knowledge, reputation and relationships, and its members should agree among themselves on what is wrong. A parent who wants to change the household bedtime routine can talk it over with the other adult and the oldest child before explaining it to everyone. That way the announcement comes as a shared decision rather than one person's wish, and you are not left alone at the first objection.$$

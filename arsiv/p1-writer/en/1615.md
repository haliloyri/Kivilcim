Dave Ramsey agrees that paying off the most expensive debt first costs less money. Then he tells people to start somewhere else, with the smallest balance on the list, and he has a reason that has nothing to do with arithmetic.

[[story]]
In *The Total Money Makeover* (2003), Dave Ramsey proposes an order for paying debts that surprises people who know their way around a spreadsheet. You write every debt on one sheet and sort them from the smallest balance to the largest, and the interest rate does not enter into it. The house is left off the list. Ramsey calls the method the debt snowball, and in his seven-step plan it is step two, after a small emergency fund.

The mechanics are simple. You pay the minimum on every debt except the smallest, and every spare dollar goes to the smallest one. When it is gone, the payment you were making on it moves onto the next debt, so the amount you throw at each debt grows with every one you close. The picture Ramsey has in mind is a snowball that picks up more snow as it rolls downhill.

He does not pretend the maths favours him. On paper, clearing the highest-interest debt first is cheaper. He recommends the other order because, as he puts it, personal finance is about 80 percent behaviour and 20 percent head knowledge. The book's subtitle, *A Proven Plan for Financial Fitness*, points the same way, since a plan for money works like a plan for exercise: it helps only to the extent that you keep doing it.

Seeing a small debt disappear gives a person a finished job to point at, and that keeps the effort going. For Ramsey the real danger is a plan that is flawless on paper and abandoned halfway. Someone who stares at one large balance for months with no sign of movement may quit, and then the interest savings on paper are worth nothing. Each line crossed off the list also speeds up the next one, because the payment behind it keeps growing.

[[lessons]]
$$**Choose the plan you will actually finish.** A man clearing out his garage can start with the biggest corner and be worn out by noon, or he can start with the one shelf he can finish in an afternoon and be glad to come back the next day. Ramsey puts the smallest debt first for the same reason: an early finish makes the long job easier to continue. When you plan a big task, you can pick the first step by how soon you can complete it rather than by how efficient it looks on paper, and start seeing progress from the first day.$$

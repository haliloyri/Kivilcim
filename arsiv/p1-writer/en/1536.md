Riders on one cycling team carried their own pillows from hotel to hotel, and a surgeon was brought in to show them how to wash their hands. None of it had anything to do with pedalling.

[[story]]
In *Black Box Thinking* (2015), Matthew Syed tells how Dave Brailsford took over British Cycling in 2003. His method rested on one plain idea: improve every factor that affects a rider by one percent, and stop looking for the single big change. The logic was that one such gain is too small to notice but dozens together can add up. Each improvement was an experiment, kept if the results supported it and dropped if they did not. That way the programme learned what worked by measuring it and did not have to guess.

The list of small things was long and ordinary, because the aim was to make every detail a little better and nobody was after a flashy invention. Riders were given their own pillows and mattresses so they would sleep the same way in any hotel. A massage gel was tested. A surgeon taught them to wash their hands properly, to cut the chance of infection. Accounts of the programme also mention that the floor of the team truck was painted white so that dust would show. None of it looked impressive alone.

The results came within a few years. At the 2008 Beijing Olympics, British cyclists won 8 gold medals. At London in 2012 they won 8 more. That same year Bradley Wiggins, riding for Team Sky, which Brailsford also ran, became the first British winner of the Tour de France.

Wiggins later criticised the "marginal gains" label in harsh terms, according to press reports, so the formula is not beyond argument. Syed ties the story to black box thinking: results are measured, failures are recorded and every change is tested. The medals were not the work of one pillow or one gel. What stood behind them was the sum of many small tested changes, and that causal claim is Syed's own reading.

[[lessons]]
$$**Break a result into its parts and test each part.** A small bakery that changes one thing a week, such as the baking time or the packaging, and writes down how sales moved, learns which change helped without rebuilding the whole shop. Brailsford's pillow and gel trials ran on the same logic: a change stayed only if the measurements backed it. You can pick one part of your own work, change just that, and compare the result with the old one before you decide whether the change deserves to stay.$$

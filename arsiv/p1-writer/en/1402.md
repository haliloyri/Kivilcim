Thirty thousand pairs a year, sold mostly to backwoods outlets and small-town family stores. The company that made Hush Puppies was thinking of phasing the shoe out, and nobody there suspected where the next buyers would come from, or who they would turn out to be.

[[story]]
Hush Puppies were classic American brushed-suede shoes with a lightweight crepe sole. Sales had fallen to 30,000 pairs a year, and Wolverine, the maker, was thinking of dropping the shoe that made the brand famous.

Malcolm Gladwell tells the rest in *The Tipping Point*. At a fashion shoot, two company executives, Owen Baxter and Geoffrey Lewis, ran into a stylist from New York. She told them the classic Hush Puppies had suddenly become hip in the clubs and bars of downtown Manhattan. Resale shops in the Village and Soho were selling them, and people were going to the small stores that still carried them and buying them up.

The executives were baffled. Lewis says they were told Isaac Mizrahi was wearing the shoes himself, and at the time he had no idea who Mizrahi was.

By fall 1995 things happened in a rush. Designer John Bartlett called, wanting the shoes for his spring collection. Anna Sui wanted them for her show. In Los Angeles, Joel Fitzgerald put a twenty-five-foot inflatable basset hound, the brand's symbol, on the roof of his Hollywood store and gutted the gallery next door to turn it into a Hush Puppies boutique. While he was still painting and putting up shelves, the actor Pee-wee Herman walked in and asked for a couple of pairs.

##The first kids wore the shoes because no one else would.##

In 1995 the company sold 430,000 pairs, and the next year four times that. In 1996 Hush Puppies won best accessory at the Council of Fashion Designers awards at Lincoln Center, and the president of the firm went up on stage to accept it. By Gladwell's account his company had almost nothing to do with the achievement. Nobody had run an ad telling people the shoes were cool. The first kids were not trying to start a trend, and the designers used the shoes as an incidental touch in their shows.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask who already uses your product, and why.** What changed the shoe's fate was a small group in the right place, not a large campaign. A bakery that finds its bread on the menu of the cafe next door can ask the owner what customers say about it and how they first heard of it. That lets you decide whom to reach by listening to a real user instead of guessing, and the answer may point somewhere you had never thought to look.$$

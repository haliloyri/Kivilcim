[[open]]
Jonathan Haidt argues that childhood moved from the playground to the phone within a few years, and that teenage anxiety and depression rose with it. One family's refusal cannot reverse that, because its child pays alone.

[[story]]
In *The Anxious Generation*, Haidt calls the change a Great Rewiring of childhood: from a play-based childhood to a phone-based one. In the United States, he reports, rates of anxiety, depression and self-harm among teenagers jumped in those years, most sharply among girls. The shift, he says, took only about five years to complete.

He points to two shifts. Children lost unsupervised play in the real world, and that time went to smartphones and social media. In his words, children were overprotected in the real world and underprotected online. The brain of a human child, like that of any young mammal, expects play: outdoors, without adults, taking small risks of its own choosing. Screens took that place. He lists four harms of the phone-based childhood: social deprivation, sleep deprivation, fragmented attention and addiction.

Haidt says a single family cannot fix this. A child whose parents refuse a phone is left out of the group chat where the whole class is talking, and learns the next day what everyone else already knows. The parents who set the rule make their own child pay for it, and the families who did not set one lose nothing.

So his proposals aim at communities, not households. No smartphone before high school. No social media before sixteen. Phone-free schools. Far more unsupervised play and independence. If phones are locked away for the school day, no child is left out, and the parent who made the rule is no longer alone.

Some researchers dispute that phones caused the rise in teenage distress, and much of the evidence is correlational. What Haidt claims is plain: a rule that is hard to keep alone gets easier when everyone adopts it at once.

[[lessons]]
$$**Make a hard rule together with others who face the same cost.** In Haidt's phone example, one family saying no leaves only its own child outside the group. On a street where every household tries to keep the same quiet hour for the babies, the effort works only if neighbours agree on it, and nobody feels singled out by it. If a habit is hard to change alone, you can look for a few people with the same problem and agree on one shared rule for a month.$$

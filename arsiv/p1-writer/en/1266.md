[[story]]
In the chapter on amor fati in *The Obstacle Is the Way*, Ryan Holiday pairs Edison's burning factory with a second image: the boxer Jack Johnson in his famous fifteen-round fight with Jim Jeffries. Jeffries, the Great White Hope, had been called out of retirement to defeat the ascendant black champion. Johnson was genuinely hated by his opponent and by the crowd.

Johnson enjoyed it anyway. He smiled, joked and played the whole fight. Why not, Holiday asks. Another reaction had no value, and hating them for hating him would only mean picking up a bitterness that belonged to them. He refused to.

Taking the abuse was not all he did, because he designed his fight plan around it. At every nasty remark from Jeffries's corner, he gave his opponent another lacing. At every low trick or rush, he would quip and beat it back without losing his cool. When one well-placed blow opened a cut on his lip, he kept smiling, a gory, bloody but cheerful smile.

Every round he got happier and friendlier as Jeffries grew enraged and tired, and eventually lost the will to fight. The bout ended with Jeffries on the floor and every doubt about Johnson silenced. The novelist Jack London, reporting from ringside, wrote that no one understands this man who smiles. ##The story of the fight is the story of a smile.## If ever a man won by nothing more fatiguing than a smile, London said, Johnson won that day.

Holiday separates the stages. Learning not to kick and scream about what we cannot control is one thing, and indifference and acceptance beat disappointment or rage. That is only a first step. Better is love for all that happens to us. The goal is to get past "I'm okay with this" to "I feel great about it." It is the idea Nietzsche gives at the head of the chapter: greatness is wanting nothing to be different, and not merely bearing what is necessary but loving it.

Holiday makes no claim that this is free or that the good will always outweigh the bad; he writes only that there is always some good, even if barely perceptible at first, contained within the bad, and that we can find it and be cheerful because of it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Turn a difficult behavior into material for your plan.** Johnson did not treat the abuse as an obstacle to his fight, he built the fight around it. A consultant whose client interrupts every meeting could stop dreading it, put the main point in the first sentence and plan one short line to pick the thread back up after each interruption. That turns something you cannot avoid from a complaint into a design condition, and the next meeting becomes a small test of your plan instead of a repeat of the last one.$$

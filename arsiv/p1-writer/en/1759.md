[[open]]
A doctor took a small piece of a woman's tumour and did not ask her permission. The cells in that piece would go on dividing in a laboratory long after she was dead.

[[story]]
Henrietta Lacks was a Black woman from Virginia, a mother of five, from a family that worked a tobacco farm. In 1951 she was treated for cervical cancer at Johns Hopkins. A doctor took a sample from her tumour without her knowing. At that time practice did not require consent.

Henrietta came to the hospital because of a pain she could not explain. In those years the hospital had separate arrangements for Black patients, who were treated in a separate ward. Her treatment began there, and her tumour was sampled.

According to Rebecca Skloot's *The Immortal Life of Henrietta Lacks*, the cells kept on dividing in the lab. No human cells had managed that before. The cells, called HeLa, became the first known immortal human cell line.

When she came for treatment, she had pain in her abdomen. Black patients at that hospital waited in a separate corridor. She died in October 1951, aged 31.

Her cells were used in work on the polio vaccine and in many other fields. They helped with large-scale testing of the vaccine, and an industry grew around them. Her children did not know for years that their mother's cells were travelling the world.

Her family did not learn of it for decades. Some members could not afford their own health insurance. Science was using one woman's body while her children stayed outside the story.

The doctor who grew the cells, George Gey, was among the first to keep human cells alive in culture. That was a turning point in medicine. Henrietta's cells kept dividing even though she had died. Her family only learned of it years later, from a phone call.

##Skloot shows that one of medicine's great gifts was taken from a forgotten woman's body, without her knowledge.## The cells lived on, while the woman who gave them her name stayed nameless for a long time.

[[lessons]]
$$**If you benefit from someone's contribution, tell them and say their name.** A team presenting a project might give the credit to one person, though others supplied the data or the labour. Adding a slide with their names makes it visible who did the work. That helps you remember whose shoulders the progress stands on. It costs a minute and changes how the work is told.$$

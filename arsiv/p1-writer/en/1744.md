On one small island, people decided to kill every one of their pigs. Nobody forced them to. On another island, far away, people kept cutting trees until the last one fell, and nothing like that decision ever came.

[[story]]
Jared Diamond sets two Pacific islands side by side in *Collapse*. The first is Easter Island, where people cut down the forest to move and raise giant statues. In Diamond's reading, the statues were part of a contest between rival groups to show strength. As the trees went, the soil began to wash away, canoes could no longer be built, and food sources shrank along with the forest.

The second island, Tikopia, covers less than five square kilometres. Diamond describes it as a society that lasted a very long time on that small patch. Its people lived on limited land too, but they saw the problem coming.

The pigs were wrecking the gardens and competing with people for food. The Tikopians decided to kill every pig on the island. Giving up something you already have is never easy, and they did it anyway.

They treated their own numbers with the same seriousness. The island could not feed an unlimited population, so the Tikopians deliberately limited how many people lived there, by methods Diamond describes in the book. The land became a densely tended garden of tree crops, close to one orchard from end to end.

The two choices were linked. The land could carry only as many people as it could feed, and the Tikopians changed their own habits to stay inside that limit.

The statues stayed where they stood. The forest that could have moved them was gone.

Diamond puts the two islands together and asks why one society saw a limit coming while the other did not. ##The Tikopians chose to give up something valuable before the shortage arrived.## On Easter Island, by the time the last tree was cut, there was very little left to choose.

[[lessons]]
$$**Give up something you value before you are forced to.** A small café owner can drop a menu item that wears out the kitchen every evening while sales are still good. If she waits, she has to pull the same item in a hurry while money is already leaking. Letting it go early lets her decide calmly what has to stay alive, and it frees time for the work that remains. The cost of choosing is smaller than the cost of being chosen for.$$

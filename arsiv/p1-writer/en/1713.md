Ryan Holiday was still young when he became an executive at American Apparel. He had risen fast, crises kept breaking around the company, and the more his ambition grew, the harder it became to say out loud that he had gotten something wrong. He only saw it once he stepped back.

[[story]]
Ryan Holiday opens *Ego Is the Enemy* with his own rise. The introduction tells of his early success first and the setbacks that followed it second. Years earlier he had left college to apprentice under the author Robert Greene, and in that role he had chosen, on purpose, to be a student.

Then came the fast climb. While still young, he became an executive at American Apparel, where he watched the crises around the company and his own ambition up close. It showed him that success does not make a person wise on its own.

As approval from outside grew, admitting a mistake got harder. Keeping quiet and learning from someone else, the easy part of the apprenticeship, had become the hardest job in the executive's chair. He was the same person who had started out wanting to learn, and what had changed was the amount of approval around him.

Holiday turned that break into the starting point of his book. As he sees it, wherever you are and whatever you are doing, your worst enemy already lives inside you: your ego. He defines ego as an unhealthy belief in our own importance and follows it through three stages, when you aspire, when you succeed, and when you fail. The book's three parts are built in that order.

One chapter in the first part is titled "Talk, Talk, Talk," since ego prefers talking to doing. Another is "Become a Student." A third asks "To Be or To Do?", a choice between being someone and doing something. His apprenticeship years became the book's first piece of advice.

##Young success had narrowed Holiday's view.## In the second stage, ego makes success intoxicating and blinding, and a person can stop learning without noticing. Holiday does not suggest trying to destroy the ego. He suggests keeping the student's stance on purpose after the win, as it came naturally in the apprenticeship.

[[lessons]]
$$**Keep asking for feedback after a win.** A small bakery that delivers its first big order well and then stops asking customers "what did we miss?" will be the last to learn about its flaws. Approval turns the volume of criticism down, so hearing it takes a deliberate effort. If you ask someone about your mistakes right after a success, you turn that effort into one concrete step and catch what slipped by while it is still easy to fix.$$

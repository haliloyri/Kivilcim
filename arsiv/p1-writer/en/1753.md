When two groups of survivors from the same shipwreck got back to England, there were two different stories. Each blamed the other. So who was telling the truth, and who was going to decide?

[[story]]
HMS Wager was part of Anson's expedition, whose targets were Spanish ships. In 1741 the ship ran onto rocks off the coast of southern Chile, near an island, and the survivors lived on that shore for a time, fighting hunger and cold. Then they split into two parties.

According to David Grann's *The Wager*, one group, led by the gunner John Bulkeley, set out in a lengthened boat, went through the Strait of Magellan and reached Brazil. The others, among them Captain David Cheap and the young officer John Byron, came home much later by another route, with the help of local people.

The two accounts did not match. Each framed the other's conduct in terms of mutiny and the killing of a sailor. Cheap had shot a seaman in anger, and Bulkeley's side had defied Cheap. Bulkeley's journal was printed in 1743, and Byron published his own narrative only in 1768, twenty-odd years later.

Back in England, the Admiralty held a court martial on the loss of the ship. Cheap was found to have done his duty, one officer was reprimanded, and the mutiny was not prosecuted. The court left open the question of which group had told the truth. Four men from Cheap's party had finally reached Chile.

The accounts also differed on who had given which orders, and on whose conduct on the shore had been fair. Neither side's version cleared the other.

The Admiralty was facing two accounts of one event, and who was right was not easy to settle. Grann says the Admiralty chose not to punish anyone. ##Two accounts from the same wreck contradicted each other, and the court ruled for neither.## Surviving did not bring a shared version of what happened.

[[lessons]]
$$**When two sides tell the same event differently, look at what each gains from its version.** Two colleagues describing the same customer complaint may each shrink their own error and enlarge the other's. Asking each of them separately what they saw and what they did lets you hold your verdict back. By the time you decide, you have more than the single version that reached you first. Nothing in that approach requires you to distrust anyone in advance.$$

Robert Liston could take off a leg in about half a minute, and a study guide gives the time as 28 seconds. Speed was the surgeon's pride. Joseph Lister would go after something no one in the operating room could see, with a bottle of carbolic acid.

[[story]]
Lindsey Fitzharris opens *The Butchering Art* with Liston's operation in December 1846, a mid-thigh amputation. Surgeons of that time were proud of gowns stiff with old blood, and some called the smell the good old surgical stink. Wounds were believed to go bad because of bad air. Hospitals had no way to wash wounds or hands, and surgeons did not have to wash before they touched a patient.

Joseph Lister worked in Glasgow. In 1851, during an outbreak of erysipelas on a ward, he had noticed that patients who had just been operated on were infected worst. Later a chemistry professor, Thomas Anderson, drew his attention to Louis Pasteur's work showing that microscopic organisms cause rot. Lister decided that the pus in wounds came from them.

Pasteur had suggested filtering them out, heating them or killing them with chemicals, and the first two could not be used on a wound. Lister tried Condy's fluid, a household disinfectant, and the limb still festered. He tried zinc chloride, salicylic acid, thymol and iodine. None suited. Then he heard that carbolic acid had been used to treat sewage in Carlisle, and he asked Anderson for a sample.

In August 1865 an eleven-year-old boy, James Greenlees, was brought to Glasgow with a compound fracture, the bone through the skin. Lister dressed the wound with a pad soaked in carbolic acid. The wound did not fester, and the boy's leg healed.

He published his results in the Lancet in 1867 and later developed a carbolic spray for the operating room, along with absorbable catgut stitches to replace silk thread that carried infection. Many surgeons, though, refused for years to believe that invisible organisms mattered, and they resisted him.

[[lessons]]
$$**Treat a cause you cannot see as real when evidence points to it.** Lister could not see what was inside a wound, but Pasteur's work and a failed first dressing told him where to look. A baker whose loaves go stale too fast can check the bench, the flour store and the cloths instead of shrugging that it happens sometimes. Testing the unseen suspect once tells you whether it was the problem, and the next batch comes out of a kitchen you understand better.$$

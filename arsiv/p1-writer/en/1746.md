A bronze tablet found at Lyon carries a version of an emperor's speech to the Roman senate. The speech argued for letting the leading men of Gaul sit among the senators. Rome was handing seats to the grandsons of its enemies.

[[story]]
Mary Beard's *SPQR* explains Rome's strength through a habit as much as through war. Rome gave citizenship to peoples it had defeated and to the slaves it freed. Many Greek cities guarded membership closely, while Rome kept the door ajar.

The children of freed slaves were Roman citizens too. That was not a common rule in the ancient world. Beard notes that even Rome's own foundation stories describe a city filled by people who came from elsewhere. In a generation or two, a former slave's family could blend into the families of citizens.

Under the emperor Claudius, this attitude was argued out in the senate. The leading men of Gaul were to be admitted, and a version of the speech survives on a bronze tablet found at Lyon. In the version preserved by Tacitus, Claudius points to Athens and Sparta, strong cities that weakened because they kept their defeated as outsiders. Rome, he said, had done the opposite.

The title *SPQR* is a Latin abbreviation for "the Senate and People of Rome". Citizenship there was a real status with legal and tax consequences. Opening it to outsiders was not a decorative gesture. It changed how the state worked.

Centuries later, in AD 212, the emperor Caracalla went further and gave citizenship to almost every free person in the empire. People from Spain to Syria came under Roman law with one decision. Some of the consequences were about taxes, and Beard treats the reasons for the grant as debated.

##Rome did not leave the people it defeated as outsiders. It made them members.## The Lyon tablet shows that this reached all the way to the senate. A bronze slab is a small piece of evidence for a large claim, but it is a concrete one.

[[lessons]]
$$**Treat newcomers as full members from the start.** A newcomer who only watches practice at a sports club cannot contribute what they are good at. When someone hands them a real job, such as drawing up the match schedule, both the club and the newcomer start to own the result. That shows you what the new person can bring and makes belonging come from the work itself. It also means you stop speaking of them as a guest.$$

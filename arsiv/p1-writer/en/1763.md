A French chemist published the melting point and density of a metal he had just isolated, and a Russian told him those numbers had to be wrong. Shaped into a spoon, the same metal vanishes in a cup of hot tea.

[[story]]
In 1875 the French chemist Paul-Émile Lecoq de Boisbaudran became the first person to separate gallium from its ore, hold it in his hand and describe it. He published its melting point and its density.

Mendeleev had good reason to speak up. He had built his periodic table while writing a textbook, under a deadline that pushed him to arrange the 62 elements then known into one compact chart, and the chart had a gap below aluminium. He had predicted the element that belonged there, calling it eka-aluminium and writing down what it should be like. Gallium sat in that gap. Kean calls Mendeleev the father of the periodic table, though not the only person to have thought of one.

Looking at Lecoq de Boisbaudran's numbers, Mendeleev said they must be wrong. He was right. Lecoq de Boisbaudran had to correct his data and publish it again, and Sam Kean, in *The Disappearing Spoon*, reads that exchange as proof the periodic table could predict: a theory had corrected an experiment. Mendeleev then tried to claim more of the credit than he deserved.

Kean's title comes from a prank played with the same metal. Someone hands a guest a cup of hot tea or coffee and a gallium spoon. The guest takes it for aluminium, which gallium sits directly below in the table and closely resembles, and starts to stir. Gallium melts at about 30 degrees Celsius, so the spoon goes soft and dissolves in the cup.

Kean wrote the book to show that the table touches art, politics and war as well as chemistry. In the 1940s, cups lined with cadmium made people ill, and Kenneth Parker, heir to the founder of the Parker pen company, profited from a fountain pen tipped with ruthenium. Stan Jones, a US Senate candidate, drank liquid silver against the Y2K "virus", and his skin turned permanently blue. He told a magazine he would do it again.

[[lessons]]
$$**Open a dry subject with its strangest true detail.** A training manager facing a room that dreads a compliance session could start with one odd incident from a real case before showing a single slide. Once the room is curious about what happened, the dull rules have a story to hang on. The listeners can retell that incident afterwards to people who were not in the room, and the rules travel with it. The manager also learns quickly, and without any survey, whether the detail was strange enough, because the room either leans in or checks its phones.$$

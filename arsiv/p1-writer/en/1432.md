Don Buckout was a district sales manager at Intel, a long way from headquarters. He sat down and wrote a telex of eight pages, and it reached the company's president. What it said was that a competitor was winning, in a market where Intel's own 16-bit chip was slipping.

[[story]]
Late in 1979, Intel's 8086, the company's first high-performance 16-bit microprocessor, was losing ground to Motorola's 68000. In *Measure What Matters*, John Doerr tells what happened next under the name Operation Crush. It began with Buckout's eight-page telex to headquarters.

His boss, Casey Powell, forwarded it to Andy Grove, then Intel's president and COO. The executive staff met within a week, and a task force formed the week after. It judged Zilog a minor threat and Motorola, an industry giant, the real danger.

The objective was one sentence: establish the 8086 as the highest performance 16-bit microprocessor family. It came with a countable target of 2,000 design wins, meaning customers agreeing to build the chip into their own devices and products.

In January 1980, Crush teams went out to field offices around the world, carrying videos of Grove. By the second quarter the whole sales organization was running the new approach. Intel changed no product for the campaign. It changed how it sold, putting long-term systems and service ahead of short-term ease of programming, and aiming its pitch at executives rather than at programmers.

##By the end of 1980, Intel had beaten Motorola.## In the third quarter it had been on track for the 2,000, and the cheaper 8088 later powered the first IBM PC. In 1986, when Intel left memory chips to focus on microprocessors, the 8086 family had won back 85% of the 16-bit market.

Doerr also points to culture. Grove expected people to bring problems upward, and Buckout and Powell did so without fear of punishment. That is how a telex from a district office reached the president's desk at all.

[[lessons]]
$$**Write a goal as one sentence and one number.** A goal that everyone can repeat and everyone can count lets a whole group pull in the same direction. The owner of a small café who writes "serve the best breakfast in the neighborhood" and adds how many breakfast plates a week would count as success gives the staff something to aim at on every shift. When the week gets messy, a number to check keeps the plan from drifting, and it tells you when you have arrived.$$

Silver medalists at the Olympics looked unhappier than bronze medalists, even though they had finished a place higher. The difference had nothing to do with the medals. It sat in the head of each athlete, in the other result each one was imagining.

[[story]]
In *The Power of Regret*, Daniel Pink describes a study of the 1992 Barcelona Olympics. Victoria Medvec and Thomas Gilovich of Cornell and Scott Madey of the University of Toledo collected video of about three dozen silver and bronze medalists. The footage showed their faces right after the event and on the podium. Neutral raters did the scoring, and they judged only the expressions.

Raters scored the athletes' expressions for happiness on a 10-point scale. Right after their events, bronze medalists averaged 7.1 and silver medalists 4.8. Only one place separated the two finishes. The athletes who had done better looked less happy, and bronze medalists looked happier on the podium too.

The researchers also looked at what the athletes said in interviews. Bronze medalists made more "at least" statements. Silver medalists made more "if only" statements.

##The silver medalist's mind was on the gold just missed, and the bronze medalist's on the fourth-place finisher with no medal.##

Pink calls an imagined alternative a counterfactual. "If only" looks upward at something better, while "at least" looks downward at something worse. Same podium, similar medal, and the feeling depended on which imagined result the athlete looked at.

For the silver medalist, gold was one place away and lost. For the bronze medalist, going home with nothing was one place away and avoided. The arithmetic was the same, but it pointed in opposite directions. Pink ties this to the book's larger idea: regret is the mind asking what might have been, and the direction of that question shapes how a result feels. The athletes knew exactly how they had finished. What differed was the imagined result each kept beside it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Choose which comparison you make.** A student who barely passes an exam can compare herself with the classmate who came first and see the points she missed. She can also think of a friend who failed and see what she has. Each is an accurate reading of the same grade, yet each leaves a very different mood. Noticing which comparison you are making lets you look at a result next to both of them and then decide on purpose which one to keep in mind.$$

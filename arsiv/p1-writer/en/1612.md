[[open]]
A formula with three inputs sorts big savers from big spenders: your age, your yearly pre-tax income, and the number ten. Two people with the same paycheck can land far apart on it, and the gap says more than either person's salary.

[[story]]
In *The Millionaire Next Door*, Thomas Stanley set out to study America's wealthy and found that what you can see of wealth and what sits in the bank often differ. Many of the millionaires his research describes lived in ordinary houses and drove ordinary cars. Many people with high incomes and visible spending had little to show for it.

To make that gap measurable, the book offers a yardstick. Multiply your age by your annual pre-tax income from all sources except inheritance, then divide by ten. The result is the net worth you would be expected to have.

Stanley then sorts people by how they compare with that number. Someone whose actual net worth is at least twice the expected figure is a prodigious accumulator of wealth, a PAW. Someone whose net worth is less than half of it is an under accumulator of wealth, a UAW.

The yardstick measures a household against its own income, so a large paycheck raises the bar instead of clearing it. A doctor earning far more than a schoolteacher is expected to have far more, and may still land in the UAW group.

##The surprise is who falls where: high income and large wealth often did not go together.##

The book describes the PAWs as people who live below their means and who invest steadily. Their lives look modest because the money went somewhere other than the driveway. The UAWs often looked the opposite way, with a good income, a new car and a large house, and a thin balance sheet underneath.

The formula is a rule of thumb drawn from Stanley's research, not a law, and it ignores inheritance and the different costs of living in different places. Its value is the question it forces: how much of what you earned have you kept?

[[lessons]]
$$**Measure savings against income, not against neighbors.** A plumber who looks at a neighbor's new truck and feels behind has learned little about his own position. Comparing what he has kept to what he has earned over the years tells him far more, and it works whatever the neighbor drives. Do that sum once and decide whether to raise your savings before you upgrade anything. Then redo it each year, since income changes and the expected figure moves with it, and you will see whether the gap is closing.$$

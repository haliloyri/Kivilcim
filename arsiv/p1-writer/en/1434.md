Everyone at the company had goals, yet many of the goals did not fit together, and teams stalled waiting on one another. The fix that worked was a meeting where each head of department said, out loud, which other teams they were counting on.

[[story]]
In *Measure What Matters*, John Doerr gives alignment a chapter built on MyFitnessPal, written by its co-founder and CEO Mike Lee. Lee found that aligning around OKRs was harder, and more rewarding, than he had expected.

He and his brother Albert noticed that many people's OKRs did not line up. Teams got stuck because they depended on other teams. Everyone looked at their own goals, but nobody could see whose work those goals needed. One team's finished work was another team's waiting work, and the first team often did not know it.

The fix was a regular meeting. Department heads presented their OKRs, and as they presented they named the teams they depended on.

##A dependency was spoken before anyone got stuck waiting on it.## When one team said which other team its goal rested on, that other team heard it too.

In the book each OKR has a single owner, and other teams connect to it as needed, which gives them one person to ask. The chapter is titled "Align: The MyFitnessPal Story." After Under Armour acquired MyFitnessPal, the company had to face scale and integration, and OKRs became even more important. Lee wrote that he wished they had put OKRs in gear earlier, even before the company was funded.

Doerr describes alignment as running in two directions. Goals line up with the company's top-level objectives, and teams connect with one another and acknowledge their dependencies. Transparency feeds both. He writes that OKRs promised transparency to a Google team that defaulted to open: open source, open systems, open web. At Intuit, chief information officer Atticus Tysen says the key was making all OKRs visible across the company.

[[lessons]]
$$**Say which team you depend on.** Work usually stalls when one person is waiting on another and the other person has no idea. An interior designer who needs the painter finished before the curtains go up can tell the painter so, and both can plan their dates around each other. When a dependency is spoken, it stops being a surprise and becomes a date you can agree on, and you start to see who depends on you, too.$$

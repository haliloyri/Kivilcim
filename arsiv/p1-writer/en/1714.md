The recorder is running and the person across the table has gone quiet. Journalist Kate Murphy realized that this quiet usually does not mean listening. People were silent while the other person spoke, but not to understand them. They were using the pause to draft what they would say next.

[[story]]
Kate Murphy spent years as a journalist sitting across from people and talking with them. In *You're Not Listening* she describes something she kept seeing in her interviews. Even when the person across from her was quiet, he was often not listening, because the silence was being used to prepare a reply. That small gap, with the recorder still running, is where the book starts.

For Murphy, real listening asks you to put up with three things. You do not know where the conversation is going. You do not control the question. And what you hear may change your mind. All three mean letting go of control, and most of us find that uncomfortable.

Phones distract us, yet the book puts the main obstacle earlier, in the wish to run the conversation. If you are drafting your own answer, the other person's sentence is only raw material for your next move. Putting the phone in your pocket does not fix that by itself.

There is a mental side as well. We think faster than we speak or hear, so while someone finishes a sentence our mind has spare time. That gap is usually filled with what we plan to say. The silence looks like listening, but something quite different is going on inside.

Murphy also brings in a distinction from the sociologist Charles Derber. A shift-response turns the conversation toward the listener's own experience. A support-response keeps the focus on the speaker, for instance by asking a follow-up question. In the first, the turn changes hands. In the second, the speaker is still the speaker.

##Listening is hard not because it needs silence, but because it needs you to give up control of the talk.## Silence alone does not count. Listening means giving up the right to decide, ahead of time, where the conversation will go.

[[lessons]]
$$**Let the other person steer the conversation.** When a friend describes a rough start in a new job and you are already preparing your advice, you are still listening to your own sentence. If you drop the reply and ask one question about what they just said, what you hear can change your view. That shows them you took their words seriously and puts you in front of a perspective you had not considered before.$$

[[open]]
One second before a man pulls a trigger, something in his brain has already moved. Robert Sapolsky starts there and keeps stepping backwards in time, and every step produces another true answer to why.

[[story]]
The first chapter of Sapolsky's *Behave* takes one behavior, a violent act, and asks why it happened. He answers on a series of clocks, each of which becomes its own part of the book. Nothing he says is allowed to rank above anything else.

One second before, the question is which brain regions were active, such as the amygdala and the frontal cortex. The frontal cortex is the part that restrains impulses and the last region of the brain to mature, around the middle of the twenties. Seconds to minutes before, it is what the person saw, heard or smelled that set things off. Sapolsky shows that even a split between us and them can show up in how the brain responds to a stranger's face.

Hours to days before, hormones and recent stress come in. Sapolsky explains that testosterone does not create aggression. It amplifies tendencies already present. Months and years before, the question becomes the person's experiences and the changes of adolescence, and further back lie childhood, the fetal environment, genes and culture, where individualist and collectivist societies teach people different things. At the far end sits the long history of evolution.

He argues that all of these layers are true at once. Putting the brain in a different category from the environment is a mistake, because both are links of one chain, and the book follows the chain backward through chapter after chapter, each leaning on a different science.

Which one we choose to call the real cause changes how we judge the person. Ask about the choice made a moment before and you meet one kind of human being. Ask about the years before and you meet another.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask what came before a behavior at more than one scale of time.** Sapolsky's list runs from one second before the act to the history of the species, and every level has something to say. When a neighbour slams a door on you in the corridor, calling him rude is quick but incomplete, since you do not know how his morning or his week has gone. If you ask what happened before, you give yourself a fairer judgment and a better chance of answering him in a way that fits what actually happened.$$

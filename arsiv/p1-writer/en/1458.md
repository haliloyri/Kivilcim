[[open]]
In a lab in Parma, a macaque sits perfectly still while a thin wire listens to one cell in its brain. That cell normally fires when the monkey reaches for a peanut. Right now the monkey is not reaching. Someone else is.

[[story]]
The researchers were Giacomo Rizzolatti's group at the University of Parma, and V.S. Ramachandran describes their work in *The Tell-Tale Brain*. They were recording from single neurons in the premotor cortex of macaques, a region that helps plan movement. Recording from one cell, rather than a cluster, meant they could say exactly what that cell did and when. It is patient work: you wait for the cell to speak and note what the animal is doing at that second. One neuron fired when the monkey reached out and took a peanut.

Then came the part nobody expected. The same neuron fired when the monkey sat still and watched someone else reach for a peanut. Its own hand did nothing. It was a strange thing to see on a recording: a cell that belongs to the hand's own movement, active while the hand lay quiet.

These cells came to be called mirror neurons, because another creature's action was echoed inside the watcher. Ramachandran believes humans have them too, and that they may help us imitate what we see and read what another person is about to do. Learning by watching, which children do all day, looks a little less mysterious from here. Rebuilding someone else's movement inside yourself would be much harder without cells like these.

He gives a touch-sensitive subset the nickname Gandhi neurons, since they blur the line between you and someone else. He also suggests that the social difficulties in autism could be linked to a fault in this system. Both ideas are his hypotheses, not settled findings.

And he predicted a big role for them: mirror neurons, he said, would do for psychology what DNA did for biology, giving a scattered field one framework. That was a forecast. Parts of the mirror-neuron story have been criticised since, and the claims about empathy are still open.

[[lessons]]
$$**Show people what you want them to learn before you explain it.** The monkey's cell responded even though the monkey did nothing, which suggests that watching is already a kind of practice, and that words are not the only way to teach. A nurse showing a new colleague how to change a dressing can do two of them herself, talking very little, before she hands over the gloves. When the newcomer finally tries, the hands already have a rough idea of the sequence, and the few remarks she makes land on something that has been seen.$$

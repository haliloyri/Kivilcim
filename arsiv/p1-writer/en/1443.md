A cold room, a dial on the wall, a hand turning it all the way to the top. It feels like the obvious move, and a great many people make it. The heating does not work the way that hand assumes, and neither does the fridge in the kitchen.

[[story]]
In the old thermostat that Don Norman describes in *The Design of Everyday Things*, a single dial sets the temperature you want. Walk into a cold room and the hand goes straight to the top, because people believe that will warm the place faster. It does not. The dial only decides when the heating shuts off, and the heat output is the same whatever number it points at. Hand on the dial or not, the same amount of heat comes out.

The refrigerator is a stranger case. It has two controls, one marked for the freezer and one for the fresh-food compartment, and users take each to set the temperature of its own space. In fact one control sets the compressor level, and the other shifts how the cold air is divided between the two compartments. The labels suggest two separate settings, but the compartments are tied together.

So the trouble lands in the food. Make the freezer colder and the fresh-food section chills too, until a salad freezes. Make the fresh-food section colder and it pulls cold out of the freezer, and the ice cream begins to thaw. Fix one shelf and the other pays for it.

Then there is the waiting. The temperatures take about a day to settle, so after every turn of a control you leave the fridge alone and come back to see what it did. Getting the settings right takes about a week.

Norman's diagnosis is that the controls suggest a false conceptual model. According to the book, a conceptual model is usually highly simplified and does not have to be complete or even accurate, as long as it is useful. The test is whether the picture in the user's head is good enough to predict what the device will do. The dial turned to the top and the two labelled controls both fail it.

One critic of the example argues that the fridge's real flaw lies in the controls themselves, not in how they are labelled.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask what people think a control does, then fix the setup where they are wrong.** A picture in someone's head can differ from what the machine really does, and nobody notices until something goes wrong. Say a repair shop's new booking screen has a button called Hold, and the staff assume it reserves the part when it only hides the order from the list. The manager can ask each person what they think Hold does before the first busy day. If the answers differ, she renames the button or changes what it does, and the mistake never reaches a customer.$$

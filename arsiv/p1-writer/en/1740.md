Inside an espresso bar in Milan, a Starbucks marketing manager was watching the people more than the coffee. A barista stood behind the counter and regulars stood in front of it, and the room had a hum of conversation. When he got back to Seattle he would have to describe this to his bosses.

[[story]]
Howard Schultz tells the story in *Pour Your Heart Into It*, written with Dori Jones Yang. He had joined Starbucks in 1982 and was running its marketing. In 1983 he travelled to Milan on business, and there he walked into the Italian espresso bars.

What he noticed there was that baristas and customers seemed to know one another. People used these bars as meeting places, and coffee was only part of what the place offered. Starbucks at the time sold roasted beans and equipment, and did not make drinks to order in its stores.

Schultz saw in Milan what Starbucks was missing, and he thought it mattered for the company's future, because what the stores lacked was an experience, not another product. Back in Seattle he took what he had seen to the founders' table and argued that Starbucks should serve espresso drinks as well as sell beans.

##Schultz could not convince anyone, but he asked for a corner.##

The founders, led by Jerry Baldwin, held back. One reason was that they saw themselves as coffee merchants and not as people in the restaurant business, and the idea sat far from the company's identity as they understood it.

In April 1984, after Milan, he was allowed to run a small espresso bar in a corner of Starbucks's sixth Seattle store, at Fourth and Spring. It was a modest trial, a corner inside an existing store, yet it let him test in Seattle what he had seen in Italy. Even after the trial, the founders declined to expand it. Schultz left in 1985 and started his own espresso-bar company, Il Giornale, modeled on the Milan bars, and the book goes on to describe how he later bought Starbucks.

[[lessons]]
$$**Behind what you sell, customers may be coming for something else, and you can see it by watching how people use the place.** A bookshop sells books, but some visitors come to linger among the shelves, and a manager who only counts sales might never notice. Schultz saw in Milan that the espresso was an occasion and the real draw was the company. If you watch customers while they use the space and not only while they pay, you find the need underneath your product earlier.$$

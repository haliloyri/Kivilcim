Six-tenths of a second. That is what separated Washington from Italy at the line in Berlin, and a thousand metres earlier nobody would have bet on it. The gap tells you less than what nine men did to close it.

[[story]]
Halfway down the two-thousand-metre course, the Washington eight was behind. Stroke Don Hume was ill. Coxswain Bobby Moch called for a higher rate and, for a moment, considered asking Joe Rantz to take over setting the rhythm instead. Hume answered the call and lifted the beat. That mattered more than it sounds, because the stroke sets the rhythm for everyone behind him, and a sick stroke who cannot hold it can drag the whole boat off its pace. The boat did not fall apart. It settled, and then it began to move.

The race had gone wrong from the start. The crew drew lane six, the outside lane, and could hardly hear the call to go. A boat that begins like that wants only one thing, and every rower in it feels the same pull: to dig in harder and make up the loss with muscle.

In Daniel James Brown's *The Boys in the Boat*, that urge was exactly what these men had been taught to distrust. They were young men from working-class families, not wealthy rowing clubs, and Rantz was among those working to stay at the university. As a teenager he had been left to fend for himself, and he had learned to rely only on himself. In a shell, that habit was a handicap, because nobody can row an eight alone.

Boat builder George Pocock and coach Al Ulbrickson kept returning to one idea they called swing: eight rowers and a coxswain moving as one body. Strength alone did not produce it, and the strongest arms could slow a boat if they pulled out of step. Rantz's whole history pushed him the other way, toward trusting nobody's pace but his own.

So the comeback in Berlin was not a burst of nine private efforts. It was nine men fitting themselves to one rhythm, the stroke, the coxswain and the seven rowers between them. In the last stretch the boat came through and won, 0.6 seconds ahead of Italy, with Germany a little further back. Gold was won in the second thousand metres, from the worst lane in the race, by a crew that had learned to move together.

[[lessons]]
$$**When you fall behind, match the rhythm before you add force.** Rantz had to give up his lifelong habit of trusting only himself and fit his pull to the men beside him, because a trailing boat that pulls harder out of step only slows down. In your own group the same thing happens when a deadline slips and each person speeds up alone: the efforts collide instead of adding up. Listen to the pace the others are holding and settle into it first. Then the same effort carries further, and you feel it almost at once, without working any harder.$$

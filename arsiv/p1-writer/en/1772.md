For about ninety-six minutes, Charles Whitman fired from the tower at the University of Texas in Austin. The evening before, he had typed a note asking that someone look inside his head after he died.

[[story]]
In *Incognito*, David Eagleman uses Whitman's case to ask how much of a decision belongs to the conscious self. Whitman, a former Marine, killed his mother first and then his wife. In the late morning he climbed the tower and fired on the people below until police shot him.

The note he typed on the evening before spoke of unusual thoughts and worsening headaches, which he called tremendous during a stressful stretch in May. He asked for an autopsy, hoping it would find a biological cause for what he was doing, and for cremation afterward. His father authorized the autopsy. In March, at his wife's urging, Whitman had gone to a campus psychiatrist, who noted his hostility and his fantasies of shooting people from the tower. He did not return for the follow-up, the only professional help he is known to have sought.

At the autopsy the pathologist, Coleman de Chenar, found a pecan-sized tumor in the brain, near the amygdala, a region involved in fear and emotion. He first called it an astrocytoma. After a three-hour hearing, a commission found it was a glioblastoma. Its psychiatrists said a link between the tumor and the shooting could not be established with clarity, but the tumor conceivably could have affected his control of emotion and action. The neurologists said that current knowledge did not explain what he did.

Eagleman does not use the case to excuse the acts. He uses it to argue that biology shapes behavior in ways the person cannot see, and that asking only who is to blame misses what could be learned and prevented. He adds a case from medicine: some Parkinson's patients on a dopamine drug developed a compulsion to gamble, and the behavior ended when the drug was stopped.

[[lessons]]
$$**Count your body as one of the deciders when you make an important decision.** Whitman's tumor suggests a choice may not come from a single conscious self at all. A bookkeeper who signs off a big supplier contract at the end of a long day may see it differently the next morning, because sleep and hunger had a say in it. If you make large decisions when you are rested, you let yourself ask afterwards whether the choice was really yours.$$

A hand turns a faucet in one second. The tub takes minutes to fill. Donella Meadows starts her account of systems in the gap between those two speeds: the quick thing you do, and the slow thing you are actually watching.

[[story]]
Meadows draws the plainest system she can think of: a bathtub. The water in it is the stock, the faucet is the inflow, and the drain is the outflow. A stock is whatever you can see, feel, count or measure at a given moment, and in a tub that means the water level.

Turning the faucet takes a second. Filling the tub takes minutes. The level does not follow your hand; it follows the difference between what runs in and what runs out. If the two match, the water stays where it is, and if more comes in than leaves, it climbs.

When faucet and drain are both open and run at the same rate, water keeps moving through the tub and the level never twitches. Someone glancing in would call the tub still. Looking at the level shows you the result, and it can hide the two flows that produce it.

There is also more than one way to fill a tub. You can open the faucet wider, or you can close the drain, and the level rises either way. To change a stock, you have to look at the outflow as well as the inflow.

##The hand moves in a second, and the level takes minutes to catch up.##

Meadows says stocks change slowly even when the flows into or out of them change suddenly. That is why she calls them delays, buffers and shock absorbers: they let what comes in and what goes out be out of step for a while. She counts delays among the strongest forces shaping how a system behaves, and the lag can cause trouble as readily as it can hold things steady.

The trouble comes through feedback. A loop that has to wait for a stock to change before it can respond is likely to swing back and forth, because the information it acts on arrives late. The steadiness comes from the same slowness. Close the faucet and the tub does not empty at that moment; the level stays, and the drain only wears it down over time. A stock that took long to build takes long to drain.

[[lessons]]
$$**Judge a change after the level has had time to move.** In Meadows's bathtub, someone who checks the water one second after turning the faucet sees no difference and might decide the faucet does nothing. The flow has already changed; the level simply needs minutes to follow it. And the faucet is not the only lever, since closing the drain raises the level too. Before you call a new measure a failure, ask which stock it is meant to change, whether the gap between inflow and outflow has really shifted, and how long that stock needs to respond, then look again at exactly that point.$$

On a world map the east coast of South America fits against the west coast of Africa like a piece of a puzzle. Alfred Wegener was not the first to notice it, but he was the one who turned it into a theory, and the geologists turned the theory down.

[[story]]
Alfred Wegener noticed how the coasts of Africa and South America fit together. He also set side by side the similar fossils and the similar rock belts found on the two shores. One example of a rock belt was the Appalachian Mountains of North America, which seem to continue across the ocean into the Scottish highlands. He gathered it all into one idea: the continents had once been joined and had drifted apart. He named that single ancient landmass Pangaea and set the theory out in a book in 1915.

According to the book, Wegener was a meteorologist, not a geologist. In Bill Bryson's *A Short History of Nearly Everything* that detail matters, since people inside the field were slow to accept such a large claim from an outsider.

The geologists preferred to explain the same fossils on both shores another way. In Bryson's account, scientists of the time leaned toward explanations such as sunken land bridges. The idea that the continents themselves moved was much harder to accept.

The real objection was the mechanism. Wegener could not show a convincing force that would push the continents. Geologists could not see how continents could plough through the solid ocean floor. The observation was strong, but the force to explain it was missing, and the theory was rejected for that reason. On one side were coasts that fit exactly, on the other a physics that could not explain the movement.

Wegener died in 1930 on an expedition to Greenland and did not live to see his idea accepted. Ocean-floor measurements and plate tectonics later confirmed the basic direction of his observation.

##What was right was the observation; what was missing was the mechanism to carry it.## Rejecting an idea and finding its explanation incomplete are not the same thing.

[[lessons]]
$$**Do not throw out a strong observation because its explanation is missing.** A mechanic may not yet know which part makes the odd noise in a customer's car, yet seeing that the noise exists is still valuable. Noting the observation and the missing explanation separately makes it easier to see where the problem is, and you keep the first while you go on looking for the second. That way an idea does not go in the bin because of the part not yet proven, and it finds a ready observation waiting if a measurement arrives later.$$

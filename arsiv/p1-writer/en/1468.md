Benjamin Franklin's Club of Honest Whigs met at the London Coffeehouse, and a writer on innovation thinks the site should carry a plaque. His case for it rests less on Franklin than on who else came through the door, and what happened when they talked.

[[story]]
Steven Johnson makes a claim in *Where Good Ideas Come From* that sounds almost too small to matter: many ideas of the Enlightenment have a coffeehouse in their history. He looks for them in crowded rooms rather than in a lone genius's study. His TED talk starts in London's coffeehouses and ends with Darwin's long, slow hunch, so the room comes first and the time comes after.

Johnson calls such settings liquid networks. They are places where different people and different ideas bump into each other and mix. London's coffeehouses pulled people from different fields into one room, which meant that a question stuck in one field could land in the ear of someone from another.

In a 2010 NPR interview, Johnson used the London Coffeehouse as his example. Franklin's Club of Honest Whigs met there to exchange ideas, he said, and he would like to see a plaque on the spot.

His motto for all of it is *chance favors the connected mind*. Someone working alone can stumble on a good idea, but a person who shares questions with people from other fields has far more chances to stumble on one.

In the talk Johnson adds a detail about drink. Coffee and tea were beginning to replace alcohol as the all-day drink, and he suggests that this shift may have helped conversations in those rooms run clearer.

##What Johnson looks for is less a specialist digging deeper in one field than people who can be where fields overlap.## A single mind has its limits, and a room of mixed minds has different ones. That is the picture he draws of the Enlightenment: a place where a good question had many chances to meet its answer.

[[lessons]]
$$**Sit where other fields talk.** Ideas collide more often when people from different fields share a room than when each person works alone. A software developer who always eats lunch with other engineers might sit with someone from accounting at the end of the month and hear the name of a problem she has never seen in her own work. A recurring lunch or a regular chat makes those encounters likelier without anyone having to plan them, and you do not need to know in advance where the useful answer will come from.$$

At noon on June 21, the Sun shone straight down into a deep well at Syene, and vertical sticks there threw no shadow. Far to the north, in Alexandria, a librarian read about it in a papyrus book and asked what a stick did in his own city at that hour.

[[story]]
Eratosthenes lived in Alexandria in the third century B.C., some 2,200 years ago, and ran its great library. He was an astronomer, historian, geographer, philosopher, poet, theater critic and mathematician, and his book titles ran from *Astronomy* to *Freedom from Pain*. Envious contemporaries called him Beta, the second letter of the Greek alphabet, as if he were second best at everything. Carl Sagan argues in *Cosmos* that in many fields he was Alpha.

Alexandria was the greatest seaport of its time. About four hundred years earlier, a Phoenician fleet hired by the Egyptian pharaoh Necho had sailed from the Red Sea down the east coast of Africa, out into the Atlantic and home again through the Mediterranean. The voyage took three years, probably in frail open boats.

One day Eratosthenes read in a papyrus book that at Syene, a frontier outpost near the first cataract of the Nile, vertical sticks cast no shadow at noon on June 21.

The shadows of the temple columns shrank as noon came on and then vanished entirely, and down in a deep well the Sun showed itself in the water at the bottom. It stood straight overhead.

So Eratosthenes asked what a stick in Alexandria did on the same day at noon. It cast a shadow. Two cities, one Sun, one moment, and a shadow in only one place: a flat Earth could not do that, so the surface had to be curved.

The gap between the two shadows put the cities about seven degrees apart on the globe, which is roughly one fiftieth of a full circle of 360 degrees. All that was missing was the distance.

Eratosthenes hired a man to pace out the ground between Alexandria and Syene. It came to about 800 kilometres, or 500 miles. Multiplied by fifty, those 800 kilometres gave him about 40,000 kilometres, or 25,000 miles, for the whole circumference of the Earth.

Sagan says the result was accurate to within a few percent. That is his claim. The ancient sources give the distance in stadia, nobody knows exactly how long Eratosthenes's unit was, and historians argue about how large the error really was.

[[lessons]]
$$**Compare two simple observations to find what you cannot see.** A baker whose loaves come out pale on some mornings can stop guessing and write down the oven setting and the dough's temperature on a good morning and on a bad one. Laid side by side, the two notes show which of the two changed between them, and everything that stayed the same drops out of the search, so the list of suspects gets shorter at once. That turns a question that seemed to need a full investigation into one the baker can start on tomorrow.$$

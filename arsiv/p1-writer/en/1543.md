The same hotel, the same bathroom, the same towel. The only thing that changed between two notes was whose habits the guest was told about.

[[story]]
Every chapter of *Yes!* opens with a scientific case study, then states its conclusions, then says how to use them, and the titles are questions. Chapter two asks what shifts the bandwagon effect into another gear. The authors are Noah Goldstein, Steve Martin and Robert Cialdini, and the chapter draws on a hotel study by Goldstein, Cialdini and Griskevicius.

In the first test, two messages were compared. The standard one asked guests to help save the environment and show respect for nature by reusing their towels during their stay. The other said that almost 75 percent of guests who are asked to join the hotel's resource-saving program reuse their towels. Data ran for eighty days, only each guest's first eligible day counted, and only guests staying at least two nights were included. The standard message got 35.1 percent reuse. The message about what most guests do got 44.1.

The second test had five messages. One was the standard. Four attached the same 75 percent figure to different groups: hotel guests, guests who had stayed in the same room, fellow citizens, and men and women. The figure came from an earlier study two years before, and it was identical in all four. The results rose in steps. The standard message stayed at 37.2 percent. The norm messages combined reached 42.8. The one naming guests of the same room reached 49.3.

Against the standard message, that is about a third more reuse. The towel was the same and so was the room. What moved was the group the number was said to belong to. The effect is not guaranteed everywhere: in some replications, the general norm helped while the same-room version did not stand out. The pattern is real, but its size varies. The norm also has to be a good one. A nearby chapter of the book warns against telling people that most others behave badly, since that can make the bad behaviour sound normal.

[[lessons]]
$$**When you say what others do, name the group closest to your listener.** Guests of the same room beat guests in general, since people measure themselves against whoever feels nearest. A building manager who wants more recycling could say that most flats on this staircase sort their waste, if it is true, instead of saying most people do. A neighbour hears that as a habit down the hall, and joining it feels easier than joining a general campaign.$$

On a drive through the French countryside, Seth Godin watched cows go by. The first few held his attention, and a little later none of them did. That small lapse of interest turned into a question about marketing, and into the name of a book.

[[story]]
In *Purple Cow*, Seth Godin describes a drive through the French countryside. Cows stood beside the road, and the first few were interesting. After a while they were not. The same animals, the same fields and the same road kept coming, until every cow had become part of the scenery. The cows had not changed, and what had changed was how much attention they still earned.

Then Godin pictured something different: a purple cow. You would notice it. You would turn to the person beside you and say something about it. You would never describe the hundred ordinary cows you had passed, but you could not keep quiet about the purple one.

##What people talk about is what they first notice.##

He calls this quality "remarkable", meaning worth making a remark about, and it is what gets people talking. Godin places the marketing problem right here. A company advertising an ordinary product competes with countless other ads, and since the product is ordinary it disappears into the field.

So he suggests starting with the product itself rather than a bigger advertising budget. If a product is not worth talking about, what does the money spent to polish its image actually solve? The book's answer is to make the product itself worth noticing. The advertising budget comes second, because something worth mentioning tends to be talked about anyway.

More cows in the field only made each one harder to see. Godin goes on to argue that playing it safe is the risky path. A product that looks like everyone else's seems to stand on safe ground, yet that is exactly how it gets lost among the cows. The purple cow looks odd at first glance, but it is the only cow anyone remembers from the trip.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you spend to spread your product, ask whether anyone would bother to mention it.** A neighborhood bakery that sells the same loaf as three others can enlarge its sign, but customers only pass the word along when they find something different enough to tell a friend. Godin's cows show the same rule: the more of them you see, the less you see each one. If you make one thing truly different instead of making the message louder, you improve the odds that people will carry it for you.$$

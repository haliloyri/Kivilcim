[[open]]
Two people sit down in a laboratory and argue about something real. When they finish, blood is drawn from each of them. The researchers are not interested in who won. They want to know what the argument left behind in the blood.

[[story]]
Psychologist Janice Kiecolt-Glaser studied married couples. She brought them into a research setting, asked them to talk through a disagreement, and collected blood samples from them. Gabor Maté describes the work in *When the Body Says No*.

Blood is a practical place to look, because defence cells travel through it and can be counted. The conversations could turn hostile or stay calm, and the researchers compared what the samples showed. After the hostile ones, the immune measures in the couples' blood were lower than after the calmer exchanges. Measuring immunity means counting those cells and checking how well they work. Natural killer cells are among the measures this research looks at, and they are the cells that find and destroy virus-infected or damaged cells. ##One argument had left a measurable mark in the blood.##

It is worth being precise. Nobody claims that a quarrel makes a person ill. What was seen was a short-term difference after one conversation, and the study alone does not show how long it lasts. Maté uses it as an illustration for the field called psychoneuroimmunology: the brain, the nervous system and the immune system share the same chemical messengers, so what happens in one reaches the others.

Prolonged emotional stress, he explains, keeps cortisol high and inflammation switched on. Cortisol is the body's stress hormone, the one that prepares you to deal with a threat. A short spike helps, but weeks and years of it upset the balance of the immune system. For Maté the brain is an organ and emotions are biochemical events, so he finds the line between "mental" and "physical" illness artificial. He does not present any of this as fate. Feelings leave traces, and that does not mean anyone is doomed.

[[lessons]]
$$**Give your body a pause after a fight.** An argument is an emotional event, and in these couples it was a measurable one too, so the tension may not simply vanish when the talking stops. Someone who walks out of a tense meeting and straight into the next call carries all of it along. Ten minutes outside, or even a brisk walk to the end of the corridor, gives the body a chance to come down before the next conversation starts. Afterwards you may notice where the tension stayed, in the shoulders or in the stomach.$$

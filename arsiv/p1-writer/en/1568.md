Before any child had touched an instrument, a researcher asked each one how long they imagined playing it. The question seemed too simple to matter, but the answers turned out to predict who would improve, and nobody could have guessed it from how the children played.

[[story]]
In *The Talent Code*, Daniel Coyle describes a study by the Australian researcher Gary McPherson. McPherson followed 157 children as they picked an instrument and began to learn it, and he videotaped their practice along the way.

Before the children had chosen an instrument, he asked each of them one question: how long did they expect to keep playing? Some pictured a short stretch. Others pictured themselves playing for years.

Coyle presents that answer as the factor that most separated the children who made progress from those who did not. It was asked before a single note had been played, so it could not be measuring skill. It measured how far ahead each child could see themselves in the picture.

##Coyle reads this as ignition: the moment a child connects with a future version of themselves who plays, so that the effort starts to feel worth it.##

As Coyle describes it, ignition is set off by signals that tell a person a place in some group is within reach. A child who can see themselves among the musicians has a reason to practice that a child outside the picture lacks.

Ignition is one of three elements Coyle draws from his tour of talent hotbeds, alongside deep practice and expert coaching. Practice and teaching supply the method, he argues, and ignition supplies the fuel that gets a child to sit down with the instrument at all.

The study is an observation, and it cannot say why some children imagined a long future with the instrument and others did not. Coyle's point is narrower. A child's picture of who they might become can matter before any lesson has taught them anything.

The question cost nothing. It needed no test, no equipment, and no expert to score it, only an adult willing to ask a child to look ahead.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask where someone sees themselves, not what they can do now.** A teacher starting an evening language class can learn more by asking each student what they hope to do with the language a year from now than by testing what they know today. A student who says she wants to read aloud to her grandchildren in the family's old language has just told the teacher what the lessons should lead to. You can then point the practice at that goal from the first week, and check in on that goal when motivation dips.$$

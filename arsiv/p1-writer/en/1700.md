At Princeton, a student flipped a coin. Heads moved a stock's price up, tails moved it down, and the line on the chart began to look like a real company's. Then they showed the chart to a man whose job was to read exactly that kind of line.

[[story]]
Burton Malkiel, an economist at Princeton, published *A Random Walk Down Wall Street* in 1973. The book argues that the short-term moves of stock prices cannot be reliably predicted from past charts. Known information, it says, is reflected in prices quickly. The title's "random walk" is a path whose next step cannot be predicted from the steps before it. The book was written for the ordinary reader rather than for Wall Street's professionals, and it has been reissued many times since.

To illustrate it, he had his students flip a coin. Each flip moved a stock's daily price up or down, and the students plotted the results on a chart. What they held was not the chart of a real stock but of a coin.

Malkiel took the chart to a chartist, someone who tries to predict future prices from patterns in past ones. The chartist took it for a real stock and urged him to buy. When he was told that the line had been drawn from coin flips, he did not take it well.

Another image from the book is famous too: a blindfolded monkey throwing darts at a newspaper's financial pages could pick a portfolio as good as one chosen carefully by experts.

##Whether the chart came from a real stock or a coin, the pattern-reader saw the same thing in both.##

The research Malkiel popularised found that most actively managed funds fail to beat the market average over the long run, especially after costs. His advice to an ordinary investor was simple: buy and hold a broad, low-cost index fund, and pay attention to costs, diversification and time. About three years later, in 1976, Vanguard launched the first index fund open to ordinary investors.

Not every economist agrees with this view. The argument over how unpredictable markets really are is still going.

[[lessons]]
$$**Put your effort into what you can control, not what you can predict.** A family saving for a home cannot know where interest rates are heading. It can decide how much to put aside each month and which fees it pays. That way the energy that might have gone into guessing at an uncertain matter goes into the small decisions that really shape the outcome. The forecast stays interesting, but the plan no longer rests on it, and if events go the other way the family still knows its own decisions were sound.$$

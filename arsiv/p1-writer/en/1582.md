Daniel Pink's explanation for why some careers are losing value comes down to three words that begin with A: Abundance, Asia and Automation. Each one takes a piece of routine work out of the hands that used to hold it.

[[story]]
In *A Whole New Mind* (2005), Daniel Pink argues that the Information Age is giving way to what he calls the Conceptual Age. The Information Age paid well for logical, step-by-step thinkers such as lawyers, accountants and programmers. Pink says three forces are shifting that balance, and he names them Abundance, Asia and Automation.

Abundance means that most people in rich countries can now buy far more than they need, and cheaply. A product that merely works no longer wins on that alone, because design, beauty and meaning start to count.

Asia stands for the fact that routine white-collar work, preparing tax returns for example, can be done abroad at lower cost. Automation means software now takes over routine, rule-based analysis. Someone who processes numbers by rules, whether in an office or inside a program, can be replaced fairly easily. Work that follows rules is work that can be described, and work that can be described can be written into software or sent to another office. Working out why a particular client is anxious is much harder to describe, and so it stays put.

Pink offers this as a warning about skills that rest on rules alone. He wants readers to keep the left-brain style of logical, linear thinking and to add the other style: seeing the big picture, understanding people and putting pieces together. For that he lists six senses: design, story, symphony, empathy, play and meaning. By symphony he means the ability to see the whole and put the parts together.

He also admits that the left brain and right brain split is a simplified picture, since neuroscience does not divide the brain so neatly, and he uses it as a metaphor for two ways of thinking.

[[lessons]]
$$**Separate the rule-following part of your work from the part that depends on people.** An accountant who sees that checking the figures can be done by software can spend the freed hours explaining to an anxious shop owner what those figures mean for her. Pink's three forces all pull routine analysis away from local hands, while understanding a particular person is something that software or a distant office cannot take over. Sorting your own tasks this way helps you decide which parts to hold on to, which to hand to a machine, and where your learning time is best spent.$$

Fifteen years in prison, and then a DNA result that excluded the man. The district attorney still had two things on his side: a detective he trusted and a tape recording of a confession. Someone asked him for his scientific basis.

[[story]]
Carol Tavris and Elliot Aronson tell this in the chapter "Law and Disorder" of *Mistakes Were Made (But Not by Me)*. Bruce Castor, a district attorney in Pennsylvania, had rejected a DNA result that cleared a man named Bruce Godschalk. Godschalk had served fifteen years, and police had recorded his confession on tape. Godschalk later said detectives had fed him the details and he repeated them to end the interrogation. ##"I have no scientific basis. I know because I trust my detective and my tape-recorded confession."## That was Castor's answer.

The case record adds an ending. Castor's office sent the evidence to a second laboratory, which also excluded Godschalk, and in February 2002 Castor asked a judge to vacate the convictions.

A Texas case runs along the same lines. A young woman had been raped and murdered, Roy Criner was in prison for it, and the DNA recovered from her body did not match his. Prosecutor Michael McDougal replied that this meant the sperm found in her was not his. In that reading, the result said something about the sperm and nothing about Criner's guilt.

Florida had its own version. Over fifteen years more than 130 prisoners there had been freed by DNA testing, and prosecutors answered by mounting a vigorous challenge to similar new cases. Wilton Dedge had to sue the state just to get the evidence in his case retested, and the prosecutors objected, citing the state's interest in finality. He was eventually exonerated and released.

Tavris and Aronson do not read these prosecutors as liars. Two ideas collide in a man who thinks of himself as decent and competent: "I am good at this work" and "I helped convict an innocent person." The second weighs far more than "the lab may be wrong," so the lab is what gets doubted. The authors call the tension cognitive dissonance and the work of easing it self-justification. Nobody has to lie for this to happen.

[[lessons]]
$$**Decide in advance what evidence would change your mind.** Once people have committed to a decision, they read new information through it and look first for reasons to keep it. A café owner who has just picked a new supplier can write one line before the first complaints arrive: which result would make her switch back. When a complaint does come, she checks it against a standard she set while she was still open to being wrong, instead of arguing with it in the moment. The same line works for a hire, a diet or a route to work, because it turns "do I admit this?" into "did the thing I named happen?"$$

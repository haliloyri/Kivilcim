Susan David points to a few extra words that change how a thought lands: "I'm a bad mother" becomes "I'm having the thought that I'm a bad mother." She uses it as the way out of what she calls getting hooked.

[[story]]
In *Emotional Agility*, David says feelings and thoughts can snag us, and she calls the result getting hooked. We are hooked when we go on acting on old, outgrown ideas, when we blame a thought for what we did, or when we fixate on being right.

The book is built around four practices: showing up, stepping out, walking your why and moving on. Stepping out is the second. Before it comes showing up, which means facing a feeling instead of bottling it up, brooding over it or forcing yourself to be positive, all of which she counts among the patterns that hook us.

Most of our thoughts, she says, are not facts. They are evaluations and judgments entwined with emotions. "He's purposely ignoring me" and "I'm a fake" are her examples. Both feel like a record of what is true, and both are interpretations.

Her remedy is stepping out. It means experiencing thoughts as just thoughts, which is all they are. She gives a concrete sentence for it. In place of "I'm a bad mother," say "I'm having the thought that I'm a bad mother," and add the emotion by name: "I'm having the emotion of shame."

Putting feelings into words, as one account of her work puts it, gives them less power. In her own words, the wiggle room and breathing space that you create gives you the great gift of choice. The thought is still there, and what to do about it stays open.

In a 2013 *Harvard Business Review* article she wrote with Christina Congleton, David says the prevailing wisdom holds that negative thoughts and feelings have no place at the office, and that this goes against basic biology.

The approach grows out of acceptance and commitment therapy, which Steven Hayes developed, and David's principles are derived from it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Name a harsh thought before you act on it.** When the mind presents an interpretation as a fact, a person tends to decide on the strength of it, and many of those decisions repeat old habits. A designer who thinks "my idea is stupid" just before presenting can say "I'm having the thought that my idea is stupid" and then separate that thought from what she actually knows about the work. The choice to walk into the room is then hers, with the thought as one input.$$

On a factory line the hands repeat one movement for hours, and many of the people who do that work find it deadening. One worker saw the same hours differently. He had no new machine and no new job, only a habit of setting himself small targets inside the work he already had.

[[story]]
Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi describes a factory worker whose job was a single movement, repeated for hours at a stretch. Plenty of people in that kind of work find it boring, meaningless and draining, and they say so plainly.

Picture the line: one movement, hour after hour, with each batch finished against the clock. This worker kept handing himself small targets. Could he finish a batch in less time than the last one? Could he make fewer mistakes than before, and be quicker this morning than he had been yesterday morning? The comparison was with himself: this morning against yesterday morning. The answers did not need a supervisor. Each one was waiting in the pieces he had just finished, so the batch itself told him how he had done.

##Csikszentmihalyi reads this as a man turning the same task into a flow activity.## The difficulty was set by the worker himself. Feedback arrived with every finished piece, and his concentration came without being forced. Three things had been added without changing the job: a challenge he chose, an answer he could see at once, and attention that needed no effort.

The book treats this as a skill that anyone can learn. For any activity you can ask what you could add to stretch yourself, how you could get feedback straight away, and what would bring your whole attention to the task in your hands. Those three questions are enough to start with, whatever the activity, and none of them needs a different job or a different boss.

Flow, in his view, depends on a person's relationship with an activity and not on the activity itself. One job can wear down one person and absorb another.

[[lessons]]
$$**Give a routine task a target and a way to check it.** Someone who answers the same kinds of emails every day can feel the hours blur together. If she decides to need fewer follow-up corrections than the day before and counts them before she logs off, the job has a goal she chose and a result she can see. That lets her notice, within a single day, whether her attention was really on the work, and it leaves her something specific to adjust the next morning. Over a few weeks she also learns which parts of the task she can stretch.$$

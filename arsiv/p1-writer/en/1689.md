A company that tests people's emotional intelligence reports that EQ explains 58% of success in every kind of job. The company belongs to the authors of the book that prints the number, which makes the number worth a second look before anyone repeats it.

[[story]]
Travis Bradberry and Jean Greaves are the founders of TalentSmart, a company that administers EQ tests, and their book *Emotional Intelligence 2.0* leans on its data. They write that emotional intelligence, EQ for short, explains 58% of success in all types of jobs. They add that 90% of top performers are high in EQ.

Those figures come from the authors' own company, not from an independent study, so they are best read as the authors' claim. The claim still points at something worth looking at, which is how the book sorts a person into three parts.

The three parts are IQ, personality and EQ. The book treats them as separate things: a person with a high IQ can have a low EQ, and a person with an average IQ can have a very high one. Knowing one tells you little about the others.

What divides them most is whether they move. The authors say IQ and personality stay largely fixed through adult life, while EQ is flexible and can be raised with practice. That is why the rest of the book is a set of strategies rather than a diagnosis.

The strategies are sorted under four skills. Self-awareness is perceiving your own emotions accurately as they happen and understanding how you tend to react. Self-management is using that awareness to stay flexible and steer your behavior. Social awareness is picking up on other people's emotions and grasping what is really going on. Relationship management is using awareness of your own and others' emotions to handle interactions well.

##Each skill carries its own strategies, 66 in all.## A passcode in each copy opens an online EQ appraisal that scores the four skills and points to the strategies most likely to lift your lowest score, so a reader can start where the gap is instead of reading front to back.

[[lessons]]
$$**Spend effort where change is possible.** If intelligence and personality mostly hold still while EQ responds to practice, the sensible place to invest is the part that moves. A sales rep who keeps cutting customers off cannot raise an IQ score, but can practice waiting until the customer finishes the sentence, and that habit can be trained week by week. The 58% figure deserves the weight of its source, a company's own data, yet the sorting into fixed and flexible traits stays useful either way, because it tells you where practice can pay off.$$

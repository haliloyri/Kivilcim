James Clear's answer to a skipped workout is to put on your running shoes and stop right there. He means it. The advice sounds almost too small to be worth following, and that smallness is exactly why he thinks it works.

[[story]]
In *Atomic Habits*, published in 2018, Clear calls it the Two-Minute Rule: when you start a new habit, it should take less than two minutes to do. What shrinks is not the goal but the starting line.

So "read before bed each night" becomes "read one page." "Go for a run" becomes "tie my running shoes." "Do thirty minutes of yoga" becomes "take out my yoga mat." Studying becomes opening your notes, and folding laundry becomes folding one pair of socks. At first glance these look absurd. One page changes nobody, and tying shoes is not exercise.

Anyone who has come home tired and eyed the sofa knows the problem the rule is built for. The evening arrives, the sofa wins, and the workout that sounded reasonable at breakfast never starts. Clear's fix does not ask for more willpower at that hour. It asks for less.

Clear's reasoning is that the hard part is starting. Once you have started, continuing is far easier. Someone who ties her shoes usually walks out of the door, and someone who reads a page often reads another. A new habit should not feel like a challenge.

These tiny starts work as gateways. Once you have shown up, you are usually already doing the thing, so the small step often grows by itself.

##Two minutes is a gateway, not a trick.##

Clear is clear about the limit of the rule. Staying at two minutes is not the point. The point is to master the habit of showing up, because a habit has to be established before it can be improved. Trying to optimize a habit that has not yet taken hold is like adding floors to a building without a foundation.

So the evening ask is modest. You are not required to run for twenty minutes. You tie the shoes and open the door, and your energy that day decides the rest. Some evenings the two minutes really do end at two minutes, which is fine, since the habit has still happened that day.

[[lessons]]
$$**Shrink the first step until it is easy to say yes.** If you have been putting off paperwork for months, "sort the files" is a heavy sentence, while "put the folder on the desk" hardly weighs anything. Once the small step is done, continuing is much easier than never beginning. Try it on one task you keep postponing, and you may find the delay came from the size of the start rather than the size of the job. That is useful to know before you plan the next one.$$

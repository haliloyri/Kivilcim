[[story]]
Theranos said its Edison device could run a broad range of blood tests from a few drops taken at the fingertip. Tyler Shultz, a Stanford graduate, joined the company in 2013 and was put to work validating Edison's tests. George Shultz, the former Secretary of State and his grandfather, sat on the board. John Carreyrou tells the rest in *Bad Blood*.

The device itself gave him little reason for confidence. By his account the pipette tips fell off, the gears jammed, and coat hangers were used to pull out stuck parts, while temperature-sensitive tests were kept steady with a blanket.

On the syphilis test, Tyler found that Edison picked up only about 65 percent of the samples known to be positive, while a validation report gave the figure as 95 percent. By his account, results from Theranos devices in the lab's proficiency testing differed from those on Siemens machines by more than 300 percent, and only the Siemens results went to New York State. The two numbers sat side by side in the same lab.

In April 2014 he emailed Holmes about poor science and quality control. Balwani answered: a message like that from anyone else would already have brought accountability, he wrote, for its tone. Tyler left the company. He had also written to the state's laboratory investigations unit under a fake name and email address, and nobody ever replied.

His grandfather, who wanted Tyler to sign a one-page confidentiality agreement, arranged a meeting with Holmes. Theranos lawyers arrived with an application for a restraining order and a letter from David Boies alleging that trade secrets had leaked. George Shultz objected and asked the lawyers to leave. At a later meeting Tyler was asked to sign an affidavit denying any contact with the Wall Street Journal and to name the people he suspected of talking. He declined.

Carreyrou's first article ran in the Journal in October 2015. Tyler was one of his sources.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask where a number was measured.** A baker whose supplier says the flour has been tested should ask to see the test sheet, because a claim and a measurement can sit far apart. The question costs little, and it is easiest to ask early, before anyone has staked a reputation on the answer. It also gives the other person something specific to produce, which is easier to answer than a challenge to their honesty. Asked calmly, it gets you either the sheet or an honest "I don't know," and both tell you where you stand.$$

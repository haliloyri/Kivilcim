168 men stood in a square at Cajamarca, in front of an Inca emperor surrounded by tens of thousands of soldiers. The Spaniards carried horses, steel and guns, and the emperor's men had none of those. By nightfall the emperor was a prisoner.

[[story]]
Jared Diamond opens *Guns, Germs, and Steel* with this scene as the answer to a question. On 16 November 1532, Francisco Pizarro met the Inca emperor Atahualpa at Cajamarca in Peru with 168 Spanish men. Around Atahualpa stood an army of tens of thousands.

The Spanish had horses, steel swords, armor and firearms. The Inca had none of these. The Spaniards hid in the buildings around the square, and when Atahualpa was brought in, a priest handed him a Bible and the emperor threw it to the ground. Pizarro's men then attacked, killed many of those around the emperor and captured him that same day.

Atahualpa offered to fill a room with gold for his release. The Spanish took the gold and did not free him. Diamond's real question was why the Spanish did this to the Inca, and why the Inca did not do the same to a king in Spain. Answering it meant looking far past a single square, back thousands of years.

##The answer was not personal superiority, but where each side began.##

For Diamond, geography decided the outcome. Pizarro's men could read about earlier conquests, since writing carried the stories of other expeditions, while Atahualpa had no idea who the Spanish were. Eurasia had accumulated domestic animals, writing, steel and dense societies for thousands of years. Guns, steel and horses were the products of that long accumulation, and so were the germs.

Smallpox, spread by Europeans, had reached the Inca empire before Pizarro did. It killed the emperor Huayna Capac and his heir, and set off a civil war between Atahualpa and his brother Huascar. By the time the Spanish arrived, the empire was already weakened, and the fighting between the two brothers had divided its strength.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you judge an outcome, ask what each side started with.** If one new shop opens on a busy street and another on a deserted lane, crediting the sales gap only to the owners' hard work would be unfair. Diamond's account of Cajamarca ties the result to conditions that existed before either side stepped into the square. When you list the starting conditions first, you can judge your own wins and other people's with more fairness and learn more from them.$$

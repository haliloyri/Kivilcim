[[story]]
For about ten years, in the work behind *Leading Change*, John Kotter watched more than 100 companies try to remake themselves. The first mistake on his list is not creating a great enough sense of urgency.

That first phase can sound easy, he says, and it is not. Well over half the companies he watched failed in it. He names the reasons: executives underestimate how hard it is to drive people out of their comfort zones, grossly overestimate the urgency they have already created, run short of patience, or freeze at the thought that morale will drop, the stock will sink and they will be blamed for creating a crisis.

In the more successful cases, someone starts a frank discussion of unpleasant facts such as new competition, shrinking margins or falling market share. Because people tend to shoot the bearer of bad news, executives often have outsiders bring it: Wall Street analysts, customers, consultants. A former CEO of a large European company described the aim as making the status quo seem more dangerous than launching into the unknown.

Who holds the starting point matters too. Transformations often begin well, Kotter says, when an organisation has a new head who is a good leader and sees the need for change. If the target is the whole company, the CEO is key; if it is a division, the division general manager is. Bad results cut both ways: losing money catches people's attention but leaves less room to manoeuvre, while with good results convincing people is much harder but you have more resources.

##In a few of the most successful cases a group manufactured a crisis.##

One CEO deliberately engineered the largest accounting loss in the company's history, creating huge pressure from Wall Street. One division president commissioned first-ever customer-satisfaction surveys, knowing the results would be terrible, and then made the findings public. Kotter's rule of thumb is that urgency is high enough when about 75 percent of a company's management is honestly convinced that business as usual is totally unacceptable.

These figures come from Kotter's own observations and estimates, not from a controlled study.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you ask people to change, put the facts that make standing still risky in front of them.** People move when they see a problem with their own eyes, which a persuasive proposal alone often fails to achieve. A team lead who wants to change a slow handover process can show the team the list of recent missed deliveries before saying a word about the fix. That way everyone sees why a proposal is needed before they are asked to judge it, and the discussion starts from shared facts.$$

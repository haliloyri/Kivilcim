You get so absorbed in a task that you lose track of time. The authors of one strengths book count that as a clue to talent, but only as the last of their clues. They look at several others before it.

[[story]]
Donald Clifton and Marcus Buckingham keep three things apart. Knowledge is the facts and lessons learned. Skills are the steps of an activity. Talent is any recurring pattern of thought, feeling or behavior that can be productively applied. They define a weakness as anything that gets in the way of excellent performance.

Their test for a strength is hard. In the book's words, an ability is a strength only if you can fathom yourself doing it repeatedly, happily and successfully. The goal is consistent, near-perfect performance.

To find your own, they say, there is one sure way: step back and watch yourself for a while. Your spontaneous reactions give the clearest trace of your talents. Three more clues follow.

Yearnings reveal the presence of a talent, particularly when they are felt early in life. Rapid learning offers another trace. Satisfactions provide the last clue. For satisfactions the book suggests a test: see whether you become absorbed in the activity to such an extent that you lose track of time.

The excellent performers Gallup studied were rarely well rounded. On the contrary, the authors write, they were sharp. Avoiding your strengths to concentrate on your weaknesses, they add, is not a sign of diligent humility.

For managers, Gallup's advice follows from the same clues: watch for each person's natural talents, then position and develop people so those talents are transformed into strengths. Since talents are lasting, the authors say a great deal of time and money should go into selecting people properly. Remedial fixes such as a communication class count as damage control rather than development, and Gallup accepts them only occasionally.

A large base sits behind the clues: a little over two million interviews and about twenty-five years of work. The profile asks you to choose between paired statements, reads instinctive reactions, and reports your five most powerful signature themes out of 34. Example themes include Achiever, Activator, Empathy, Futuristic and Strategic. Clifton, whom Gallup calls the Father of Strengths Psychology, developed the assessment while chairman of the company, and Gallup launched it in 2001.

[[lessons]]
$$**Test whether an ability is a strength by asking if you could repeat it.** The book calls an ability a strength only when you can picture yourself doing it repeatedly, happily and successfully. A baker who is good at the bookkeeping but checks the clock each time, and who notices that kneading dough makes the morning vanish, can note the difference as a clue. That note lets the baker run a small experiment with how much of the week each task gets, without having to make a final decision yet, since the observation is a clue and not a verdict on the person.$$

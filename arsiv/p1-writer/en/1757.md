A physicist was told that every equation in his book would cut the number of readers in half. Could black holes, the beginning of time and an expanding universe be told without equations? He kept just one.

[[story]]
Stephen Hawking was writing *A Brief History of Time* for general readers. The book was to cover black holes, the beginning of time and the expansion of the universe. He was told that every equation he put in would cut the sales in half.

Hawking explains the warning in the book's preface. A reader who meets an equation, he was told, will put the book down. He did not want to lose that reader, so he rewrote his own habits.

Hawking mentions the warning in the book's preface. The sentence was short but its effect was large: each equation could shrink the readership. He took it as a warning against losing readers.

So he decided to leave the equations out. It was not an easy choice, because the language of the ideas he was explaining was mathematics. In place of formulas, he leaned on comparisons, pictures and everyday images.

One equation did stay in the book: E = mc². Even that one he felt he had to include, because the physics would not move forward easily without it. The words carried the rest.

While writing, Hawking kept the reader in mind. In each chapter he asked himself how someone who knew none of it could follow it. The answer was often a picture or a comparison. He used the same method on black holes.

Dropping something that could halve the sales was not only a matter of style. Hawking was writing for anyone curious, not for physicists. That aim shows in every sentence. The book sold in the millions.

He wrote for a general reader who might be curious but not trained. In each chapter, he reached for a comparison that could be held in the hand. Black holes, the start of time and the expansion of the universe all received the same treatment.

##Hawking built the explanation around what the reader could follow, not around what he knew.## A whole universe was explained with a single equation.

[[lessons]]
$$**When you explain something, build it around how much your listener can follow, not around how much you know.** An engineer presenting a complicated result to a board might cut the slides down to the one chart that matters. She could rewrite the text and delete every term she would not use over dinner. The audience can then follow her and decide knowing what they are deciding. Her expertise shows in what she leaves out.$$

After the war Richard Feynman sat in a Cornell classroom feeling that physics had stopped being fun. Then he made himself a promise: he would play with whatever interested him and ignore whether it mattered.

[[story]]
In *Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman!*, written with Ralph Leighton, Feynman arrives at Cornell burned out. During the war he had worked at Los Alamos on the atomic bomb. Now he kept teaching, but physics no longer gave him pleasure, and the more he felt he had to produce, the more stuck he got.

Then he remembered why he had once loved it: he had played with it. He had not asked whether a problem mattered to nuclear physics, only whether it was interesting and amusing. As a boy he had liked fixing radios. He decided to go back to that, working on whatever caught his eye and not worrying about importance.

One day in the cafeteria, as he was eating, a student tossed a plate into the air like a Frisbee. A red Cornell medallion was printed on it, and as it wobbled he saw the medallion spinning faster than the wobble. By his account it went around twice for each wobble, though the physicist Sidney Coleman wrote to Physics Today in 1988 that the ratio is actually the reverse.

Nobody had asked him to look into it. He sat down and worked out the equations of the wobbling plate, treating it as a game. He showed them to Hans Bethe, a colleague in the department, and Bethe, not much impressed, asked what the importance was. Feynman said there was none; he was doing it for the fun of it, with no experiment or paper in mind.

The plate led him to think about how electrons behave, and that thinking grew into the work for which he won the Nobel Prize in 1965, shared with Julian Schwinger and Sin-Itiro Tomonaga, for quantum electrodynamics. Feynman writes that the whole business he got the prize for came from piddling around with the wobbling plate.

[[lessons]]
$$**Give a purposeless question a short stretch of time when work has gone flat.** Feynman's plate calculation had no use, yet it brought him back to the physics he had stopped enjoying. An accountant who feels drained by month-end reports might spend half an hour on a number puzzle with no deadline and find some curiosity left for the spreadsheets afterward. Treating a small question as play, not as one more task, can give you the interest you need to start again.$$

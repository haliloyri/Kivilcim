[[story]]
William Knox D'Arcy made a fortune in Australian gold and moved to London, where investment promoters sought him out. In *The Silk Roads*, Peter Frankopan follows what came next: a search for oil in Persia.

D'Arcy paid for the venture, but the way in to the shah ran through Antoine Kitabgi, a Catholic of Georgian background and a well-connected official in the Persian administration. For a price, Kitabgi could sell access to the oil and open doors to the shah.

The shah's court had learned that if it held out long enough, the British would pay. In 1901 he and D'Arcy agreed on oil exploration in Persia. Russia was not pleased to see Britain taking the lead in exploiting its neighbour. The tsar had even cabled the shah urging him not to sign, and D'Arcy left the northern provinces out of the deal so as to give no umbrage to Russia.

The search was long. D'Arcy and his partners struck oil in May 1908, while an order to stop drilling was still on its way. By the summer of 1914 the British government had bought a 51% stake in the Persian concession, and Winston Churchill, pressed by Admiral Fisher, had grasped what oil could do for the Royal Navy, which was replacing coal with oil.

In June 1914, Calouste Gulbenkian had put together a concession for Mesopotamia's oil in which Anglo-Persian held the majority. Then came the maps. In the Sykes-Picot plan, settled well before the war ended, France was to receive Syria and Lebanon, and Britain Mesopotamia, Palestine and the Suez.

Frankopan writes that the global significance of that 1901 concession is on a par with Columbus's crossing of 1492. It is a large claim for a deal made through a go-between, and its cast is a shah who named a price, an official who sold access and a neighbour who watched.

[[lessons]]
$$**Find out what the other side knows about your need before you negotiate.** A freelance translator who tells a client her own deadline is tight has handed the client the power to name the rate, because the client now knows she cannot walk away. She keeps her position by deciding her lowest acceptable fee beforehand and keeping the urgency to herself, so she can talk about the number calmly. While the client cannot see her hurry, the price stays open to negotiation. Before your next negotiation, you can list what the other side can already see of your situation and what they cannot.$$

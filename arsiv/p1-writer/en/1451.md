Most people spend the last minutes before sleep going over what went wrong today. Joseph Murphy argued that those minutes are better used for something else, and the method fits into a few sentences. It asks for something that is harder in the dark than it sounds.

[[story]]
A boy in Australia, in a story Joseph Murphy tells, pictured his medical diploma hanging on the wall every night as he fell asleep. In Murphy's telling, a doctor he worked for took a liking to him, employed him as an assistant and later sent him to medical school at the doctor's own expense. The story comes from *The Power of Your Subconscious Mind*, where Murphy, a minister, builds practical exercises around exactly this moment.

He treats the drowsy minutes between waking and sleeping as special. The conscious mind loosens its grip, he says, and the subconscious is at its most open. His first method is to think calmly of the result or answer you want and let that thought carry you off. There is no effort in it, and no list to work through. He warns against forcing, because trying to compel the subconscious by will power backfires.

He calls a version of this a mental movie: you watch the scene you want as if it were already happening.

For a problem, he describes something different. You state it once, clearly, trust the subconscious to take it up, and then stop chewing on it. The book's phrase is to turn it over to the subconscious. It runs against what most of us do in bed, which is to go through the same worry again and again, usually with the same result: no answer and less sleep.

The last detail is small and easy to remember, and it is thanks. As you drift off you thank the subconscious for the answer, as though it had already been given.

Murphy says the subconscious keeps working while the body sleeps, so an answer may be there when you wake. That is his claim. The book runs no test of it. It invites the reader to try and judge by their own mornings, and the boy's story is a story, not proof.

[[lessons]]
$$**Name a problem once before you sleep, then leave it alone.** Going over the same worry in bed rarely solves it and mostly costs you the sleep you will need tomorrow. A teacher dreading a difficult parents' evening could write the one thing she does not know how to say on a card, leave it on the desk and go to bed. That is close to what Murphy means by turning it over to the subconscious, and in the morning you meet the same question rested, with one written line in front of you instead of a night's worth of half-thoughts.$$

[[story]]
Percy Fawcett was a British explorer who believed that the remains of an advanced civilisation lay in the Brazilian jungle. He called the city he sought Z. David Grann's *The Lost City of Z* follows the story of that pursuit.

Fawcett's view of the forest had turned from curiosity into obsession. He believed Z was real and that reaching it mattered for an explorer. That belief drew his family along with him, and he took his own son. For someone who knew how dangerous the forest was, it was a heavy decision.

Fawcett had mapped border regions in South America before. Once in the forest, he could stay out for months. His belief in Z had turned from curiosity into obsession, and he thought reaching it was vital for an explorer. It drew his family along with him, and he took his own son.

In 1925 he set out on his last expedition with his son Jack and Jack's friend Raleigh Rimell. His last known letter, to his wife, was written at a place called Dead Horse Camp at the end of May. Nothing more was heard from the three men.

Expeditions were organised to look for them, and some of the searchers died or vanished. The jungle did not give back what it took. For years Fawcett was remembered as an obsessive dreamer.

Decades later Grann followed Fawcett's trail into the Amazon himself. According to the book, archaeologists have since found evidence that large, organised settlements really did exist there. Each searcher had carried a belief of their own into the forest.

His last letter was written in a tone meant to reassure. Even so, the silence that followed lasted years. Each expedition sent to find him brought back a new story, but none brought a firm answer. Every one of them carried a belief of its own into the trees.

##Fawcett was partly right, and that cost him and those who followed him dearly.## His idea held up, but his way of testing it destroyed him.

[[lessons]]
$$**Test a strong conviction in small steps before you stake everything on it.** A café owner could try one new menu for a weekend at her counter before putting all her savings into a second restaurant. A small trial shows cheaply whether the idea holds. That way she can keep believing in it without paying the full price of being wrong. The size of the bet can follow what the trial shows.$$

[[story]]
In Warren Berger's account, investors and industry figures doubted the Airbnb idea. Angel investor Paul Graham said, "No one would want to stay in someone else's bed." Graham later became a seed investor in the company. With no money for ads, the founders launched in Denver during the 2008 Democratic National Convention and pitched their story to news producers, which got them on CNN. The founders, Joe Gebbia and Brian Chesky, were roommates in San Francisco in 2007 with no jobs and little money, and they had challenged the assumption that travelers needed established hotels. Gebbia said that being both travelers and hosts helped them see the opportunity. Berger sets them beside Robin Chase, who questioned the idea that cars could not be shared.

Berger folds the scene into a wider observation: questioning often has an inverse relationship to expertise, which he counts among the interesting and appealing things about questioning. The people who know a field best are often the ones least likely to ask its most basic question.

To get at the other side he quotes a line that goes back to the Zen teacher Shunryu Suzuki: in the beginner's mind there are many possibilities, but in the expert's there are few. Berger links this beginner's mind to seeing without bias, free of an expert's habits. His advice is to cultivate an outsider's or novice's eye for basic why questions about established practices. A naive question can expose assumptions everyone else has overlooked.

He turns to the attorney. Attorneys are trained never to ask a question they do not know the answer to. Berger asks what is wrong with carrying the same rule into business.

The harder task, he says, is to keep asking after experts have told you, in effect, that you can't change this situation and that there are good reasons things are the way they are. Asking fundamental, naive questions about what we observe takes guts, in his words. He credits a child's naive question for the Polaroid camera, and he adds that schools and business cultures tend to reward rote answers over questions.

[[lessons]]
$$**Look at your own field like a newcomer.** Expertise quietly turns questions into settled facts, and "that's how it's done" ends up stopping the thinking. An accountant who has prepared the same report in the same way for years can look properly at a trainee's question about what a column is for, instead of answering "we've always had it." The accountant can also ask it first: if I designed this report from scratch today, which column would I include? That way the expertise stays, and the newcomer's eye gets one turn at it without anyone having to grant permission.$$

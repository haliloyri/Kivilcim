[[story]]
In *Against Empathy*, Paul Bloom describes empathy, feeling what another person feels, as a poor guide to moral decisions. His image is a spotlight: it lights up one person at a time and leaves everyone outside the beam in the dark.

The spotlight usually turns toward someone nearby, someone in view, someone like us. One of Bloom's examples is the girl who fell into a well in Texas in 1987. A single named, visible child attracted far more concern than larger harms with no face and no name. The concern was real, but its distribution was not fair.

Another is the school shooting at Newtown in 2012. The grief for the children was enormous, and Bloom does not deny that it was real, and he observes that equally real suffering elsewhere did not draw anything like that attention.

The trouble comes when our choices follow the feeling alone: help goes where the light falls, and a need outside the beam catches nobody's eye. The light tends to land on what is close, vivid and familiar. What the two examples share is what they leave out, the many who face the same danger but have no name to attach to it.

Bloom draws a line between two things. Feeling what another person feels is empathy, while caring about their wellbeing is compassion. He argues against relying on the first and for the second. The gap between worry for a child in a well and worry for a crowd nobody can picture shows how narrow the beam is.

The book's subtitle is *The Case for Rational Compassion*. Stopping the caring is not what he asks of us. He wants feeling joined by evidence, and by the question of how many people an action will reach.

[[lessons]]
$$**When a story moves you, ask who stands outside the beam.** A parents' association might be touched by one student who needs textbooks and want to spend the whole fund on him. That is a kind impulse, but it is also fair to ask whether the same money could reach more students in the class. Setting the number and the evidence beside the feeling lets you decide without silencing the feeling. You still give, with a clearer idea of where the gift lands. The feeling gets you started, and the numbers help you aim.$$

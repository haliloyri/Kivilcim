Kathryn Petersen took over a Silicon Valley company whose technology was solid and whose executive team was the trouble. Among her first tools was a trip to Napa Valley, and it included questions about childhood and the worst job anyone had ever held.

[[story]]
In *The Five Dysfunctions of a Team*, Patrick Lencioni tells the story of Kathryn Petersen, the new CEO of DecisionTech, a Silicon Valley company with good technology and an executive team that did not work as a team. She takes the group to an offsite in Napa Valley. One of the things she uses there is a plain personal exercise.

Each executive answers a short list of questions: where they were born, how many siblings they have, where they fall in the birth order, an unusual hobby, and the worst job they ever had. Nobody has to confess anything. These are answers anyone could give over dinner.

Lencioni's reasoning is that low-risk answers still change how people see one another. Someone who has heard a colleague's worst-job story sees a person with a history, not only a title on an organization chart. That small step is where what he calls vulnerability-based trust begins.

He defines trust narrowly. It is the confidence that a colleague will not use your weakness against you, so that admitting a mistake, asking for help or saying you don't know costs nothing. Without it, he argues, team members hide their mistakes, hesitate to ask for help, and read each other's intentions in the worst light.

Trust sits at the bottom of his pyramid. Above it come healthy conflict, then commitment, accountability and finally results, and each layer leans on the one below. ##In Lencioni's model, a team whose members cannot admit mistakes to each other cannot argue honestly either, so everything above trust gets weaker.##

The book delivers all this as a fable first and lays out the model in plain terms afterward. DecisionTech's executives had the skill. The Napa questions are Lencioni's way of showing where the work has to begin.

[[lessons]]
$$**Start with a small admission.** Trust tends to grow faster when someone goes first with something low-risk and true. A new shift lead in a restaurant kitchen might open her first meeting by saying which part of the job she is still bad at, and the crew then has room to say the same about theirs. Try it in your next group conversation with an admission that costs you little, and notice whether anyone follows. If they do, honest talk about bigger problems has somewhere to start, and if not, you have learned how safe that group feels.$$

About one in five American soldiers in Vietnam used heroin there, and people at home braced for a wave of addicts coming back. Johann Hari uses that moment, and a set of caged rats, to ask what actually holds a person to a drug.

[[story]]
In *Chasing the Scream*, published in 2015, Hari describes the classic addiction experiment. A rat sits alone in a bare cage with two bottles, one of plain water and one of water laced with a drug. The rat keeps returning to the drug bottle. For years that was read as proof that the drug itself takes hold of whoever tries it, and the case for harsh drug laws leaned on that reading.

Bruce Alexander, a Canadian psychologist, asked a different question. Was the rat drawn to the drug because it was a drug, or because a lone animal in a bare cage has nothing else to do? He doubted that anyone could learn much about addiction from an animal shut away from every normal part of its life.

So he built what he called Rat Park, a large space with other rats, toys and room to wander.

A rat in a cage like that, he reasoned, was a poor stand-in for a person living an ordinary life among others.

##The park rats were offered the same two bottles, and they drank far less of the drug than the rats left alone.##

Hari sets that result beside Vietnam. As he tells it, about one in five American soldiers used heroin there, and many had feared a wave of addicts coming home. Most of them stopped once they got back. The drug had not changed, but the surroundings had: a family, a job, familiar faces. From this Hari draws a conclusion he repeats throughout the book, that addiction is shaped as much by whether a person has bonds as by chemistry. His shorthand is that the opposite of addiction may be connection.

A caution belongs here. Later attempts to repeat Rat Park gave mixed results, and Hari's book has drawn criticism of its own. So read it as a serious challenge to the lone-rat experiment, not as a settled verdict. What it does well is move the question from what the drug does to what the cage is like.

[[lessons]]
$$**Look at the cage as well as the habit.** Suppose a colleague scrolls through his phone alone at his desk every lunch hour. Telling him to put the phone down rarely works, while asking what else his lunch hour holds, and who is in it, tells you far more. The same question applies to your own habits, because it shows you when and where they show up instead of treating them only as a matter of willpower. Sometimes the fix is a fuller hour, not a stricter rule, and you only find that out by looking at the hour itself.$$

Tesla, SpaceX, LinkedIn, Palantir, YouTube, Yelp and Yammer all trace back to one small startup. Its co-founder holds that list up as an odd fact that needs an explanation, and he thinks the explanation lies in how the first team was built.

[[story]]
Peter Thiel, co-founder of PayPal, lists these companies in *Zero to One*: PayPal alumni started or helped build Tesla, SpaceX, LinkedIn, Palantir, YouTube, Yelp and Yammer. Thiel is on the list himself, as a co-founder of Palantir. People have nicknamed the group the PayPal Mafia.

He does not treat that as luck. He points to how the early team was put together. PayPal, he writes, hired people who were talented and who also wanted to work with each other, and many of them became friends.

Another detail he credits is that each employee had a clearly defined job. With each role unique, colleagues were not fighting each other over the same ground.

##Thiel's broader point is that a startup's founding team is its foundation, and one that is flawed at the start cannot be repaired by what is built later.##

Seen that way, the list stops looking like a coincidence of talent and starts looking like a consequence of selection. The people who left PayPal carried habits of working together with them: a reason to trust each other's judgment, and a clear sense of who did what.

The list is striking, but it does not prove that the hiring caused the later companies. Other things were also true of PayPal's alumni and of the years they worked in, and a founder who tells the story is not a neutral narrator. The book offers the pattern as evidence of how much the first team shapes a company, not as a formula.

Still, the idea is easy to test on a small scale. Look at the first five people on any project and ask whether you would choose them again for how they work together, not only for what they know.

[[lessons]]
$$**Pick teammates for how they work together.** A small print shop owner who hires only for speed on the machines may end up with fast people who cannot cover for each other. Asking who each candidate will pull well with, and giving every hire a clear job, makes it likelier that colleagues help instead of compete. You can use the same questions to look at your current team and find the one gap worth fixing first, before the next person joins, and use that gap when hiring.$$

[[story]]
In Adam Higginbotham's *Midnight in Chernobyl*, a safety test is scheduled for reactor four of the Chernobyl plant on April 25, 1986. If the power failed, could the turbine, still spinning down, keep the cooling pumps running until the diesel generators took over? The test was meant for the day shift, but the grid controller in Kyiv asked that the reactor stay online. So the job fell to the night shift.

Deputy chief engineer Anatoly Dyatlov oversaw the test. Shift supervisor Aleksandr Akimov and a young operator, Leonid Toptunov, sat at the controls. The reactor's power sank to about 30 megawatts, far below the level the test called for, and in that state the reactor was unstable.

To bring the power back up, the operators pulled out nearly all the control rods. The emergency core cooling system had also been disabled for the test. Dyatlov insisted that the test go on.

At about 1:23 a.m. someone pressed AZ-5, the emergency shutdown button. The control rods began to descend. Their tips were made of graphite, and as they entered the bottom of the core they first raised the power instead of lowering it. Within seconds the reactor exploded.

A few kilometres away, Pripyat, the city built for the plant's workers, was asleep. The first reports from the control room said the reactor was intact. The plant's own firefighters were on the roof within minutes, and several of them died of radiation sickness in the following weeks.

##The flaw in the rods had already shown itself in earlier incidents, and the operators had not been told.##

Soviet officials at first put the blame on the operators' mistakes. The picture in the book is wider: the operators' errors sit next to a flawed design and a weak safety culture. The first outside sign of the accident came two days later from Sweden, where radiation alarms went off at a nuclear plant. The hands on the button had pressed it without knowing what it would do.

[[lessons]]
$$**Look at the setup behind the mistake as well.** Say a receptionist at a small clinic has booked two patients into the same hour. If her manager only reminds her to be careful, the problem returns next week with someone else. If he checks whether the booking screen hides double entries, the next person is protected from the same slip. That way he changes the setup that made the error easy instead of blaming a person, and his staff keep their trust in him.$$

More than 17,000 people filled in a questionnaire about their childhoods: what happened at home, who did what, what was missing. Then the answers were set beside the same people's health as adults, and the question was whether the two had anything to do with each other.

[[story]]
Kaiser Permanente is a health plan in the United States. More than 17,000 of its members in San Diego answered questions about difficult experiences in childhood, such as abuse, neglect and a troubled home. The questions all looked backwards.

The study was led by Vincent Felitti and Robert Anda, and Bessel van der Kolk gives it a prominent place in *The Body Keeps the Score*. Once the answers were in, the researchers put two things side by side: the hard experiences people reported from childhood and their health as adults. Both kinds of information came from the same people, which is what made the comparison possible.

The finding was clear. The more difficult experiences a person reported, the more likely they were to face health and emotional problems later. ##A questionnaire about childhood turned out to be linked to adult health.##

Van der Kolk presents the study as evidence that childhood can leave lasting marks on body and brain. His reading is that a child growing up in a frightening home adapts by staying on constant alert. Being on alert means noticing the tension in a room before anyone else does. In that house, the adaptation works. In adulthood the same person may read an innocent sentence as a threat, and that can strain relationships and daily life.

In his account, a child's brain and body are shaped by their surroundings, and if the surroundings are not safe, the body secures safety in its own way. He adds that these patterns can be worked on, and he does not treat them as fate. Much of the book is about how. The survey shows an association, not a cause, and it does not say that every hard childhood leads to the same outcome or that the link holds for every person.

[[lessons]]
$$**An oversized reaction may have an old reason.** A habit that looks out of proportion today may once have been a way of staying safe, and it may still be running on old conditions. An employee who goes straight into defence at the mildest feedback, for instance, may come from a place where criticism meant trouble. Seeing this lets you watch when the reaction switches on, and treat yourself with a bit more patience instead of simply getting angry at it.$$

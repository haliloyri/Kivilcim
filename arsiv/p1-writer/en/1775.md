Sugarcane farmers in India are paid mostly once a year, at the harvest. Researchers tested the same farmers a month before it, when money was at its tightest, and a month after it. The two sets of scores did not match.

[[story]]
In *Scarcity*, Sendhil Mullainathan and Eldar Shafir describe studies of the same farmers tested twice. The farmers earn most of their income at a single harvest, so they are poorest just before it and comparatively well off just after. A month before the harvest and a month after it, the researchers gave them tests of fluid intelligence and executive control. Before the harvest the farmers scored about 9 to 10 IQ points lower, a gap that coverage compared to a night without sleep.

At a mall in New Jersey they ran a different test. Shoppers were asked to think about how they would pay for a car repair, and then took spatial and reasoning tests. Lower-income shoppers did poorly when the repair was described as expensive and fine when it was cheap. Higher-income shoppers did well in both versions.

A third study was a lab game in the style of Angry Birds, played with a slingshot. Players assigned to be poor had 30 shots in all, three per level, and those assigned to be rich had 150, fifteen per level. Players could borrow extra shots. The poor who borrowed did worse than the poor who could not, and borrowing made no difference to the rich. The poor also took longer to aim each shot, and the poor who could not borrow scored more points per shot than the rich.

From these results the authors argue that scarcity captures attention and points it at the pressing need, leaving less bandwidth for everything else. That narrowing helps with the immediate target and makes other concerns and long-term consequences easy to miss. Decisions that look foolish may then come from the situation and not the person. Later replication attempts were mixed: the findings on overborrowing and narrowed focus have held up better than the claim that scarcity on one task wears the mind down for the next.

[[lessons]]
$$**Delay a big decision made under pressure.** A shop owner who settles a large supplier order on the day the rent is due is choosing with only the rent in view. Waiting until the next morning, or asking one calm person, lets the other parts of the decision into the picture. The choice is the same, but there is more attention available to make it. She can then read a rushed choice as a result of that day's pressure and not as a verdict on her judgement.$$

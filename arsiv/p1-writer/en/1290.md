David Goggins keeps a jar, and nothing in it can be eaten. It holds an Air Force exam he passed and 106 pounds he lost in three months. At mile 70 of a race, with both feet broken, he reached into it.

[[story]]
Goggins's mother kept a cookie jar stocked even when money was short. As a boy he got joy and gratitude from taking a cookie out of it, and the jar held a variety of them. In *Can't Hurt Me* he borrows the name for his own past: every obstacle he overcame, every small victory and every set of odds he beat counts as a cookie.

He gives examples. Studying until his reading level was good enough. Wanting the Air Force, which meant passing the entry exam, and passing it. Losing 106 pounds in three months to become eligible for SEAL training.

The jar did its hardest work in a 24-hour race. He had entered with no training, and he needed a 100-mile result to qualify for later races. By mile 70 he was off pace with both feet fractured, severely dehydrated and showing signs of kidney failure, with thirty miles to go.

He reached into the jar for BUD/S, the SEAL candidates' basic training, and above all for Hell Week. There he had kept going through bacterial pneumonia and two fractured shins, in a course that ran about 130 hours on less than four hours of sleep a night. The feeling of that came back. "My adrenaline took over," he writes, and his pace picked up. He finished the hundred miles.

##Every cookie in the jar is an event that happened.##

The jar works because it is evidence. A pep talk can be argued with, but a list of dated, real things you got through is harder to wave away. Goggins suggests writing it down in a journal, wins and obstacles overcome alike, so that when pain arrives you can, in his words, dunk your fist in and pull out a cookie.

[[lessons]]
$$**Write down what you got through, and keep it for a hard day.** On a bad day the mind reports only the feeling of that moment and wipes out older proof. A nurse who has noted her toughest shifts in a short notebook can open it before a night she dreads and read her own record. That lets her make the decision on facts she lived through instead of on her mood, and if she still chooses to quit, she does it with evidence in hand.$$

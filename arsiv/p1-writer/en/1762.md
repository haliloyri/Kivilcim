A ship struck rocks because nobody knew how far east or west it was. Famous scientists were looking for the answer in the sky, while a craftsman who worked with wood was heading in a completely different direction.

[[story]]
In the 1700s sailors could find their latitude but not their longitude. Dava Sobel's *Longitude* opens in 1707, when ships of the British navy ran onto rocks off the Scilly Isles. Ships lost their course and many sailors died.

The problem was that a ship could find its latitude by looking at stars or the sun. For longitude one had to know the time correctly. A ship carrying the clock of its home port could compare it with local noon and see how far east or west it had come.

In 1714 the British Parliament promised a large prize for a method of finding longitude at sea, up to twenty thousand pounds. That sum shows how important the problem was to shipping and trade. Famous astronomers searched the sky, the stars and the motions of the moon for the answer.

John Harrison was a self-taught clockmaker from Yorkshire. In his view, a clock that kept accurate time at sea would solve it. He gave decades of his life to building clocks.

The peak of the effort was H4, a large watch. On a voyage to Jamaica in 1761, H4 lost only about five seconds in 81 days. Even so, the Board of Longitude put off paying him.

His first clock had been tried on a voyage to Lisbon in 1736. The Board always seemed to want more proof. Harrison waited for years. He received his final reward in 1773, after the king stepped in.

Before H4, Harrison had built other, larger clocks. His first had gone to sea on a voyage to Lisbon, and it had shown that a clock could keep time on a ship. The prize was never simple to claim, and the Board kept asking for more trials.

##The solution came from a craftsman who kept time, not from the experts who watched the sky.## Everyone looked in one direction, and he worked from the other.

[[lessons]]
$$**When everyone is looking for an answer in the same place, find someone working on the same problem from another side.** A team might keep improving the same approach again and again. Asking someone from outside your field how they would solve it gives you a different view. Where you were stuck, a new route can open. The most useful person may not belong to your field at all.$$

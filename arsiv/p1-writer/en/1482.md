After hundreds of pages on algorithms, work and politics, one author closes his book with a habit that looks almost old-fashioned. It takes two hours of his day, and he insists that it belongs in a book about the future. His reason has to do with who is learning to read you.

[[story]]
Yuval Noah Harari ends *21 Lessons for the 21st Century* with a chapter on meditation. It follows the chapters on technology and politics, and it connects to them. The old advice to know yourself, he says, matters more in the twenty-first century, because there is now serious competition: corporations and governments are learning to hack humans. On the face of it, the chapter looks like a change of subject: a book about algorithms, work and politics ends with a person sitting still.

His own start was less dramatic. A friend urged him to try a course, and in 2000 he sat a ten-day Vipassana retreat with the teacher S. N. Goenka.

What he took from it was a daily habit. Since that first course, he writes, he has meditated for two hours every day. Each year he also takes a long retreat of a month or two. Look at the proportions: ten days in 2000, then two hours a day, then a month or two of retreat every year. The habit grew from one short course into a fixed part of his life.

Harari describes what the practice gives him in plain terms: it helps him observe reality as it is. For him, meditation is a way to watch his own mind at work.

##If others are learning to read you, you had better learn to read yourself.##

That is how the last chapter connects to the rest of the book. A person who has spent hours watching their own reactions arrive is harder to steer than one who has never looked. Harari asks his readers to look at the same thing he looks at every day: what is going on inside them, as it happens.

[[lessons]]
$$**Watch your reaction before you act on it.** If other people can predict what you will feel, the only protection is knowing your own mind better than they do, and that comes from regular practice rather than a single insight. A shop assistant who feels a flash of irritation at a rude customer can learn to name it, such as "I am annoyed", before she answers. Naming the feeling gives her a moment to choose her reply, and over time she notices which situations tend to set her off, which was information she did not have before.$$

The story goes that a survey asked graduates whether they had clear, written goals for their future. Three in a hundred said yes, and ten years later their earnings were said to dwarf everyone else's. Then people went looking for the paperwork.

[[story]]
Brian Tracy tells this story in *Goals!*, and reviews of the book repeat its numbers. Graduates of Harvard Business School were asked whether they had "set clear, written goals for your future and made plans to accomplish them." Three percent had written goals, thirteen percent had goals they had not written down, and eighty-four percent had none. Ten years later, the account says, the thirteen percent earned about twice as much as the eighty-four, and the three percent earned about ten times as much as the other ninety-seven combined.

##Nobody has been able to find the study.## An older version names Yale and the class of 1953, and journalists who traced it found no record that it took place. A Yale library page is reported to say so directly, and a Harvard version has left no trace either. One summary of *Goals!* notes the same gap, and Tracy is among the people named as spreading the story.

A real study exists, though it is smaller and from a different place. Gail Matthews at Dominican University recruited 267 people from businesses and networking groups, and 149 finished. She split them into five groups. The first only thought about their goals. The last wrote them down, made commitments to specific actions, shared them with a friend and sent that friend weekly progress reports.

Among those who sent the weekly updates, more than 70 percent reported successful progress, counting anyone more than halfway there. For people who kept their goals to themselves and never wrote them, the figure was 35 percent. These were self-reports, but the group that did the most is the group that moved the most.

Tracy calls writing goals down transformative and wants them specific and measurable. What holds up is that writing, telling someone and checking in each week went together.

[[lessons]]
$$**Pair a written goal with someone who asks about it.** A goal that lives only in your head is easy to rephrase when the week goes badly, and a goal on paper with a person waiting for news is harder to forget. A student who writes "finish the thesis chapter" on a card and agrees to text a classmate every Friday about it has made the goal visible twice. The weekly message also gives her a natural moment to say what got in the way, so the next week can start from a real description instead of a vague intention.$$

Someone tells you that a certain number always has its own colour for them. Most of us would smile and change the subject, but one neuroscientist built a test instead, using nothing more than a sheet of printed numbers and a few that did not belong.

[[story]]
In *The Tell-Tale Brain*, V.S. Ramachandran writes about people for whom printed numbers come in colour. A 5 is always one hue and a 2 always another, as if each digit had been painted. Most of us would nod politely and move on, and scientists were slow to take such reports seriously. Ramachandran wanted to know whether this was something people imagined or something they actually saw.

So he built a page. He printed rows of 5s and tucked a few 2s in among them so that the 2s formed a shape, a triangle for instance. Someone who looks at it cold sees a block of 5s. To find the shape they have to hunt, line by line.

People with number-colour synesthesia did something different. Their 5s were one colour and their 2s another, so the triangle stood out from the page almost immediately. That was the point of the test. Nobody had to describe an inner experience, and the result could be timed. If the colour had only been a fancy, picking the 2s out of the 5s would not have come any easier to them.

His explanation is about neighbours. The part of the brain that handles the shapes of numbers sits right next to a part that handles colour. In these people the two seem to switch each other on, perhaps because a gene left extra wiring between adjacent areas. Seeing the number calls up its colour. That is Ramachandran's proposal, and later reviews say the neural basis has still not been settled.

One more detail caught his attention. Synesthesia turns up far more often among artists, poets and novelists than in the general population. His reading is that the same cross-wiring could make it easier to link two things that seem to have nothing to do with each other, which is exactly what a metaphor does.

[[lessons]]
$$**Turn an odd claim into something you can test.** When someone says they perceive what you cannot, you are not stuck choosing between believing them and nodding politely, because you can propose a small test, the way Ramachandran hid 2s among 5s. A colleague who insists she can taste which of two coffee machines made a cup could be handed two unmarked cups and asked to say which is which. That way nobody is called a fraud or a genius, and you both have something concrete to look at, even if the test only shows where the claim stops.$$

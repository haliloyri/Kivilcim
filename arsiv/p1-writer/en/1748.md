[[story]]
On 10 May 1996, two commercial expeditions, led by Rob Hall and Scott Fischer, climbed Everest on the same day. In *Into Thin Air*, Jon Krakauer writes that he was in Hall's group, and he was also reporting for a magazine. Both teams were on the same mountain on the same morning, and the clients had paid a large fee to be there.

The guides had set a turnaround time. Anyone not on the summit by two in the afternoon was to turn back. The rule was simple and everyone knew it. Yet bottlenecks formed in the narrow sections near the top, fixed ropes were late, and by two o'clock many climbers were still going up.

Krakauer reached the summit before two and started down. Above him, others were still climbing. Clouds were rising and there was a queue on the summit ridge.

Hall's client Doug Hansen, a postal worker, reached the summit after four. Hall had turned him back close to the top a year earlier. This time Hall stayed with him, and the hour on the summit showed that the rule now lived only on paper.

A storm came that evening. Over two days, 10 and 11 May, eight people died on the mountain. Hall, Fischer and Hansen were among them. Hall spoke by satellite phone with his pregnant wife, Jan Arnold, for the last time.

Krakauer ties the disaster to decisions on the mountain, among them the failure to enforce the turnaround time. ##The rule had been set, but no one had a moment of decision that would make it hold.## Why two o'clock stayed on paper is one of the hardest questions the book asks.

[[lessons]]
$$**Set your stopping rule before the pressure starts, and name who will enforce it.** A building crew can write down at the start that work stops when the budget runs out. Halfway through the project everyone will say it is almost done, which makes the decision in the moment very hard. A rule written in advance backs the choice you made calmly against the one you would make tired. It also gives someone the job of saying stop when you cannot.$$

[[open]]
Hold out a banana to a chimpanzee, and offer it a promise instead: unlimited bananas, but only after death, in chimpanzee heaven. It will not take the deal. Yuval Noah Harari builds a surprising amount of human history on that small refusal.

[[story]]
In *Sapiens*, Harari points out that other animals cooperate in small groups of individuals who know each other. Humans are unusual because they can cooperate flexibly with huge numbers of strangers. His explanation for how comes from that banana. You could never persuade a chimpanzee to give up a banana in return for the promise of endless bananas in chimpanzee heaven.

People make trades like that all the time. They follow a god, a nation, a currency, or a set of rights none of them has ever seen. These work because enough people tell the same story at once, and Harari calls such shared stories imagined orders.

His example from the business world is a company such as Peugeot. A Peugeot factory is real, and so are the cars rolling off it, but the company itself has no body. It exists only in the shared imagination of the people who deal with it.

##The law treats a company as a person.##

It can own property, borrow money and be sued. Its owners are not personally liable for its debts, and a court can dissolve it, though no car, factory or worker is destroyed by that decision. The company is a character in a story, and the story holds because lawyers, banks, judges and customers all keep playing along.

A banknote works the same way. The paper has little use on its own, and it is worth something only because everyone agrees that it is. Nations and human rights depend on agreement of the same kind, which is why they can be built, defended or changed.

None of this makes them fake in Harari's telling. A shared story with enough believers can move armies, raise cities and run a large company. What a chimpanzee cannot do, and a person can, is cooperate with a stranger over something that exists only because both of them agree it does.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask who agreed to a rule and whether they still do.** A household that has a chore chart on the fridge is running on an agreement, and it works only while everyone treats it as binding. When a rule starts causing arguments, you can check what the group originally agreed to, and whether it still does. You may find that the simplest fix is to renegotiate the agreement together, while everyone still cares about it, and that the argument was really about a rule nobody remembered choosing.$$

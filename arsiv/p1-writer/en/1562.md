"More asleep than awake, even when we are awake." One book describes people getting through an ordinary day in those words, and then goes on to what can be done about it.

[[story]]
In *Full Catastrophe Living*, Jon Kabat-Zinn writes that people run on automatic pilot so much of the time. He describes them as more asleep than awake even when they are awake. He calls this condition a blanket of unawareness and says it is not harmless: it may be lethal, either rapidly or slowly. These lines are quotations readers have shared; the text around them was not read for this account.

The idea carries its weight in Part III, on stress. Chapter nineteen is called "Stuck in Stress Reactivity" and chapter twenty "Responding to Stress Instead of Reacting." A sentence quoted from the book says that our habitual and automatic reactions to the stressors we meet, particularly maladaptive ones, determine in large measure how much stress we experience. The same event does not weigh the same on everyone. What differs is how a person answers it.

The book places this against a wider background of stress and illness. Chapter seventeen, as one summary tells it, credits Hans Selye with popularising the word stress in the 1950s and argues that stress contributes to illness alongside pathogens.

According to one summary, the way out the book proposes is to become aware of one's own thoughts. The book also tries this on ordinary activity: Part I trains attention on everyday tasks such as eating. Its examples include a woman called Mary, whose body scan revealed tension linked to childhood trauma, and a young woman whose walking meditation helped with extreme anxiety. In the chapters on pain, it gives a small case of interrupting an automatic reaction, which is to see a headache as just a headache rather than as "my headache."

Not every reader accepts the picture. One critical blogger thinks the book overdraws everyday unawareness and presents ordinary negative feelings as if they were illnesses.

[[lessons]]
$$**Notice the reaction before you act on it.** Part III separates a stuck reaction from a response, and the difference starts with noticing the reaction. A shop assistant facing a sharp customer could notice her jaw tightening before she answers. If she gives that moment one breath, she may say the very same sentence, but she says it by choice and no longer by habit. The part that makes a choice hard is not seeing the reaction at all.$$

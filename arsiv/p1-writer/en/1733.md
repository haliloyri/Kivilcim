A hundred and forty-four publishers said no to the same book. They said people did not read collections of short stories, and some found the title silly. The two authors kept doing something every day that they could count.

[[story]]
Jack Canfield and Mark Victor Hansen had put together a collection of short, inspiring stories: *Chicken Soup for the Soul*. Canfield tells this story in *The Success Principles* as a principle.

They took the book to publishers and were turned down. According to sources, the refusals reached 144 and lasted about eighteen months. One source says more than 130, and 144 is the figure most often given. One account has the pair first pitching 22 publishers in New York, then going to well over a hundred more.

The publishers gave the pair two reasons. People did not read collections of short stories. And they thought the title was silly.

In the end one publisher agreed to read the book. Canfield's own view, as quoted in a magazine profile, is that the longer you persist, the more likely your success.

##One hundred and forty-four refusals lasted until a single "I'll read it".##

*The Success Principles* lays out 64 principles. One of them is Canfield's Rule of 5: do five specific things every day that move you toward your goal. The idea is that small, specific actions repeated daily add up.

Sources say the pair used this rule to promote the book. One account mentions a set number of interviews a day. Not every detail is the same across sources, so the sales and timeline figures are left out here.

The book has another principle, about asking. Canfield ties the habit to his mentor W. Clement Stone and advises asking with certainty, boldness and confidence once you know what you want. The two principles fit together: hearing no is no reason to stop asking.

Canfield presents the method as a principle. No independent source has confirmed in numbers how the book actually sold.

[[lessons]]
$$**When the answers are no, count what you did, not the answers.** Someone looking for work might feel worn down after four interviews that led nowhere, but if they count how many actions they took that day, tomorrow has a clear target. Canfield's Rule of 5 rests on that idea: the result is not in your hands, but today's five actions are. When the tally is tied to actions rather than replies, you end the day with a measure you can keep meeting.$$

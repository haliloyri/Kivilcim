[[story]]
Chapter fourteen of *Yes!* is titled "How can one small step help your influence take a giant leap?" Its case rests on the 1966 experiment by Freedman and Fraser. As one summary of the chapter puts it, getting a yes to a small, reasonable request roughly doubles the yes to a much larger one.

Housewives in California were phoned and asked whether they would answer a few questions about the household products they used. Three days later, a second call came. This time the question was whether five or six men could enter the home and spend two hours going through cupboards and storage places, listing what was there. Of the women who had answered the first questions, 52.8 percent agreed. Women who were asked only the large request were less than half as likely to say yes.

The same paper describes a second study. One group was first asked to put a small "Be a Safe Driver" sign in a window, and almost all agreed. Later they were asked for a large, unsightly "Drive Carefully" sign on the lawn. Of those who had accepted the small sign, 76 percent said yes. Of a group asked only about the large sign, 17 percent did. Accounts differ on the size of the first sign and the gap between the requests.

Freedman and Fraser's own explanation was a shift in how the helper sees herself: a person who has agreed once starts to think of herself as someone who cooperates. Cialdini ties the technique to consistency, since people like to stay in line with commitments they have already made.

The effect is not the same everywhere. Later research has been mixed: some donation experiments found no significant difference, and the original figures have rarely been matched. Yet the pattern of a small yes preparing a larger one has turned up again and again.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask for a small step first.** Once the first phone questions were answered, a two-hour home visit became far more likely to get a yes. A parent organising a school garden day could ask another parent to bring one folding chair before asking them to run the stall. If the first step is taken freely and for a real reason, the next one feels closer, and anyone who does not want it can still step back without pressure.$$

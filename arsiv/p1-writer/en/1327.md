[[open]]
Three columns, thirty lines each: things to do, things to have, and things to be. The exercise starts with a blank page and ends when ninety lines are filled in, with no editing along the way and no answer too odd to write.

[[story]]
In *The Success Principles*, Jack Canfield starts from a plain claim: you cannot pursue what you have not named. One of his practical steps is a list.

As the book's exercise is usually summarized, you write thirty things you want to do, thirty things you want to have, and thirty things you want to be before you die. Ninety lines is more than a quick wish list, so it takes more than the first ideas that come to mind.

The three columns ask different questions. What you want to do is about activity: places to go, skills to learn. What you want to have is about possessions and results. What you want to be is about character and identity, the kind of person you want to be when the list is done.

Canfield treats this clarity as a starting point for everything else in the book. Without it, he argues, the rest of success advice has nothing to aim at.

##A list that long tends to push past the answers you give out of habit and toward the ones you would not have said aloud.##

With all ninety lines on one page, the pattern is visible at a glance: which column filled quickly, and which one went slowly. The exercise has no step for judging the lines as they appear. The page is for collecting what you want, and sorting comes afterward.

A written list does not make any item happen. It supplies the choices to act on, and it is a coaching method, not an experiment: nothing in it shows that people who finish the list reach more of what is on it. What it gives is a first honest inventory.

[[lessons]]
$$**Name more wants than you think you have.** A shop assistant who feels stuck at work could write ninety lines about what she would like to do, own and become, and might find that half of them have little to do with the job she is thinking of leaving. Seeing that on paper changes the question from whether to quit to what to build toward. Read the lines back a day later and mark the ones that still pull at you, because those are the ones worth turning into plans.$$

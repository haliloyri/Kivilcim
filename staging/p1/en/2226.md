Carts and wagons roll through a city at dawn, loaded with the dead. Somewhere in the same city an emperor is working out his own accounts. He is deciding what the word plague should mean when he writes it down for himself, and which kind he can still do something about.

[[story]]
From the middle of the 160s an epidemic ran through the Roman world. Modern historians call it the Antonine Plague, after the family name of Marcus Aurelius and his co-emperor Lucius Verus. It reached Rome while the two emperors were also facing invasions from the north, and it did not fully pass for years.

The Historia Augusta, a late and uneven collection of imperial biographies, gives a few lines on the capital in those years. Its author writes that the dead were carried off in carts and wagons, that thousands died including many nobles, and that the emperors set up statues to the most prominent of them. He adds something more practical. Funerals were performed even for the poor at public expense, and the emperors confirmed strict laws about burials and tombs. That is a single, secondary source, and it is worth taking as a sketch of what the authorities were said to do, not as a record.

The same chapter says fear of the war with the Marcomanni was so strong that the emperor summoned priests from every side and had the city purified with rites before leaving for the front. According to the chronological table in Haines's edition, both emperors took the field against the northern tribes in 167. Marcus spent much of his remaining reign on or near that frontier, and notes attached to parts of the *Meditations* place their writing there.

What Marcus himself wrote is easier to check, because the *Meditations* is his own notebook. He never describes the epidemic in detail. In the ninth book he does write, though, a short passage about plague. Departing life, he says, would be happiest for someone who had spent every day free of falsehood, pretence, pleasure-seeking and pride. If that is not possible, there is some comfort in leaving gladly, weary of such things, rather than wanting to live on in them.

Then he asks himself a question. Have you still not learned to run from the plague? A far greater plague than any change in the air, he writes, is the corruption of the mind. One kind of plague strikes a person as a living creature. The other strikes a person as a reasoning one.

Read with the carts in the background, the passage does not tell us what he felt about those who died. It tells us where he put his own watch. Contagion lay outside his control, while his own judgment stayed within it. He kept a hand on the second, and the laws and the paid funerals show that the first was not ignored.

[[lessons]]
$$**In a crisis, look after your own judgment first and the practical arrangements second.** A nurse on a ward where everyone is exhausted cannot change how many patients arrive, but she can decide to speak calmly, check one dose twice and say thank you to the cleaner. Marcus drew that line between the damage that arrives from outside and the damage a person does to their own mind, and he kept both in view. Naming which part of a bad stretch is yours to tend gives you a place to start when everything else is loud.$$

A full-page newspaper advertisement carries a plain accusation in large type, aimed at the biggest hamburger chain in the country. One man pays for it, and keeps paying, until the company changes the oil in its fryers and then has to find a way to put the taste back.

[[story]]
For decades McDonald's french fries were cooked mostly in beef fat. According to one published history, the blend supplied by Interstate Foods was 93 percent beef fat and 7 percent cottonseed oil, later known as Formula 47. Eric Schlosser's *Fast Food Nation* (2001) is a study of the American fast food industry, and the taste of that fat belongs to the story of how its products are made.

The man who went after it was Phil Sokolof, who paid for full-page newspaper advertisements. One carried the headline "McDonald's, Your Hamburgers Have Too Much Fat!" and another "We Can't Continue to Deep-Fry Our Children's Health." He kept running them, and the chain was the target each time.

In the summer of 1990 McDonald's announced that it would move from beef tallow to 100 percent vegetable oil. The change answered the fat the ads had attacked. It also took away what had given the fries their taste, so the company added a flavouring to bring the beefy flavour back. Published accounts say the flavouring contains no actual beef and is made from wheat and milk derivatives.

Those two moves, taken together, are the interesting part. On the plate it looks like a before and after: the fat goes and the taste returns from somewhere else. A customer biting into a fry could not tell which of the two things had happened, because the flavour sat in the oil and the fat had simply been swapped out.

Later years complicated the picture. A history of the fry reports that religious and vegetarian groups sued over the flavouring, and the cases ended in settlements of about $10 million. Whatever the lawyers argued, the episode shows that a flavour you cannot taste as separate from the food can still be a separate fact that customers want to know.

The company dealt in sequence with two questions. The first was public and loud: too much fat. The second was quieter and belonged to the kitchen: what will the fries taste like without it? Answering the first question set up the second, and a fix that looks complete from outside can still leave a gap that someone will find later.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you remove something from a product or a process, find out which job it was doing besides the one you are targeting.** A bakery that swaps butter for a cheaper fat to cut cost will find that the fat was also giving the pastry its smell, its crumb and its keeping time, and each of those now needs its own fix. List every effect the old ingredient had, including the ones nobody ever put in writing, and decide which of them you will replace and which you will tell people you have lost. Then tell customers what changed, because they will find out anyway.$$

At a leprosy sanatorium in southern India, patients keep walking on feet that carry deep wounds. One of them pushes a bare hand into live coals and does not flinch. A surgeon trained in London stands and watches, and what he makes of it will change how doctors treat the disease.

[[story]]
For a long time the common belief about leprosy was simple and grim: the disease made flesh rot and fall away. Fingers shortened, toes disappeared, faces changed, and the bacillus was held responsible for all of it.

Paul Brand, born in Tamil Nadu in 1914 to missionary parents, was a surgeon who had worked through the London Blitz. In 1946 he was invited to join the Christian Medical College in Vellore, India. On a visit to the leprosy sanatorium at Chingleput he noticed what biographers describe in the same few details: patients walking on badly wounded feet, patients holding their hands in glowing coals without a sign of pain. He began to suspect that the deformities came from the loss of pain, and not from the flesh rotting by itself.

His work bore the suspicion out. Leprosy numbs parts of the body. A person who cannot feel a cut does not stop using the hand, so a small injury is never rested and never cleaned, and it turns into a chronic infection that reaches the bone. Brand's team concluded that most of the destruction came from injuries nobody felt. Painlessness, they found, was the most damaging thing about the disease.

Treatment followed from that. In 1950 Brand set up the New Life Center in Vellore, a village-style rehabilitation centre for leprosy patients, and he pioneered dapsone treatment and tendon-transfer surgery to restore the use of hands. He also designed padded shoes to protect feet that could not protect themselves. In 1960 he received the Lasker Award. In 1966 he moved to Carville, Louisiana, to run rehabilitation at the national center for leprosy patients, and in 1993 he wrote Pain: The Gift Nobody Wants with Philip Yancey, later reissued as The Gift of Pain.

Howard Cutler and the Dalai Lama mention Brand in The Art of Happiness, in the chapter on finding meaning in suffering, for his observation in India that physical pain warns us of harm to the body.

It is a strange kind of evidence. The patients Brand watched had been spared the thing everyone else wants to be spared of, and they were worse off. The pain they lacked would have told them to stop walking, to let the foot heal, to look at the hand.

[[lessons]]
$$**Read a painful signal as information before you try to silence it.** When something hurts repeatedly, whether it is a sore wrist after long hours at the keyboard, a knot in the stomach on Sunday evening or a tension that returns at every family dinner, the urge is to dull it and carry on. Ask first what the signal is protecting, because it may be pointing at an injury that is still growing where you cannot see it. Once you know what it warns about, you can rest the wrist, change the work or have the conversation, and the discomfort can stop for the right reason.$$

Every question on a love-language quiz makes you pick one of two options. Should your partner hug you, or do the dishes? You cannot say you want both, and you cannot say neither, because the quiz lets you tick only one.

[[story]]
"None of the 10 studies supported Chapman's claims." That was the verdict of psychologist Emily Impett, who in 2024 published a review with graduate student Gideon Park and Amy Muise of York University. Impett teaches at the University of Toronto Mississauga. It appeared in *Current Directions in Psychological Science*, and it looked at the research on the three ideas at the heart of Gary Chapman's *The 5 Love Languages*.

Those ideas are simple. Every person has one primary love language. There are exactly five of them. And couples who share a language have better relationships. The sentence about the 10 studies is the authors' own framing, so it helps to look at each premise in turn.

On the primary language, the studies did not back it up. On the number five, the evidence was inconsistent, and other research points to further ways people express love. On matching, Impett said flatly: "There's no support for this matching effect." When participants rate all five languages on a scale, she noted, they tend to rate every one of them as important.

There is good news inside the findings. According to Impett, people are happier when they receive any of these expressions of love. A match is not required, but being shown love still matters.

She also questions the quiz. People find their primary language by taking Chapman's test, which forces them to choose, and she calls those "trade-offs we don't have to make in real life."

Then there is the sample. In Impett's description, Chapman built the framework with "white, religious, mixed-gender, traditional couples." It leaves out things such as supporting a partner's goals outside the relationship, which may count for more to couples with egalitarian values.

The team offers a different picture, closer to a balanced diet. A relationship needs the full range of "essential nutrients," companionship and emotional support among them. The five languages could be one corner of that plate, though not the whole plate.

None of this removes the book's most practical suggestion, which is to ask what lands as love for the person in front of you. That question does not depend on any of the three premises. The review shakes the ready-made verdicts ("your language is this, mine is that, so we clash"), and the question remains.

[[lessons]]
$$**Use a popular framework to open a conversation, and let the other person's real answer overrule the quiz.** A manager can hand a team a survey and file the results, yet learn less than by simply asking what helps each person at work. A test gives you a good question to begin with, but people often want several of the options at once, and you will only hear that from them directly. So you can drop the label, ask your question and learn what that person wants today, which is a far better basis for a decision than a category you assigned in advance.$$

Picture two engineers in the same division of the same famous lab. Their brainpower is about equal, and they were hired through the same filters. Years later, one is a star and the other is average. You'd guess the star is a little sharper. You'd be wrong, and the real difference is something you can start working on this week.

[[story]]
Let's start with the assumption most of us carry around. Intelligence is what gets you admitted, hired and credentialed. So it seems obvious that the smartest people should end up the most successful, and probably the happiest too. In *Emotional Intelligence*, published in 1995, Daniel Goleman takes that assumption apart, and he starts with a number:

> IQ contributes at best about 20 percent to the factors that determine life success.

At best. That leaves most of the outcome to other things, and Goleman argues that a big share of those other things are emotional skills.

To show what that looks like, he points to a long-term study of Harvard men who were followed into middle age. These were people who had already cleared a very high academic bar. The researchers compared those with the highest test scores in college against their lower-scoring classmates. By midlife, the top scorers were not particularly more successful. They weren't clearly ahead on salary, on productivity, or on status in their fields. They also weren't happier, and they weren't more satisfied in their relationships. Among people who were all bright, the extra points on the test didn't buy much of what people hope a good life will bring.

Then comes the case that sticks with people: Bell Labs. Goleman describes engineers in the same division, all with similar intellectual ability. You don't get into a place like that without being very smart. Yet some of them were rated stars and others were rated average. If IQ were the main driver, you'd expect the stars to be the ones with a little more raw horsepower. That isn't what set them apart.

What separated the stars was how they dealt with other people. Long before they needed anything, they had built reliable networks of colleagues. So when a problem hit and they were stuck, they could send a question and get a fast answer, because the relationship already existed. The average engineers ran into the same kinds of problems. But when they reached out, they were often reaching out cold, and they waited.

##The stars weren't smarter. They had done the relationship work before the crisis, not during it.##

Goleman gives that "other stuff" a structure. Drawing on the psychologist Peter Salovey, he organizes emotional intelligence into five domains. The first is knowing your own emotions. The second is managing them. The third is motivating yourself. The fourth is recognizing emotions in others, and the fifth is handling relationships. The order matters. Everything rests on the first one, because you can't manage a feeling you haven't noticed, and you can't read someone else well while you're blind to what's happening in you.

His most hopeful claim is that these are learnable skills, not fixed traits you either have or don't. You can't do much about your IQ. You can practice noticing irritation before it takes over a meeting, and you can reach out to a colleague on a quiet afternoon.

One honest note. The broader claim that emotional intelligence matters *more* than IQ is still debated among psychologists. The narrower point holds up, though. Among people who are all smart enough to be in the room, intelligence alone doesn't decide who thrives.

[[lessons]]
$$**Being smart gets you in, but it doesn't decide how far you go.** The Harvard follow-up was a comparison among people who were all academically strong. Within that group, the top test scorers weren't clearly ahead in midlife on salary, status, productivity or happiness. Goleman's "at best about 20 percent" is a reminder that once you've cleared the bar, piling up more of the same advantage does less than you'd think. The rest of the outcome depends on other things.$$

$$**Build the network before you need it.** The Bell Labs stars and the average engineers had the same brains and ran into the same problems. The difference was timing. The stars had built reliable relationships ahead of time, so help arrived quickly when they asked. A crisis is a bad time to start building trust. It's when you find out whether you already did.$$

$$**Start by noticing what you feel.** Goleman's five domains begin with knowing your own emotions, and the other four depend on it. You can't calm down, motivate yourself, read a colleague or repair a relationship while you're unaware of what's going on inside you. Since these are skills and not traits, the first bit of practice is simply naming the feeling as it happens.$$

[[reflect]]
- Go through the five domains: knowing your emotions, managing them, motivating yourself, reading others, handling relationships. Which one comes easiest to you, and which one do you tend to skip?
- If a real problem hit at work tomorrow, who would you call? Have you put anything into that relationship lately, or would you be reaching out cold?
- Think of someone you consider smarter than you who seems stuck. Is what's holding them back intellectual, or is it about people?

&&Recall a recent moment when your emotions, not your knowledge, decided how things went. What one small change in how you handled that feeling would have changed the outcome?&&

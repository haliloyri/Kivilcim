A telephone rings in a Canadian home. The caller reads out two short sentences about a hardware store that sold snow shovels for $15 and put the price at $20 the morning after a big snowstorm. Then comes the question: was that fair? Economists had an easy answer, and the people on the phone gave a different one.

[[story]]
In 1984 Richard Thaler began a year as a visitor with Daniel Kahneman at the University of British Columbia in Vancouver. *Misbehaving* describes what the two of them did with Jack Knetsch there. They had stories about fairness, the kind people tell when a shop or an employer treats them badly, and they wanted to know what citizens actually count as unfair.

They turned the stories into short hypothetical questions and put them to ordinary people by telephone. A polling bureau sponsored by the Canadian government gave them a luxury that is hard to picture now. Each week they could ask three versions of five questions to a random sample of Canadians, and about 100 people answered each version. Over roughly six months they asked hundreds of questions, with several versions of each, to make sure snow shovels were not a special case.

The shovel question was typical. A hardware store has sold shovels for $15. The morning after a large snowstorm it charges $20. Respondents chose one of four ratings: completely fair, acceptable, somewhat unfair or very unfair. In the sample, 82 percent called the price rise somewhat or very unfair.

An economist reads that scene differently. A higher price after the storm sends shovels to the people who want them most, which is the market doing its job. When Thaler put the question to a recent class of MBA students, 72 percent found the price rise acceptable, and he notes that this was the right answer in their microeconomics course.

What the callers cared about was the store taking away something they felt entitled to. Thaler describes such actions as coded as losses. The surveys turned up many subtleties. A surcharge for paying by credit card felt more unfair than a discount for paying cash. A 5 percent raise during 12 percent inflation was acceptable, while a 7 percent pay cut with no inflation was not.

Thaler stresses that the team was not trying to say what is fair, only what makes people angry. Smart firms have learned from this. After a hurricane, he notes, big retailers such as Walmart and Home Depot sell water and plywood in the hit region at a discount, or give it away, because they are playing a long game for the customers who will return when rebuilding begins.

[[lessons]]
$$**Check whether a change will read as a loss before you announce it.** People judge a new price or a new rule against what they believed was theirs, and the arithmetic matters less than that, because a sound reason on paper does not stop something from feeling like a taking. A manager planning to change a team's shift pattern can ask which parts people will feel as something taken away, and say plainly what stays the same. That small check will not make everyone agree, but it shows you how the news will land, and it helps you foresee the anger and answer it before the first complaint arrives.$$

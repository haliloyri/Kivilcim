A 27-year-old man stopped eating under a doctor's supervision, and kept it up for more than a year. For the last eight months he was no longer even a hospital patient. He lived at home, came in for check-ups, and his doctors wrote down what his blood showed.

[[story]]
In 1973 the Postgraduate Medical Journal published a short report with a confident title: "Features of a successful therapeutic fast of 382 days' duration." The authors, W. K. Stewart and L. W. Fleming, described one patient, a 27-year-old man treated for obesity by fasting. They reported that the fast lasted 382 days and was carried out under supervision.

It was a case report, which means a description of a single person with no comparison group. What makes it worth reading is how closely the doctors measured him. For the last eight months he was ambulant and attended as an outpatient, and his blood was checked along the way. By the authors' account the patient kept to a normal weight after the fast, which is what the word "successful" in the title refers to.

Reports like this are written to put a rare event on record, not to prove that a method works. Several findings stood out. His blood glucose settled at around 30 mg per 100 ml for the last eight months, a level that is usually low enough to alarm doctors. Yet his glucose and tolbutamide tolerance tests were normal. His glucagon response was reduced and later absent, and it returned to normal when carbohydrate feeding was resumed.

Other measurements told a more mixed story. Potassium stayed normal after an initial drop was corrected without supplementation. Calcium was temporarily high near the end. Magnesium was low from the first month and stayed low. After about 100 days, the urinary excretion of cations and phosphate rose sharply, which the authors suggested might reflect the breakdown of excess soft tissue and some skeletal mass.

The authors' own summary was that prolonged fasting in this patient had no ill-effects. That sentence is the claim, and it is worth noticing exactly what it covers: this one patient, under close medical supervision, with regular monitoring and a vitamin regimen the paper records.

A single case can show that something is possible. It cannot show how often the same thing happens, to whom, or what goes wrong for the people it is not published about. Cases like this one tend to travel as striking stories while the careful boundaries stay behind in the original paper.

[[lessons]]
$$**One extraordinary case shows what can happen once, and it says little about what usually happens.** The report is real, careful and detailed, yet it describes a single supervised patient with nothing to compare him against. When you meet a striking example in a meeting, a headline or a family conversation, ask how many other cases there were, who was watching, and what the example leaves out. A vivid case is a good place to start a question, and a poor place to end one, so keep asking until you know what the typical outcome looks like, and who else is in the picture$$

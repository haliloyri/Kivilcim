[[open]]
The cigarette has no tobacco tip, only a small piece of carbon. A match is struck to light it. Nothing burns down and no ash falls, yet it still makes something a smoker is meant to breathe in. It is a cigarette that smokers have never met before.

[[story]]
Premier was a cigarette from the tobacco side of the company Bryan Burrough and John Helyar chronicle in *Barbarians at the Gate*. R.J. Reynolds put it into limited US test markets in 1988. It was designed to heat tobacco rather than burn it: a carbon tip lit, the heat passed to the tobacco and small flavor beads, and the smoker drew in a nearly smokeless vapor.

The idea looked sound. The trouble lay in how the product described itself. The advertising spoke of "clean smoke," and the press called it a "smokeless" cigarette. Many consumers took that literally and believed Premier produced no smoke at all, when in fact it produced less, not none. Expectations had been set higher than the product could reach.

The way it worked in the hand did not help. Published assessments say it was hard to light and hard to keep lit. It did not burn down like an ordinary cigarette, so it was difficult to tell when one was finished. Lit with a match, the sulfur in the flame reacted with the carbon and gave off an unpleasant smell. Every small habit of a smoker's hand and nose had been changed a little.

Taste made it worse. Smokers who tried it compared the flavor to a burnt Brussels sprout, to burning plastic or an old tennis shoe, and to barn sweepings. Those were not marketing phrases. They came from the people who had put it in their mouths.

Reynolds terminated Premier on 28 February 1989. Hundreds of millions of dollars had gone into developing and launching it, though sources disagree on the exact figure. The company said the main reason was that smokers did not like it. In the same months, RJR Nabisco was in the middle of an expensive fight over its own ownership.

The logic is simple to state. The name and the advertising made a promise, and the first person to pick the product up tested that promise through small acts: lighting, drawing, tasting. As the distance between the promise and the thing in the hand grew, whatever was good about the product sank under the first trial.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you launch something, run it from start to finish with someone who will use it out of habit.** A new reporting format, a booking tool or a meeting routine can work perfectly at your own desk, while the person using it has different hands, different eyes and different expectations. The name you give it becomes a promise, and if the first use does not keep that promise, the real strengths get lost behind the first impression. One short trial with a small group helps you test both the name and the use before anyone outside the team forms an opinion.$$

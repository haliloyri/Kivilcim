[[open]]
Zurich, on a December afternoon. Twenty-two people in one room each hold a single vote on where the world's biggest sporting event will be played. Two more were meant to be sitting there and are not, because they were suspended weeks earlier after a newspaper sting.

[[story]]
The vote was on 2 December 2010. Two of the executive committee's 24 members never cast a ballot that day. They had been suspended after the Sunday Times sent reporters posing as lobbyists for American companies. According to the New York Times and other reports, the reporters recorded Reynald Temarii of Tahiti and Amos Adamu of Nigeria offering to sell their votes. Twenty-two were left to choose the hosts of the 2018 and 2022 World Cups.

FIFA's own release says Sepp Blatter announced the result in Zurich: Russia for 2018, Qatar for 2022. In the 2018 contest England's bid received 2 votes in the first round. For 2022 the final round went to Qatar by 14 votes to 8 over the United States.

Bruce Bueno de Mesquita and Alastair Smith use FIFA in *The Dictator's Handbook* as an example of a body where a few people decide and each of them can be courted separately. That frame makes the numbers easier to read. A bid team does not need to win over the world's fans, or even the national federations. It needs a majority of a room with two dozen seats, and any one of those seats is worth about four per cent of the vote.

The sting shows how that looks from the inside. Two members, talking to people they took for lobbyists, discussed what their votes might fetch. The suspensions came before the ballot, so the process went on with the remaining voters, and how each of the 22 made up their mind is no part of this account. Later inquiries and bans followed, though this account does not rest on them. What the room itself shows is a count: a bid needed a majority of those 22 hands, and every hand belonged to one named person who could be visited, flattered or promised something.

In small electorates, the question to ask is not which bid is best, but which voters can be reached and what each of them wants. Fans, players and the host cities themselves had no vote.

[[lessons]]
$$**Count the voters before you judge the choice.** A parents' committee that chooses a school trip by a show of hands among nine people can be swayed by one early promise, while a vote of the whole school would barely notice it. The smaller the group that decides, the more each member's private interest weighs, and the less the public discussion matters. When you must win a decision, find out how many people actually decide, and spend your effort on what each of them needs rather than on a crowd that has no say in the result.$$

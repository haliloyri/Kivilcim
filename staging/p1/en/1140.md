When a friend tells you they're struggling, you probably think they're brave. When you're the one struggling, admitting it feels like weakness. Brené Brown noticed this double standard in her research, and she thinks it keeps many of us from the very thing we want most.

[[story]]
Brené Brown is a researcher. For years, she collected stories from people about connection, shame and belonging, looking for patterns in what makes some people feel deeply connected and others feel alone. In *Daring Greatly*, she lays out what she found, and it's not what most of us would guess.

She describes a group of people she calls "wholehearted." They have a strong sense that they are worthy of love and belonging. They feel connected to the people around them. You might expect them to be the ones who have learned to protect themselves best: confident, polished, careful about what they show.

It's the opposite.

##The people who felt most connected were the ones most willing to be vulnerable.##

Brown defines vulnerability simply: uncertainty, risk and emotional exposure. It's saying "I love you" first. It's asking for help. It's sharing work that might be criticized, admitting you don't know, or telling someone the truth about how you feel. None of these come with a guarantee.

The wholehearted didn't enjoy that exposure. But they accepted it as the price of connection. You can't be truly known without being seen, and you can't be seen while you're hiding behind armor.

Most of us do the reverse. We protect ourselves by keeping a distance. We put on a mask of competence, cheerfulness or indifference. And the armor works, in a sense. It keeps some hurt out. But it also keeps out the love, belonging and joy we were hoping to find.

Brown insists that vulnerability is not weakness. She calls it our most accurate measure of courage, and the birthplace of love, belonging, joy, courage, empathy and creativity.

She also names the paradox that makes this so hard:

> Vulnerability is the first thing I look for in you and the last thing I'm willing to show you.

When someone else opens up, we see courage. When we think about opening up ourselves, all we can see is our own inadequacy. The same act looks completely different depending on which side of it we're standing on.

[[lessons]]
$$**Connection needs you to be seen.** Brown's wholehearted people weren't the best protected. They were the most willing to be exposed. Real closeness depends on someone knowing the actual you, and that is only possible if you let them see it, uncertainty and all.$$

$$**We grade vulnerability on two scales.** Brown's paradox, "the first thing I look for in you and the last thing I'm willing to show you," explains a lot. In others, openness looks like courage. In ourselves, it feels like failure. Noticing that double standard is the first step to being fairer to yourself.$$

$$**Armor has a hidden cost.** Keeping your guard up does protect you from some pain. But Brown calls vulnerability the birthplace of love, belonging and joy. If you armor against the risk, you also armor against the reward, and you may not notice what you've lost.$$

[[reflect]]
- Recall a time someone admitted a struggle to you. Did you think less of them, or more?
- What armor do you reach for when you feel exposed: humor, busyness, perfection, silence?
- Where are you waiting for a guarantee before you show up fully?

Most to-do lists grow. A task gets added on Monday, three more on Tuesday, and by Thursday the list is a record of everything you have not done. Robin Sharma suggests going the other way. Each morning, write down five things. Not five big projects. Five small targets. It sounds almost too modest to bother with, and that is exactly where the idea gets interesting.

[[story]]
The practice comes from Sharma's *The 5AM Club*, published in 2018, a book built around protecting the early morning for planning and focus before the day's distractions begin. Among its tools is one he calls the **Daily 5**.

The instruction is simple. Each morning, list five small goals you want to complete that day. That is the whole method. Five items, chosen by you, before the day gets going.

What matters is the timing as much as the list. By mid-morning, most days are already being shaped by something else. Emails arrive with their own urgency. Someone needs an answer. A meeting runs long and another appears. If you have not decided what the day is for, the incoming demands decide it for you, one request at a time.

The Daily 5 moves that decision to the front. You choose the day's small victories first, while the morning is still yours, and then the rest of the day has to fit around them rather than the other way round.

##The point is to decide in the morning what the day's small wins will be, before incoming demands decide instead.##

The word "small" is deliberate. A list of five large, vague goals would be as easy to abandon as any long list. Five small, concrete targets are things you can actually finish today, and check off tonight.

Sharma then adds some arithmetic. Five small wins a day, he points out, adds up to 1,825 over a year. It is worth being clear about what that number is. It is not a measured result from people who tried the method. It is simple multiplication, five times 365, used to illustrate how small things accumulate when they are repeated day after day.

As an illustration, it does its job. A single day's five wins looks trivial. Seen across a year, the same habit becomes a large pile of finished things. The number does not promise any particular outcome. It shows why a modest daily list is not as modest as it looks, provided it is kept up over a long time.

The contrast at the heart of the idea is between two kinds of list. One is long, keeps growing and is mostly written by other people's needs. The other is short, fixed at dawn, and written by you. Sharma's bet is on the second.

[[lessons]]
$$**Choose first, react second.** The Daily 5 is written in the morning, before the day's demands arrive. That timing is the point. A short list made while the day is still quiet puts your chosen priorities ahead of interruptions, rather than squeezing them into whatever gaps the interruptions leave behind. Keeping each item small matters too: five things you can genuinely finish today give you a list you can close in the evening, instead of one that simply rolls over to tomorrow.$$

$$**Small adds up only over time.** Five small wins a day become 1,825 in a year. That is arithmetic, not a measured result, and it only holds if the habit is kept. The useful takeaway is patience: a single day's list will look unimpressive. Its value shows up across months, not in one morning, so judge the practice by whether you keep writing the list, not by how much a single day achieved.$$

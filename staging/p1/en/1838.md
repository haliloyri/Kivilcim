One autumn a NATO exercise looks, from Moscow, like the rehearsal for a real attack. Documents from a British spy source show the West how serious that fear is. The question is: if the other side's fear can be seen, can a mistake be prevented?

[[story]]
Oleg Gordievsky, the subject of Ben Macintyre's *The Spy and the Traitor*, worked in the Soviet intelligence station in Britain during the tensest years of the Cold War. This story concerns a NATO exercise called Able Archer 83, which began on 2 November 1983. The exercise simulated a chain of command leading up to the use of nuclear weapons, and it was run in a way that resembled preparation for a real war.

At the time the KGB was running a large operation to find out whether the West was preparing a first nuclear strike against the Soviet Union. It was called RYAN. KGB officers collected signs of an attack, such as a rise in blood donations or office lights burning late into the night, and reported them to Moscow. In that climate an exercise could be read not as an exercise but as a sign.

Documents from the Soviet station in London reached British intelligence, MI6. They showed that Moscow's fear was not dry propaganda but real anxiety. Prime Minister Margaret Thatcher passed these warnings on to President Ronald Reagan.

The exercise went on, and around 11 November Soviet forces stood down from their alert. How far this episode changed Reagan's thinking is disputed, and we do not know for certain. But one thing is clear: for the first time the West had been able to see from the inside why the Soviets were afraid.

This is a story about perspective, not trust. The side running the exercise had innocent intentions, but the other side read it differently. Most conflicts grow when one side says "I am doing this for defence" and the other reads "this is preparation for an attack". Each reading is logical on its own terms.

What makes this case unusual is that someone carried the second reading across the line. Usually the other side's fear stays invisible until it has already produced a reaction. Here a few documents let one side see its own action as the other side saw it, and that view was worth more than any amount of reassurance.

[[lessons]]
$$**Look not at what an action means to you but at how the other side might read it.** An announcement of a reorganisation, a price change or a short note to a colleague can be innocent for you and still be read through the other side's fears. Before you act, ask yourself: how will the person across from me read this, given their present worries? Asking that before a miscommunication happens can protect both of you from an expensive misunderstanding, and it costs a minute of imagination rather than a year of repair.$$

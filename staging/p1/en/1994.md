Over a meal in a European city, a young woman raises a glass of champagne. She has just been offered part-time consulting work with an oil company, with a signing bonus, and she is delighted. The job is invented, and the man across the table is not what he says he is.

[[story]]
Charles Duhigg's *Supercommunicators* (2024) begins its first chapter with Jim Lawler, a CIA case officer who is afraid he is bad at recruiting spies. In 1982, at thirty, he had a law degree from the University of Texas, mediocre grades and a string of dull jobs, so he phoned a CIA headhunter he had met on campus. The process took a polygraph, about a dozen interviews in different cities and exams. His scores were poor to middling, and he had no overseas experience, no foreign languages and no military service.

The final interviewer noticed other things: Lawler had paid his own way to Washington, kept going after failing tests and stayed optimistic. Asked why he wanted the job, Lawler first gave a grand speech about serving his country and spreading democracy, then heard how silly it sounded. He said his life felt empty and that he wanted to be part of something meaningful. A week later he had an offer.

At the CIA's training ground in Virginia, known as the Farm, he learned lock picking, dead drops and surveillance. He also learned that the work was mostly conversation. His training summary said to find ways to connect. Posted to Europe, he spent months at formal parties and in bars near embassies, with little to show for it. A clerk from a Chinese delegation turned down his offer to pay for gossip, saying his bosses executed people for that.

Then a colleague mentioned Yasmin, a young woman from the Middle East who worked in her country's foreign ministry and was on vacation, staying with her brother. Lawler met her at a restaurant, posing as an oil speculator. She seemed lonely. At lunch she told him she hated the religious radicals who had taken power at home, that she wanted to move to Paris or New York, and that she had saved for months for this trip. Lawler then offered her part-time consulting work for an oil company, with a signing bonus. They drank champagne, and she was delighted. Over time she added that she was ashamed of her government's crackdown on newspapers, free speech and women's education.

His boss was pleased, and then gave an order: tell her you are CIA and ask for information about her government. Lawler thought it would end the relationship. His boss answered that recruiting her without honesty would be unfair, and that she could be jailed or killed if discovered.

Over dinner Lawler told her he was a US intelligence officer, apologized for the deception and offered her a job with the CIA. She was horrified. She shook her head, cried and said that people were murdered for such things. All she wanted was to get away from him.

By then his boss had already told the division chief, the chief of station and Washington that Yasmin was recruited. Lawler had no way to change her mind, and he admitted he did not know how to build the trust the situation demanded.

[[lessons]]
$$**Check what kind of conversation the other person is in before you make your request.** Imagine a colleague describing how proud she is of a project, and you answer with a question about her timesheet. The topic may be fair, but you have changed the kind of talk without telling her, and she will feel it as a drop in warmth. Before you ask for something, listen long enough to hear what the other person is talking about, practical, emotional or about who they are, and give that your answer first. Then the request arrives inside a conversation she is still having with you.$$

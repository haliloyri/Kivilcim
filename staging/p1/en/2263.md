Five university teams receive the same list of questions about the world: oil prices, border clashes, who will still be in power next spring. Each must turn its crowd of forecasters into one set of probabilities, and a score is kept. Only one method pulls ahead.

[[story]]
Philip Tetlock and Dan Gardner's *Superforecasting* grows out of a forecasting tournament run by IARPA, the research agency of the US intelligence community. Its ACE program set out, in IARPA's words, to "dramatically enhance the accuracy, precision, and timeliness of intelligence forecasts." Five research teams competed, each running its own mini-tournament and free to develop its own methods for recruiting, questioning and combining forecasters, and one of them was the Good Judgment Project, in which Tetlock and psychologist Barbara Mellers recruited forecasters and tried out methods on them.

More than 200 questions went out over the first two years, chosen by the intelligence community. They asked about Brent crude oil prices, clashes between China and Japan in the East China Sea, and leadership turnover in Russia and Zimbabwe. Forecasters gave probabilities, and the official metric was the Brier score: the squared gap between a forecast and the outcome, where an event counts as 0 if it did not happen and 1 if it did. Say 90 percent for something that never happens and the penalty is steep.

Good Judgment won. According to a summary by Tetlock and colleagues, its best algorithms for pooling the crowd landed on the right side of 50/50 in 86.2 percent of daily forecasts. That beat the simple average of a control group, forecasters who worked alone without training, by 60 percent, and the other teams by 40 percent.

The project also ran experiments on its own forecasters, assigning them at random to conditions such as working alone or in teams so it could test what actually drove accuracy. Training in probabilistic reasoning helped, and so did working in teams where people shared information and argued over their reasons. At the end of the first year the best 2 percent were placed together in elite teams, and these "superforecasters" were far more accurate than the other groups in year two. Averaged together, five such teams of twelve beat the Brier-score goals IARPA had set for its fourth year, and they showed no drift back toward the average from one year to the next.

Every team faced the same questions and the same scorekeeper. That is what made the claim "our method works" something other people could test.

[[lessons]]
$$**Keep score on your own judgments before you try to improve them.** A restaurant owner who guesses the weekend crowd every Thursday and writes the guess down can compare it on Monday with the real count. After a few months the owner sees the pattern in the misses, perhaps that rainy Fridays are always overestimated, and can fix it, which no guess kept only in the head would ever show. Without the written guess, memory quietly edits each miss into something that was nearly right. With it, you can tell whether a new habit or a new source actually makes you more accurate.$$

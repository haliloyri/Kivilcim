Toward evening, neighbours line up outside a house holding their phones. Inside, a few bulbs are lit and a radio is playing. It all comes from a tower behind the house, built from scrap, turning in the wind. There is no company and no power line, only a family and a tower.

[[story]]
The first windmill lit a single small bulb. As an experiment that was success enough, but William Kamkwamba's story did not end there. According to published accounts, the real growth of the project came after that first light.

The next machine was bigger. In the family's house it ran four bulbs and two radios. The wind does not blow every day, so a car battery carried energy over the calm ones. In the same account, William also made his own light switches and a circuit breaker for the house wiring, using nails, magnet wire and melted rubber.

Then the neighbours came. The village had no mains electricity, but it did have mobile phones. People who wanted to charge theirs began queuing at the Kamkwambas' door. A boy's experiment had turned, almost overnight, into a village's need. A third windmill, according to the same accounts, pumped water to irrigate the fields, and taller towers and better blades followed.

Look at the order: one bulb, then four, then phone charging, then water. Each step stood on the one before. The story is not told as if it began with a grand plan. A small thing worked, people made use of it, and the use pointed to the next job.

Product builders have an old name for this: shipping the smallest working version. Instead of waiting for a flawless design, you put something that functions in front of real people, and what they do with it tells you what to do next. A plan made at a desk can be wrong, but neighbours queuing at your door are not.

Another side of the sequence is that the risk stays small. If the first bulb had not lit, the loss would have been a few parts and some effort. A big plan collapsing at one point costs far more than a small experiment collapsing. A person who moves in small steps keeps solid ground to step back onto, and every working step gives the courage to try the next, and courage is a kind of capital.

Real use is a more honest teacher than a forecast. You can calculate how many bulbs a windmill will light. What else the village will need it for, only the village can tell you.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ship the smallest thing that works and let real use decide the next step.** For a product, a process or a service, the best plan is very often not the one written at a desk but the one that comes out of how people actually behave. A windmill that lit one bulb became a radio for the household, a charging point for the neighbours and a pump for the fields. If you keep the first version small you carry what you learn into the next one much faster, with solid ground behind you at every step of the way.$$

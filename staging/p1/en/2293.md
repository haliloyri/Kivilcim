In a crowded meeting room a radio is playing, and out of the speaker comes the sound of water being poured. Then paper tears, then a jazz record begins. On an ordinary radio those sounds would dissolve into crackle. Here each one comes through separately and plainly, because the broadcast is arriving by another route.

[[story]]
In Tim Wu's *The Master Switch* radio is one of the places where an invention collides with the interests of an industry, and at the centre of that collision stand Edwin Armstrong and FM broadcasting. Armstrong patented frequency modulation in the early 1930s. FM greatly reduced the static that plagued the AM broadcasts of the day. According to one account, it cut static by a factor of more than a hundred.

Armstrong carried out his first large field tests, as published accounts have it, between 1934 and 1935 at RCA's Empire State Building facility. RCA was headed by David Sarnoff. It is reported that in 1935 Sarnoff asked Armstrong to remove the equipment from the top of the building, and that RCA turned toward television, not FM, in that period. Many accounts say RCA's stake in AM broadcasting played a part in that stance.

Armstrong did not retreat. On 5 November 1935, at a meeting of the Institute of Radio Engineers, he demonstrated FM reception on a receiver 17 miles from the transmitter. According to published accounts, the people present heard water being poured and paper being torn. Over AM those sounds would have become unrecognisable. One reporter wrote that there were "no extraneous sounds."

His station W2XMN, built in 1937 at Alpine, New Jersey, broadcast with less power than an AM station and could still be heard clearly at about a hundred miles. The technical superiority was in plain view, and everyone in the room could check it with their own ears. Armstrong's proof was an experiment, not an argument.

Yet a non-technical obstacle stood in FM's way: other people set the frame. In 1945 the Federal Communications Commission moved the FM band from 42 to 50 megahertz up to 88 to 108 megahertz and reduced the power transmitters were allowed. The stated reasons were atmospheric interference and the growth of television. According to published accounts, RCA lobbied hard for the change.

The result was that the FM receivers already produced and the existing transmitters, tuned to the old band, became unusable, and Armstrong's broadcasting network did not survive the transition. In 1948 Armstrong sued RCA, alleging that it infringed five of his basic FM patents. The case teaches that being right is not enough on its own: the product carried a difference that could be proved by ear, but the band, the receivers and the rules were set by others.

[[lessons]]
$$**Show the difference with a test your audience can check with its own ears or eyes.** Armstrong's 1935 demonstration did not stay a claim, because the sound of water and paper filled the room. When you explain an idea, figures and promises get questioned, but a person who has noticed the difference with their own senses does not argue about it as if they had heard it second-hand. Prepare a small example the other side can try for themselves in the meeting where you want to show a new method's value. A demonstration is only the first step, though, and it does not put every decision-maker in the same room, because the outcome also depends on the rules and equipment where it will be used.$$

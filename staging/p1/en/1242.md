After Abraham Lincoln died, his papers were sorted. Among them was a letter to one of his generals, written in obvious distress, sharply pointing out a disastrous failure. It had never been sent. Dale Carnegie thought that unsent letter held one of the most important lessons about people.

[[story]]
In the opening chapter of *How to Win Friends and Influence People*, Dale Carnegie sets out his first principle: **don't criticize, condemn or complain.** To show why, he tells the story of Lincoln and General George Meade.

In July 1863, the Battle of Gettysburg ended in a Union victory. The Confederate army under General Robert E. Lee retreated south. Heavy rains had flooded the Potomac River, and Lee's army was trapped, with the river in front and the Union army behind. It looked like a chance to end the war.

Lincoln ordered Meade to attack immediately, without calling a council of war.

Meade hesitated. He called a council of war anyway. He delayed and made excuses. And while he waited, the water went down, and Lee escaped across the river with his army.

Lincoln was furious. He sat down and wrote Meade a letter. In it, he told Meade he did not believe the general appreciated the magnitude of the misfortune involved in Lee's escape. The war, he wrote, would now go on indefinitely. The opportunity was gone, and he was distressed beyond measure.

##But Lincoln never sent it. The letter was found among his papers after his death.##

Why did he hold it back? Carnegie speculates. Perhaps Lincoln imagined how Meade would react: defensive, bitter, justifying himself, perhaps condemning Lincoln in return. The letter might have relieved Lincoln's feelings. It would not have changed what had happened, and it might have cost him a general he still needed.

Carnegie also notes that as a young man, Lincoln had been fond of criticizing and ridiculing people, sometimes in letters and newspaper pieces. Once, a man he mocked in print challenged him to a duel, which was stopped only at the last moment. Lincoln, Carnegie says, learned from that painful experience. He came to believe that sharp criticism almost never improves anyone.

Carnegie's argument is that criticism is futile because it puts people on the defensive and makes them justify themselves. It wounds their pride and stirs resentment. Instead of criticizing, he urges us to try to understand why people do what they do. Lincoln's favorite quotation, Carnegie says, was: *Judge not, that ye be not judged.*

Writing the letter may have helped Lincoln. Not sending it was the wiser act.

[[lessons]]
$$**Criticism triggers defense, not change.** Carnegie argues that when we criticize, people justify themselves instead of improving. Lincoln likely imagined Meade's defensive, bitter reply. A sharp letter might have felt deserved, but it would have made correction harder, not easier. Understanding usually moves people further than condemnation.$$

$$**Write it, then hold it.** Lincoln let his anger out on paper, then kept the letter in a drawer. That's a useful practice. Writing the angry message can release the feeling; not sending it protects the relationship. A day later, you can decide calmly what, if anything, actually needs saying.$$

$$**Try to understand before judging.** "Judge not, that ye be not judged" was Lincoln's favorite line. Before condemning someone, Carnegie suggests asking why they acted as they did. Most people, including those who make serious mistakes, believe they had reasons. Understanding those reasons is where real change begins.$$

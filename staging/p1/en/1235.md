On her first day of high school journalism class, a young student carefully wrote a news story. So did everyone else. Then the teacher read them, put them down, and showed the class the one sentence all of them had missed. The student was Nora Ephron, and she never forgot it.

[[story]]
Chip and Dan Heath tell this story in *Made to Stick*, in their chapter on why unexpected ideas grab our attention.

Nora Ephron, who went on to become a celebrated writer and filmmaker, remembered her first journalism class clearly. The teacher began by explaining what a "lead" is: the opening sentence of a news story, the part that tells readers what matters. Then he gave the students a set of facts and asked them to write one.

The facts went something like this. The principal of the high school had announced that the entire faculty would travel to a nearby city next Thursday for a conference on new teaching methods. Among the speakers would be several well-known figures.

The students got to work. They produced leads that dutifully summarized the facts: *Next Thursday, the faculty of the high school will travel to a conference, where they will hear from...* Who, what, where, when. All correct.

The teacher read through them. Then he told them the lead he was looking for.

> There will be no school next Thursday.

##In that moment, Ephron said, she realized journalism wasn't about repeating the facts. It was about figuring out the point.##

The Heaths use this story in two ways. First, it shows the importance of finding the core of a message: out of all the facts, what actually matters to the people hearing it? To students, it isn't where the teachers are going. It's that they'll have a day off.

Second, it shows the power of surprise. The teacher's lead worked on Ephron and her classmates precisely because it was unexpected. It broke the pattern they'd been following. Surprise, the Heaths argue, jolts us to attention, because it signals that our mental model of the situation is wrong.

To keep that attention, they turn to the work of the economist George Loewenstein, who developed a "gap theory" of curiosity. Curiosity, he suggested, arises when we feel a gap between what we know and what we want to know. That gap feels like an itch we need to scratch. It's why we keep watching a mystery to find out who did it.

Good communicators, the Heaths argue, use this deliberately. Instead of handing over all the answers at once, they first open a gap: a question, a puzzle, a surprising fact. Then they fill it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Find the point, not just the facts.** Every student got the facts right and still missed what mattered. "There will be no school next Thursday" works because it answers the reader's real question. Before communicating anything, it's worth asking which single consequence your audience will actually care about.$$

$$**Surprise earns attention.** The teacher's lead jolted the class because it broke their expectations. The Heaths show that the unexpected makes people stop and pay attention. A message that follows the predictable pattern is easy to ignore; one that breaks it is hard to forget.$$

$$**Open a gap, then fill it.** Loewenstein's gap theory explains why mysteries keep us hooked. Posing a question before giving the answer creates an itch people want to scratch. Opening a knowledge gap deliberately is one of the simplest ways to keep an audience listening until the end.$$

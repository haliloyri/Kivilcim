[[open]]
A small weather task sits on a spacecraft, holding one key. Higher up the chain, a much more important task is waiting for that key, and a crowd of middling jobs keeps pushing the small one aside. Nobody has done anything wrong. Yet the whole computer is about to give up and restart itself.

[[story]]
Glenn Reeves led the flight software team for Mars Pathfinder, and in December 1997 he wrote down what had gone wrong on the surface of Mars. The lander's single computer ran many tasks, and the bus that linked its parts worked on a rhythm of eight cycles a second. Ideas like this are the kind Brian Christian and Tom Griffiths gather in *Algorithms to Live By*, a book on computer science and human decisions.

Three tasks mattered. One, called bc_sched, set up the bus transactions for the next cycle and had almost the top priority in the system. A second, bc_dist, collected the results and ranked third. Far below sat a meteorological task that gathered weather data and passed it along through a shared pipe. Before each new cycle, bc_sched checked whether bc_dist had finished. If it had not, the spacecraft declared an error and reset the computer.

Here is how that happened. The weather task took a lock on a shared resource inside the pipe machinery, and then something more important pushed it off the processor. Several medium-priority tasks ran in the gap. When bc_dist needed to hand over its data, it needed the same lock, so it waited for the weather task, which could not run because the medium tasks kept going. bc_sched found bc_dist unfinished and reset the computer. Data in memory survived, but the day's scheduled activities were not completed until the next day.

The team could not make it happen before landing. Their tests had covered only the best case of data rates, and on Mars the real activity ran higher than expected. After they reproduced it on the ground, in less than 18 hours, the picture became clear. The flight code had kept its tracing tools, which let engineers see which task was running and when.

The cause is called priority inversion. A low-priority task, holding something a high-priority task needs, is stuck behind the medium ones, so in effect the unimportant work outranks the important work. The fix was priority inheritance: a task holding a lock temporarily takes the priority of the highest task waiting for it, so nothing in the middle can push it aside.

In the spacecraft's software the remedy was a single setting. The team changed the global configuration so that the semaphores were created with priority inheritance, tested it extensively because it applied to every such lock, and sent the difference between the onboard and Earth copies of the software to Mars as a patch.

[[lessons]]
$$**When a small task blocks an important one, lend it the important task's rank until it has finished.** In an office, picture a clerk who holds the only login the director urgently needs, while a stream of routine requests keeps interrupting the clerk. Everyone sees a slow clerk, but the real cause is that the urgent job is stuck behind the routine ones. Whoever sees the pattern can protect the holder from interruptions and let the blockage clear first, instead of pushing the director's job harder. The next time something important crawls, ask first what small thing it is waiting on, because the slow part is rarely the part with the loudest deadline.$$

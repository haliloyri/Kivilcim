A team of biologists walks a stream in the Panamanian highlands, catching frogs and wiping each animal's skin with a swab. For months every swab comes back clean. One morning in late September, one of them does not, and within two weeks the first dead frogs lie along the banks.

[[story]]
Elizabeth Kolbert begins *The Sixth Extinction* in Panama, with frogs, and one of the best-documented episodes of that story happened at a site called El Copé. Karen Lips and her colleagues had been sampling amphibians there for the skin fungus *Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis*. Not one of the 1,566 animals they had swabbed before September 2004 was infected. Then, on 23 September, they found the first, and the run of clean swabs was over.

Eleven days later came the first dead frog that tested positive, and after that the dead kept coming. Between 4 October 2004 and 15 February 2005 the team collected 346 dead frogs and five dead salamanders, anywhere from one to nineteen on a given day. Three were too decayed to test, but of the 318 bodies examined closely, 315 were heavily infected.

El Copé was home to 67 known species of amphibian. Thirty-eight of them turned up among the dead, which is 57 per cent, and nine more tested positive in frogs that were still alive. That put the infected total at 47 species. That is seven in ten species, on one stretch of forest, within a few months.

The fungus itself was a recent discovery. It had been described only in 1999, from a captive blue poison dart frog that died at the National Zoo in Washington. In 2019 a global assessment led by Ben Scheele traced the decline of at least 501 amphibian species over half a century to the same disease, ninety of them presumed extinct, and called it the greatest recorded loss of biodiversity attributable to a disease. The authors blame trade and development for breaking down the barriers that once kept pathogens apart. Other researchers have challenged how many of those 501 can really be pinned on the fungus, and the authors have defended their count. Their critics agree amphibians are in trouble. The argument is about how many species, and how much of the blame, belong to this one fungus.

The Panamanian golden frog, the species that anchors Kolbert's opening chapter, lived in the hills around El Valle, further east. According to the timeline kept by the Panama Amphibian Rescue and Conservation Project, US zoos began rescuing amphibians in 2005, a year after the die-off at El Copé. The fungus reached El Valle in 2006, as predicted, and the first rescued animals moved into a purpose-built center there in 2007. Today the golden frog is listed as critically endangered and possibly extinct in the wild. The animals that survive do so in captivity, kept by people who began collecting amphibians before the fungus reached the valley.

[[lessons]]
$$**Treat the first outbreak next door as your deadline.** A trouble that has already struck one place can be read as a schedule for the next one, as long as someone is watching closely enough to see it. A café owner who hears that the supplier across town has stopped delivering has a few weeks to line up a second supplier, while a café owner who waits for the first empty shelf has none. Pick out the one neighbour, rival or earlier project whose problem looks like yours, and ask what date the same thing would reach you.$$

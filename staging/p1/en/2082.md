Two of the most famous names on Wall Street spend a Sunday night asking for the same thing: to be inspected, supervised and regulated like an ordinary bank. At half past nine in the evening, the answer arrives as a press release, and the change takes effect only after a waiting period measured in days.

[[story]]
Andrew Ross Sorkin's *Too Big to Fail* includes the Sunday when Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley gave up the identity that had defined them. This was 21 September 2008, six days after Lehman Brothers filed for bankruptcy. Bear Stearns had already been sold, and Lehman had failed. Goldman and Morgan Stanley were two of the best-known independent investment banks still standing.

At 9:30 p.m. Eastern time the Federal Reserve Board announced that it had approved their applications to become bank holding companies. The approvals would take effect after a statutory five-day antitrust waiting period. Banks answer to the Fed and to other bank regulators. Investment banks had answered mainly to the Securities and Exchange Commission.

The same release explained what the firms gained. Under it, the New York Fed could lend to their US broker-dealer subsidiaries against any collateral eligible at its regular lending window or at the Primary Dealer Credit Facility, which had been created in March. That support also covered their London broker-dealers, and Merrill Lynch's. The Fed said the extra support would help the firms manage their funding as they moved into the new structure.

Both firms presented the change as a position of strength. According to Goldman's own account, Lloyd Blankfein said that under Fed supervision the firm would be seen as an even more secure institution, and Goldman already owned two banks holding more than $20 billion in deposits. John Mack of Morgan Stanley said the new structure would put his firm in the strongest possible position. By its own figures, Morgan Stanley had more than 3 million retail accounts and $36 billion in bank deposits at the end of August.

Some commentators called it the end of Wall Street's investment-banking model. Others pointed to the other half of the bargain: closer supervision by bank examiners from several agencies, rather than by the SEC alone. A firm that had borrowed from its lenders overnight could now borrow from the Fed against collateral. It would also let inspectors look at its books.

In one weekend the firms swapped a degree of freedom for a lender that would not run away. They did it after watching two of their rivals fail and before knowing whether their own funding would hold. That order of events is the point: the price was accepted at the moment it felt cheapest.

[[lessons]]
$$**Work out what a safety net costs before you need it.** Support from a bigger party nearly always comes with conditions about how you operate, and the conditions look lightest when the alternative is a crisis. A freelancer who relies on one large client for steady work might accept that client's rules on pricing, schedule and exclusivity, and only later see how much choice has gone. Deciding in a calm week which freedoms you would trade, and which you would not, lets you negotiate the terms while you still have options. That choice is much easier to make with a clear head than with a deadline pressing on you.$$

Tens of thousands of women were asked to eat less fat and more vegetables, fruit and grains, and to keep it up for years while researchers followed their hearts. Their cholesterol readings moved a little. The number of heart attacks and strokes did not fall by an amount anyone could tell apart from chance.

[[story]]
A low-fat diet was long treated as the sensible default for protecting the heart. Many people believed it, and many doctors said it. What was missing was a large randomized test of it in ordinary people over many years, and the Women's Health Initiative Dietary Modification Trial set out to supply one. Its results appeared in JAMA in 2006.

The trial enrolled 48,835 postmenopausal women aged 50 to 79. By chance, some were placed in an intervention group and the rest in a comparison group. The intervention was not a leaflet. It was intensive behavior modification, with the aim of bringing total fat down to 20% of calories while raising vegetables, fruits and grains. The comparison group received only diet-related education materials and carried on as before. Chance decided who went where, so any difference later on could be tied to the program, not to the kinds of women who chose to join it.

Women in both groups were followed for an average of 8.1 years. The researchers watched for coronary heart disease, stroke and cardiovascular disease overall.

The intervention changed what the women ate and it changed some measurements. Fat intake fell, LDL cholesterol dropped modestly, and diastolic blood pressure came down a little. In another trial those facts alone might have settled the question in favor of the diet.

But the main outcomes did not follow. The intervention did not significantly reduce coronary heart disease, stroke or cardiovascular disease as a whole. The authors wrote that more focused diet and lifestyle interventions might be needed to reduce cardiovascular risk.

It helps to read this carefully, in both directions. The trial did not show that a low-fat diet harms the heart, and it did not prove that fat is harmless. It tested one specific pattern, delivered in one way, to one group of women, and found no clear protection against the outcomes it measured. A trial can only test the diet that people actually manage to follow, and that is part of why such results are argued over.

Still, the shape of the finding is instructive. A belief that felt obvious, backed by plausible reasoning and respectable advice, was put to a fair test with tens of thousands of people. When the heart events were counted, the belief was left unconfirmed.

[[lessons]]
$$**A belief that sounds obvious deserves a fair test, and an unconfirmed result is information too.** The low-fat advice had logic and authority behind it, yet a large randomized trial found no significant drop in heart disease or stroke. In your own work, notice which of your convictions have never been put to a comparison, and design a small one, with a comparison group and a clear outcome, before you build more on it. Even an inconclusive answer tells you how much weight the belief can carry, and that is worth knowing before the weight grows, so the next decision rests on firmer ground$$

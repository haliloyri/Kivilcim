A student sits across from a researcher who reads out digits at a steady pace. He listens, then says them back. Like nearly everyone, he gives out somewhere around seven. Many hours of sessions later, he repeats back a string more than ten times longer than the one that used to beat him.

[[story]]
The student was Steve Faloon, an undergraduate at Carnegie Mellon University, and the experiment was run by the psychologists K. Anders Ericsson and Bill Chase. Ericsson would later write *Peak* with Robert Pool. The researchers describe Faloon as having the average memory and average intelligence of a college student, and he was paid by the hour to come in and repeat random digits back in the order he heard them.

His starting span was seven, the ordinary limit. For a long stretch of sessions that was all there was to say about him.

Faloon was a runner, and he did something nobody had told him to do. He began to read groups of three or four digits as race times. In the researchers' own example, 3492 turned into 3 minutes 49.2 seconds, a near-world-record mile. A string that meant nothing was suddenly attached to something he already knew well.

Recoding alone could not explain what followed. Someone holding seven groups of four digits tops out at 28 digits, a ceiling Chase and Ericsson pointed out themselves, and Faloon went far beyond it. According to their analysis, he also built a scheme for the groups: he clustered them into larger units and fixed where each one sat in the sequence, so that he could pull them out one after another in order.

The paper in *Science* in 1980 reports that after more than 230 hours of practice in the laboratory, his span had grown from 7 to 79 digits. On other tests with digits he matched memory experts who had trained for a lifetime. Later reports add that his span for letters stayed ordinary, so what he had built belonged to digits alone.

Two jobs were being done at once. Meaning came first: 3492 was no longer a pile of digits but a mile. Placement came second, because the order of the clusters was fixed in advance, so each incoming digit already had a place to go. That detail also shows where the extra capacity came from. His short-term memory had not stretched. He hung new digits on knowledge he already carried, the race times, and on an order he could walk through again. He found the method on his own, and it took hundreds of hours of use before it held at that length.

[[lessons]]
$$**Hang new material on something you already know before you try to hold more of it.** When a list of names, figures or steps keeps slipping away, the usual reaction is to repeat it harder. A salesperson who has to remember a dozen clients before a trade fair can tie each name to a spot on her own route to work and then walk that route in order. The list holds better because every item has an anchor and a position, and you can test it by saying the whole list aloud from first to last without any notes, and the first place you stumble is where the anchor is weak.$$

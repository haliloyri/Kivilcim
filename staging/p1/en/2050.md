A drone circles day and night over a house in Baghdad. The house looks ordinary from above, a flat roof among other flat roofs, but one man comes to it every seven to ten days. The people watching the screen want to know where he goes when he leaves.

[[story]]
Stanley McChrystal and his co-authors, in the TIME article that sets out the ideas of *Team of Teams*, list the finding and killing of Abu Musab al-Zarqawi, the head of al-Qaeda in Iraq, among the results of the network they built. By that article's account, monthly operations rose from 15 to 300 over four years. What follows is the last stretch of the hunt, taken from published military accounts.

On May 18, 2006, an interrogation report supplied a key address: the Baghdad home of Abd al-Rahman, Zarqawi's spiritual adviser. The report said he visited Zarqawi every seven to ten days. That meant Zarqawi could be reached, in a sense, without looking at him at all, by watching the man who came to see him. Drones kept watch over the Baghdad house around the clock. The value of the report lay less in the address than in the pattern: the same person, at regular intervals, going to the same place, and a pattern can be followed.

On June 7, 2006, Rahman's car was tracked north out of Baghdad. Near Baqubah he moved to a white pickup with a red stripe. The journey ended at a safe house near Hibhib where Zarqawi was staying.

A ground assault was judged not feasible, so the decision was an airstrike, and F-16s bombed the house. Lt. Gen. Gary North, the head of US Central Command Air Forces, told reporters at the Pentagon that officials were "100 percent assured" the house was Zarqawi's location. Zarqawi and Rahman were killed.

According to the published accounts, the special operations team reached the site as Iraqi police were loading a body into an ambulance. The team took control of the ambulance, and the badly wounded Zarqawi died within minutes. Iraqi Prime Minister Nouri al-Maliki announced his death the next day, June 8. General George Casey, the coalition commander, said the organization remained dangerous, and bloodshed rose that summer before gradually falling.

What stands out is how the path was laid. Nobody looked at Zarqawi directly. They looked at the person who met him regularly. An interrogation report, a tracked vehicle and eyes in the air, each from a different source, came together in one picture. The authors' broader argument is that such joining-up needs an organization where information does not stay in a narrow circle.

[[lessons]]
$$**When you cannot reach someone, look at the people who meet them regularly.** If a customer's decision-maker never answers, you can ask who talks to that person every week and who prepares their meetings. Information from a single source is usually incomplete, because each desk only knows the piece it can see. When small pieces from different desks are put together, you learn what the person you cannot see is doing and where they are heading. So do not keep your piece to yourself. Put it on the same board as everyone else's and write down what each person knows.$$

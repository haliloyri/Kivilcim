Two groups of lab animals, one lever each. The first group gets food every single time it presses. The second gets food only sometimes, on a schedule it can't predict. Which group presses harder? Most people bet on the reliably fed animals. That bet is wrong, and the reason explains a lot about the phone in your pocket.

[[story]]
In *Hooked*, Nir Eyal opens his idea of variable rewards with B.F. Skinner's experiments on operant conditioning. The setup is simple. Animals learn that pressing a lever can bring food. Some are rewarded on every press. Others are rewarded on an unpredictable schedule.

The animals on the unpredictable schedule pressed the lever more often.

That result is the seed of Eyal's argument. A reward you can count on is useful, but it doesn't pull at you. Once you know exactly what's coming, there's nothing to find out. A reward that may or may not arrive is different. The not-knowing is itself the engine.

##The uncertainty of what comes next is what keeps people checking, even when most checks turn up nothing.##

Eyal builds this into a four-step cycle he calls the Hook Model. First comes a trigger, something that prompts you. Then an action, the simple thing you do in response. Third is the variable reward, the part that may or may not pay off. Last comes investment, where you put something of yourself back into the product.

Variable reward sits at the center of that cycle, and Eyal doesn't treat it as one thing. He sorts it into three types, each tugging at a different need.

Rewards of the tribe are social. Approval, likes, being noticed by other people. You post something and don't know who will respond, or how.

Rewards of the hunt are about searching for resources or information. Scrolling a feed is the everyday example: somewhere below the next post might be the interesting one, so you keep going.

Rewards of the self are about mastery, competence and completion. Clearing an inbox. Finishing a level in a game. The pull here is the feeling of getting something done.

Seen this way, the apps that fill your day stop looking like random time sinks. Each one is offering some mix of belonging, searching and mastery, with the outcome kept just uncertain enough that you come back to see.

Eyal writes the book as a design tool for people who build products. But he doesn't stop at the mechanism. He also discusses the ethics of using it, and he puts two plain questions to designers. Would you use this product yourself? And does it improve your users' lives?

Those questions matter because the same lever works whether the product is good for people or not. The mechanism is neutral. What it gets used for is a choice someone makes.

[[lessons]]
$$**The uncertainty does the pulling.** Skinner's animals on the unpredictable schedule pressed more often than the ones fed every time. What differed was whether the next press would pay. When you catch yourself checking again after finding nothing, the missing reward isn't a failure of the system. It's part of how the system holds you.$$

$$**Different hooks for different needs.** Tribe, hunt and self are not the same pull. One product feeds your wish to be seen, another your urge to find the next thing, another your satisfaction in finishing. Naming which one is at work tells you what you're actually after when you open an app, and whether that app is the best place to get it.$$

$$**Knowing the lever lets you choose.** Eyal asks designers whether they'd use their own product and whether it improves lives. You can ask a version of the same thing as a user. Once you see the mechanism, a checking habit becomes something you can judge, instead of something that simply happens to you.$$

[[reflect]]
- Which app do you open just to see if something is there?
- When you check and find nothing, what brings you back an hour later?
- Is the pull you feel about people noticing you, new information, or the feeling of finishing something?
&&Next time you reach for your most-checked app, can you name the reward you're hoping for, tribe, hunt or self, and notice whether you actually got it?&&

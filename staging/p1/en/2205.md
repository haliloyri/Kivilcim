The model is finished and the visitors from London have come to Cambridge to see it. Two young men have built a three-stranded DNA with the phosphates tucked in the middle. One guest looks it over, and says at once that it cannot hold together.

[[story]]
In November 1951, James Watson sat in a lecture at King's College London given by Rosalind Franklin, who was presenting her X-ray data on DNA. According to one King's College account, Watson later failed to recall key figures about how much water the molecule holds. Hold on to that detail, because it comes back.

Soon afterwards he and Francis Crick, working at the Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge, had built a model of DNA as three helices with the phosphate groups on the inside. It was the first serious try. In December 1951 they invited the King's group, Franklin among them, to Cambridge to see it. The visit is one of the scenes Watson tells in *The Double Helix*, his 1968 memoir of the discovery.

Franklin's answer was immediate, and it came from her own measurements. DNA, she knew, takes up and gives off water very easily. For that to happen the sodium and phosphate groups had to sit on the outside, where water could reach them, and not buried in the core as the model had it. A structure with its charged groups packed inside would not hold together.

Lawrence Bragg, who directed the Cavendish, reacted by stopping them. Watson and Crick were told to give up model building, and the DNA problem was left to King's. They did not return to it officially until some months later, when Bragg learned that Linus Pauling was thinking about the structure of DNA. Until then the problem officially belonged to others.

The sequence is worth reading slowly. The fatal flaw was not subtle. A person who worked with the real X-ray data saw it within minutes, and the piece of information that would have prevented the mistake had been spoken in a lecture room that Watson attended a few weeks earlier. The error did not come from a lack of intelligence in Cambridge. It came from building before checking the one fact that the model most depended on.

The model failed in public and at once, in front of the people who held the data. That was an expensive way to find out, and also the fastest, because the correction arrived the same afternoon, before anything else had been built on it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Show an early model to the person who holds the measurements before you build on it.** The people closest to the raw data often see in minutes a flaw that you would only meet after months of work. A new manager who sketches a staffing plan for a shop, for example, learns more from five minutes with the person who runs the till than from weeks of spreadsheet building. Bring your rough draft early and ask what in it contradicts what they have seen, then treat the objection as free information. Your pride in the model will matter far less than the weeks you save.$$

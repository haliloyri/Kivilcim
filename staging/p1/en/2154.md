[[story]]
Nassim Taleb's *Fooled by Randomness* warns that we see the winners and forget the ones who dropped out along the way. This story is about one place where that warning has been measured: the average returns of American mutual funds. A fund that does badly is often merged into a sister fund or shut down. Once it is gone, it also disappears from the databases that work out the averages.

Burton Malkiel of Princeton ran the numbers in a study published in the Journal of Finance in 1995. As The Wall Street Journal summarised it in 1997, he used Lipper data for 1982 to 1991. The funds that lasted the whole decade averaged 17.09 percent a year. Counting every fund that existed in each year, the average fell to 15.69 percent. The S&P 500 index returned 17.52 percent a year over the same period. The gap comes from the missing.

Lipper's own figures show the mechanism inside a single year. A decade earlier, 568 diversified US stock funds had reported a return for 1986, and the average was 13.39 percent. Only 434 of those funds were still alive when the Journal looked, and their average for that same year was 14.65 percent. One year and one set of results gave two different answers, depending on who was left to be counted.

How big the effect is remains in dispute. In 1996, according to Lipper, 242 of 4,555 stock funds, about 5 percent, were merged or liquidated. John Bogle of Vanguard estimated that survivorship added about 0.3 percentage points a year to the average stock fund over the previous decade, and just under one point a year over the fifteen years to 1992. Morningstar's early results suggested that adding the dead funds back would not change the decade's average at all. The evidence is mixed.

One direct attempt to measure it started from a list. Edwin Elton, Martin Gruber and Christopher Blake tracked every fund that existed at the end of 1976. When a fund merged into another, they calculated its return using the terms of the merger, so a fund that vanished from the shelves still kept a number. Their starting point, stated in the paper's abstract, was that funds that disappear tend to do so because of poor performance.

That is the whole logic. The funds that quit were not a random slice of the funds that started. A list of survivors is a list that has already been sorted, and the sorting happened before you opened it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you trust an average, ask who is missing from it.** Every list built from what is still around, such as the shops on a street, the start-ups that give conference talks or the diets people swear by, has quietly dropped the ones that closed or gave up. A friend who admires the habits of a few successful founders is hearing only from the survivors, and the habits may be shared by the founders who failed. Asking how many started, and what became of the rest, takes a minute and often changes the answer. It lets you judge a record against everyone who tried instead of only against those who stayed.$$

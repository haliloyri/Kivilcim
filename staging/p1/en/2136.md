Two black-and-white prints lie side by side on a table, both made by the same student a few minutes earlier. One has to go into an envelope bound for a headquarters in England. A researcher with a British accent wants to know which one the student will keep and which one will go.

[[story]]
Sixty-four Harvard undergraduates answered an advertisement that promised lessons in developing black-and-white photographs, as part of a psychology study on teaching methods. Daniel Gilbert, whose *Stumbling on Happiness* is about how badly people predict their own feelings, ran the study with Jane Ebert. The students received neither money nor course credit. They got a darkroom skill and, without knowing it, a test.

Each student spent a few days shooting 12 frames on an automatic 35-mm camera. Then came a 90-minute session in the darkroom, where they chose two photographs and printed both. The researcher had one more request. Her project kept a print on file in England as an example of student work, so would they please decide which print to give up and which to take home?

The students were split at random. Half were told, "pick your favorite, but you will be able to change your mind later if you like." Their relinquished print waited in a desk drawer for five days, and the researcher promised to phone on day four. The other half were told they could take their time because they would not be able to change their minds, and their envelope went into the mail tray that night.

Another set of students only predicted. Asked how they would feel about their prints on day four, they said that having the option to swap would make no difference to them. The students who lived it saw something else. Those whose choice was final came to like their kept print more than those who could still swap. Nine days in, at least four days after the swap option had expired, the gap was still there.

One detail makes it stranger. The students with the option almost never used it, and they had said they would not (an average of 2.5 on a nine-point scale). When the researcher phoned on day four, only one wanted to swap. The door stood open and nobody walked through it, yet it dimmed the picture they were holding.

A second experiment asked a different question: would people choose that open door anyway? Eighty-nine Harvard students read a description of a poster-choosing study in two versions, one with a final choice and one where they could swap for a month. Of them, 66.3 percent preferred the changeable version, and 84.3 percent thought the average student would too. By the authors' account, the chance to change your mind weakens the mental processes that normally help you settle into what you chose.

[[lessons]]
$$**An exit you never use can still cost you the pleasure of what you chose.** Think of a job you accepted while keeping three applications alive, or a sofa bought with a 30-day return window that you keep measuring against other sofas. In both cases, your mind keeps comparing instead of settling, so the thing you picked looks a little duller than it would if the matter were closed for good. When the decision is big and the exit is not truly needed, set a date after which the choice is final, and give yourself a real chance to enjoy it.$$

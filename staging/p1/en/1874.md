A trader correctly and early believes the housing market will crash, and he buys two billion dollars of protection. To pay for it, he takes on sixteen billion dollars of another risk. He was right, and he still lost billions.

[[story]]
Howie Hubler was a bond trader at Morgan Stanley. He had joined the bank's fixed income division in the late 1990s. In 2003 he was put in charge of a group formed to bet against subprime mortgages, and in 2006 he was promoted to run the Global Proprietary Credit Group. So before the crisis there was someone inside the bank who saw the problem in housing early and even acted on it.

The group bought two billion dollars of credit default swaps on risky mortgages. That protection carried a premium that had to be paid. To finance it, Hubler's traders sold other swaps, this time on AAA-rated collateralized debt obligations, or CDOs. The amount was sixteen billion dollars. The group considered those products safe, but they contained similarly risky subprime mortgages.

Follow the logic step by step. On one side the group held protection against the risk. On the other it earned a premium by taking the risk on. At first glance the two seem to offset each other, and cheap protection with seemingly free financing is an arrangement any trader would like. Yet the side that was sold was tied to the same disease as the side that was protected. When housing went bad, the two billion dollars of protection gained in value, but the sixteen billion dollars sold cost far more.

The result, in the crisis of 2008, was a loss of about nine billion dollars. The group had earned two billion dollars on its original swaps, but that was small next to the loss. In October 2008 Hubler was offered the choice between resigning and being fired. So the man who read the housing market correctly was remembered also as the man who lost the bank's money.

What makes the story instructive is the clash between being right and losing. His idea was sound, and he even held the position to match. The flaw lay in the structure that paid for the protection: a product believed safe turned out not to be, and it overwhelmed the insurance many times over. A hedge is not a hedge if the thing paying for it carries the same risk. It is hard to notice at the start, because two items sitting in one account on paper hide the fact that they share a fate. The difference only shows when the event arrives and the loss is added up.

[[lessons]]
$$**When you protect yourself against a risk, look at where the money for that protection comes from.** When a team signs a backup supplier, a person saves money or a system is backed up, nothing is really protected if the backup sits inside the same problem. The sixteen billion dollars sold to fund Hubler's two billion of protection hung on the same crack. When you design a backup, ask yourself: could the thing holding this up fail for the same reason as the thing I am protecting? If the answer is yes, your plan has a gap and needs a separate source, and seeing that before you rely on it is far cheaper than learning it in a crisis.$$

David Goggins had been spending his training time lifting weights, not running. Then he lined up to run 100 miles in a single day. The race was in San Diego, the clock was 24 hours, and two things rode on it: a qualifying spot for Badwater, one of the hardest ultramarathons, and money for the families of fallen special-operations soldiers. His body had other plans.

[[story]]
The event was a 24-hour race in San Diego. Goggins, as he tells it in *Can't Hurt Me*, went in aiming for 100 miles. Hitting that mark would qualify him for Badwater, and the run was also raising money for the families of fallen special-operations soldiers. Those were strong reasons to finish.

What he did not have was preparation. He had been focused on lifting weights, not distance. He had done little real running training before stepping up to a distance most runners build toward for a long time.

The hours exposed that gap. His legs and feet hurt badly. His kidneys began to struggle, and blood showed up in his urine. At points he was barely moving.

Meanwhile his mind did what minds do in that situation. It kept producing reasons to stop, and they were convincing ones. The body was hurting. The goal was still far off. Stopping would have looked perfectly reasonable to anyone watching.

He kept going. Within the 24 hours, he reached 100 miles. Afterwards he was in poor physical shape for a while; the finish did not come free.

From experiences like this Goggins built what he calls the "40% rule." ##When your mind tells you that you are done, he estimates, you have used only about 40 percent of what you have.## The first "I can't" is not a reading from a gauge. It is an early warning, sent long before the tank is empty.

Two things keep the rule honest. First, the 40 percent figure is Goggins' own rule of thumb, drawn from his experience, not a measured physiological fact. Second, he presents it as a way to question the first "I can't", not as permission to ignore real medical danger.

The San Diego race itself shows both sides. He had more in him than his mind admitted in those hours. He also paid for going in underprepared, with kidney trouble and a stretch of recovery afterwards.

That tension is what makes the story more useful than a simple tale of toughness. The mind's stop signal arrives early, and it is often worth questioning. But some signals are real warnings. The smart move is to decide in advance which is which, and to prepare well enough that you are not sorting it out for the first time halfway through the hardest day of your year.

[[lessons]]
$$**The first "done" is an estimate.** In San Diego, Goggins' mind kept telling him to stop long before he actually had to. His 40% rule turns that into a habit of questioning: the first "I can't" is a protective guess, not a measured limit. Treat it as the start of a decision, not the end of one.$$

$$**A strong reason raises what you can bear.** He was not running 100 miles for fun. He needed the distance to qualify for Badwater, and the run was raising money for fallen special-operations soldiers' families. A reason that clear gives you something to hold on to when comfort says quit.$$

$$**Skipping preparation has a price.** He finished, but with almost no running training behind him, he paid in kidney trouble and a long recovery. Pushing through can work. Preparing first means you have less to push through, and fewer real warnings to argue with.$$

[[reflect]]
- When did you last stop at the first "I can't", and later suspect you had more left?
- In your work or training, which signals are worth questioning, and which are real warnings you should obey?
- What reason is strong enough to keep you going on the thing you're finding hard right now?

&&Next time you think "I'm done", can you ask whether that's your limit or just your mind's first warning?&&

A hackberry seed has a hard shell, and the tree builds that shell out of mineral while the fruit is still growing. A geochemist looked at that stony shell and asked a plain question: could the water the tree drank have left a signature inside it? The answer took fifteen years of collecting.

[[story]]
Hope Jahren, the geobiologist who wrote *Lab Girl*, built part of her research on the hackberry, a tree of the genus *Celtis*. Inside the fruit, the seed sits in a hard shell called the endocarp, and that shell is largely aragonite, a form of calcium carbonate. The tree makes it as the fruit grows.

In 2001 Jahren and three co-authors published a test in *Quaternary Research* of what that mineral remembers. They gathered hackberry fruits from 101 sites in 13 US states and one Canadian province, collected between 1979 and 1994. Then they measured the oxygen isotopes locked in each shell's carbonate, which are versions of the oxygen atom with a slightly different weight, and set them beside the oxygen isotopes of the water at the same place. The collection spanned fifteen years.

The two measurements moved together, with a correlation of 0.88. An intensive study of three trees in Sterling, Colorado, supported the link. In the Great Plains the shell also followed the average daily maximum temperature of the growing season, although that correlation was weaker, at 0.73. The shell carried a local signal.

That correlation is what turns a shell into a measuring tool. Shells grown in water of known composition show how the signal behaves, and an old shell can then be read against that pattern to estimate the water its tree once drank. Nobody has to wait for the climate to repeat itself; the record is already sitting in a cave floor or a drawer of seeds.

Because the shell is mineral, it can last, and the same measurement can be run on old seeds. Hackberry endocarps from Pintwater Cave in southern Nevada, dated to the early Holocene, pointed to precipitation with an oxygen value of about -4 per mil, against roughly -9 to -10 per mil today. The authors read this as summer monsoon moisture reaching the region, and they added that warmer temperatures could produce a similar shift. They also noted that applying the Great Plains relationship to the Great Basin is debatable. They flagged that caveat themselves.

In 1997 Jahren and a co-author had already tested whether this carbonate can be dated with radiocarbon. Their answer was yes, as long as the shell's structure and mineralogy remain intact. Hackberry seeds also turn up often as fossils, which researchers connect to their mineral shells. The condition mattered.

[[lessons]]
$$**Check what a material records about its own making.** Many things keep a trace of the conditions they formed in, and the trace is easy to miss because we look at the thing only for its use. A teacher who keeps every student's first draft can later see how the thinking changed, which a polished final essay hides completely. Once you look for that built-in record, you can answer a question about the past by reading what is already in front of you instead of asking people to remember, and you will think twice before throwing away the material that holds it.$$

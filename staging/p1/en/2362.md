The night before the biggest match of his life so far, a young man steps out of the shower and sees his hair begin to come apart in his hands. It is not his own hair. A phone call to his brother is the first thing he does.

[[story]]
Andre Agassi was in his first Grand Slam final, at the French Open, facing Andres Gomez, and he was wearing a hairpiece. He had started wearing one to disguise hair loss, and in his memoir *Open* (2009) he says that on that final's eve it began to fall apart after a shower. His own guess, in the book, is that he probably used the wrong hair rinse.

So he called his brother Philly. Together they clamped the hairpiece back into shape with clips and pins. By the account Agassi gave when the excerpts appeared, it went onto the court held together with pins. The player the tennis world saw in that final had a bold mane of hair, and a piece of it was fastened on with hardware.

He also wrote about what he asked for before the match. He prayed, in his words, "not for victory, but that my hairpiece would not fall off." Think about how that sentence sorts his priorities. A final at Roland Garros is the kind of day players build careers toward, yet his one request to heaven concerned his scalp.

The logic of that night is worth following. The fix was improvised by two people with clips and pins, so it carried a built-in risk: it could fail at any point in a match played in public. Every sprint to a drop shot and every wipe of the brow became a small test of the repair. A player in that position is in effect playing two matches, one against Gomez and one against his own head, and only one of them has a scoreboard.

Gomez won. Agassi does not blame the wig for that, and nothing in these accounts says it decided anything. What the story shows is the cost carried alongside the match: part of his attention was spent on a secret that could be exposed by a gust, a lunge or a wet towel. Two years later he won Wimbledon, and in the book he tells the hairpiece story openly, long after the secret had stopped mattering to his results.

The detail that stays is the phone call. A secret that large was shared with exactly one person, his brother, and that was enough to get a wig repaired with clips at night. What it could not do was take the weight off the player who still had to walk out the next day and play.

[[lessons]]
$$**Name the thing you are hiding, because it competes with the work for your attention.** Anything you keep covered needs part of your mind to stand guard, and that part is not available for the task in front of you. A manager hiding a shaky budget, or a student pretending to follow a course they stopped following weeks ago, will each notice the same drag on their focus in important moments. Telling one trusted person about it will not make the problem vanish, but it usually frees some of that attention for the job itself, and it leaves less of your mind standing guard.$$

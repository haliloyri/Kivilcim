A champion fighter says that anyone who wants to be great needs three people in their life. One who's better than them. One who's worse. And one who's exactly their equal. Ryan Holiday thinks this simple formula is one of the best defenses against ego.

[[story]]
In *Ego Is the Enemy*, Ryan Holiday has a chapter with a simple title: *Always Stay a Student*. At its center is Frank Shamrock.

Shamrock was a champion in mixed martial arts, one of the most demanding sports there is. Later, he became a respected trainer. Over the years, he developed a system for how fighters improve. He called it **plus, minus and equal**.

Every fighter, Shamrock said, needs three kinds of people.

The first is a **plus**: someone better than you, who can teach you. Training with someone above your level is humbling. They beat you. They show you what you don't know. They expose weaknesses you didn't realize you had. That's exactly why you need them.

The second is a **minus**: someone less skilled than you, whom you can teach. It might sound like this is just a favor to them. It isn't. Teaching forces you to understand your craft well enough to explain it. You discover gaps in your own understanding the moment someone asks, "But why?"

The third is an **equal**: someone at roughly your level, to test yourself against. Not a teacher, not a student, but a real rival. An equal pushes you in ways a stronger or weaker partner can't, because every match could go either way.

##Take away any one of the three, and growth starts to stall.##

Holiday explains why this matters so much for ego. Ego hates being a student. It wants to feel that it already knows. The more successful you become, the stronger that temptation gets. You stop seeking out people better than you, because being beaten feels bad. You stop teaching, because you're too busy. You surround yourself with people who agree with you instead of people who challenge you.

Shamrock's system works against every one of those tendencies. It keeps you humble, because there's always a plus. It keeps you clear, because you have to teach a minus. And it keeps you honest, because an equal will show you exactly where you stand.

Holiday's conclusion is simple. However far you go, stay a student. The moment you believe you've learned it all is the moment you start to decline.

[[lessons]]
$$**A plus keeps you humble.** Training with someone better than you is uncomfortable, and that's the point. They reveal what you don't yet know. Ego avoids these people because losing hurts. Seeking them out on purpose is one of the clearest signs that you're still serious about getting better.$$

$$**A minus sharpens your understanding.** Teaching someone less experienced isn't just generosity. It forces you to explain what you do, and in explaining, you find the gaps in your own knowledge. Many experts only truly understand their craft after trying to teach it to someone else.$$

$$**An equal keeps you honest.** A peer at your level can't be dismissed as a beginner or used as an excuse. Every contest with them is real. That kind of honest competition shows you where you actually stand, not where your ego would like you to believe you are.$$

- Who is your plus, your minus and your equal right now?
- Which of the three is missing from your life at the moment?
- Where have you quietly stopped being a student because you feel you already know enough?

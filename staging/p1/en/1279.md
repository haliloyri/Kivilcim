Ask most people what stops them from being brave at work and they'll say fear. Brené Brown says that's not quite it. The bigger obstacle, in her account, is the thing we build to avoid feeling that fear. She calls it armor, and once you know what to look for, it shows up in ordinary offices every day.

[[story]]
In *Dare to Lead*, published in 2018, Brown sets out what courageous leadership actually requires. Her research for the book included interviews with around 150 global C-level leaders, people at the very top of their organizations, about what brave leadership demands.

She starts with a word many leaders would rather avoid: vulnerability. Brown defines it as the emotion we experience during times of uncertainty, risk and emotional exposure. Not knowing how something will turn out. Putting your name on a decision that could fail. Being seen when you'd rather not be.

Her argument is that there is no courage without vulnerability. Every brave act involves uncertainty, risk or exposure. If you refuse to feel those things, you can't do the brave thing either.

So what gets in the way? The obvious answer would be fear. Brown argues otherwise.

##The biggest barrier to courage isn't fear itself; it's the armor people put on to protect themselves when they feel vulnerable.##

Armor is whatever we reach for to avoid feeling exposed. The book draws a line between two ways of leading: armored leadership and daring leadership. One of the forms of armor she describes is perfectionism. It looks like high standards from the outside. From the inside, it works as protection: if everything is flawless, nobody can criticize you.

Here is the catch. Armor does protect the person wearing it. But it also blocks what their team needs from them. Trust needs someone willing to be seen. Honest feedback needs someone willing to hear it. Connection needs someone who isn't hiding.

That is the trade Brown puts in front of leaders. The same protection that makes a leader feel safe can make the people around them feel it isn't safe to be honest. The leader stays covered, and the truth stays outside.

Daring leadership, in her framing, means taking some of that armor off: accepting uncertainty and exposure as the price of courage, rather than as a threat to be managed away.

[[lessons]]
$$**Courage runs through vulnerability.** Brown defines vulnerability as uncertainty, risk and emotional exposure, and says there is no courage without it. That turns a common idea upside down. Feeling exposed is not a sign you're doing leadership wrong; it's often a sign you're doing the brave part. Trying to lead without ever feeling it usually means avoiding the hard calls. Admitting you're unsure, or backing a decision that might fail, is the exposure she's describing, not a lapse in leadership.$$

$$**Armor has a cost you don't see.** Perfectionism, one of the forms of armor Brown names, can look like diligence. But the book's contrast between armored and daring leadership shows the side effect: the protection that keeps criticism out also keeps honesty, trust and connection out. The leader feels safer, and the team learns to tell them less, which means the leader hears about problems late.$$

[[reflect]]
- When you feel exposed at work or at home, what do you usually do to protect yourself?
- Can you remember someone whose honesty about not knowing something made you trust them more?
- Where might your high standards be doing the job of armor?

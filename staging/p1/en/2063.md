Children press a thumb and finger into a C shape just below the collarbone and rub for twenty or thirty seconds, one hand resting over the navel. The teacher says it wakes up the brain. Nobody in the room doubts her, because that is how children learn. Outside the room, a columnist is reading the teacher's manual somebody has mailed him.

[[story]]
"Processed foods do not contain water." That sentence sits in the Brain Gym Teachers Edition, according to Ben Goldacre, who was sent a copy by someone anonymous and wrote about it in the Guardian in March 2006. The programme, which he also covers in *Bad Science*, gave children elaborate exercises said to "enhance the experience of whole brain learning". Goldacre found it in at least hundreds of British state schools. He compiled a list of school websites that mention it and counted about 12,000 Google hits for Brain Gym on UK government web pages.

The explanations were what bothered him. Children were told to drink a glass of water before the exercises, because water is a major component of blood and carries oxygen to the brain. In "Brain Buttons" they rubbed beneath the collarbone, which the manual says stimulates the carotid arteries and sends oxygen-carrying blood to the brain. Rocking the head back and forth increased circulation to the frontal lobe. "Hook-ups", in which fingers press against each other, were said to connect the body's electrical circuits. A special yawn supposedly increased "oxidation", which Goldacre pointed out is what makes iron rust, and is not the same as oxygenation.

The exercise itself was never his target. He said plainly that a break from class is good for performance, and aimed his criticism at the invented science wrapped around it. Children, he wrote, are predisposed to learn from adults, especially teachers, and telling them stories about fairies is fine but lying to them about science is not. The same teacher who tells a class the heart pumps blood round the body is also telling them that rocking the head sends blood to the frontal lobe.

A week later, on 25 March 2006, angry emails arrived from teachers. An assistant head asked Goldacre whether he had visited any classrooms, interviewed any teachers or questioned any children. His reply was that he did not need to visit a classroom to find out whether there is water in processed food.

Goldacre saw the same pattern as in the nutrition business. The advice on offer was sensible: take a break, eat some vegetables. But advice nobody can copyright is hard to sell, so the seller needs a scientific frame that makes the product look unique.

[[lessons]]
$$**Test a piece of advice and the reason given for it as two separate things.** If a neighbour says an evening walk will do you good because "points on the soles of your feet wake up your brain", you can happily join the walk and politely decline the explanation. Splitting the two stops you from dropping a good idea because of a bad reason, and lets you say the reason is wrong without making the other person feel foolish. You keep the walk and the accurate information alike, and you can apply the same split to a diet tip, an app's promise or the justification for a work method.$$

Before the first bell at a suburban high school outside Chicago, a gym is already loud. Teenagers in chest straps watch a number on a wrist display climb while they run, and a teacher calls out the target. In an hour or so, the same students will be sitting in a reading class, pencils out.

[[story]]
In 2003 the psychiatrist John Ratey visited Naperville Central High School in Illinois, and the school's physical education staff took his ideas about exercise and the brain seriously. Paul Zientarski, who coordinated PE and health for the district, later described what they built: a class they called Learning Readiness PE, held before the school day began and often known as zero-hour PE. It started in 2004.

The district already had a habit that suited the experiment. Phil Lawler, a former PE teacher, had pushed for what Naperville called "new PE", which graded fitness and healthy living rather than sports skills, and he and Zientarski had been putting heart-rate monitors on students for years. In the new class, according to Zientarski, students did cardio three mornings a week while wearing the monitors, aiming for a zone of roughly 155 to 200 beats a minute for 20 to 35 minutes. On the other two days they climbed, swam or tried gymnastics.

The first group was small. In Zientarski's account, sixteen struggling readers were in the literacy program, and nine of them chose the early-morning class while seven did not. After a semester the nine had gained about 1.4 years in reading level, and the seven about 0.9.

The next year the district tried it with 48 students in literacy sections. The class that came right after the morning workout gained 1.5 years, and the class held last thing in the day gained 0.7. An Education Week report from 2008 added a detail that mattered to the schedulers: when the PE class and the reading class sat six hours apart, the gains were smaller. Counselors began recommending that the hardest classes be placed right after PE.

Zientarski also reported a math version, with an introductory algebra class of 24 students split into two groups of twelve, in which the morning-exercise group improved about twice as much in its first semester.

None of this was a controlled trial. Groups were tiny, students had chosen their own schedules, and the figures come from the people running the program. The district's own associate superintendent told Education Week that she wanted university-based studies before changing schedules across the district. Even so, the pattern was consistent enough that Zientarski says 35 districts have adopted parts of the program, and it gave Ratey a school-sized example for a question his book asks: what does a body that has just worked hard do for a mind that is about to?

[[lessons]]
$$**Schedule the hardest thinking right after you have moved.** The Naperville data suggest timing mattered as much as the exercise itself, because the gains shrank when hours separated the workout from the class. You can try the same logic in an ordinary week: if you write reports, put the report right after a brisk walk, a flight of stairs or a bike ride to the office, and leave email for the sagging hours. Because timing is something you control, you can test it on your own work without any equipment. Keep your own record of how the session went, since Naperville's numbers came from informal comparisons and your own notes are the same kind of evidence.$$

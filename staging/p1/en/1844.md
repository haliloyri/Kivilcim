[[open]]
A drug company holds a compound that kills leukemia cells in a dish and in mice and leaves normal cells alone. Turning it into a medicine will be slow and expensive, and the company hesitates. A researcher outside the company keeps pushing for the one thing that will settle the question: a trial in people.

[[story]]
Brian Druker studied chronic myeloid leukemia, or CML, and this story is a real episode from the history of cancer, the subject of Siddhartha Mukherjee's *The Emperor of All Maladies*. The disease is linked to the Philadelphia chromosome, which makes an abnormal protein, and the plan in this story was to aim a drug directly at that protein.

The compound came from industry. In 1992 chemists at Ciba-Geigy found imatinib, then known as STI571. In 1996, work done together by Druker and the company showed that it killed CML cells in culture and in mice, and that it spared normal cells.

Novartis was reluctant at first. Without a push, the compound might have stayed a promising result in a laboratory notebook. Druker moved from Boston to Oregon Health and Science University, OHSU, and kept pressing for the drug to be tested in patients.

It was. In the first trial, the patients who received a high dose all responded. On 10 May 2001 the FDA granted accelerated approval, and the drug, sold as Gleevec, launched at a price of 26,000 dollars a year. In 2009 Druker received the Lasker Award.

The dates show how the pieces fit. Between the laboratory result of 1996 and the approval of 2001 lay five years of testing, and the Lasker Award came eight years after the launch. Each step depended on the one before it: a target, a compound, a result in cells, a result in mice and then patients.

Look at what made this work. Nobody was guessing at a chemical and hoping. The protein that drove the disease was known, a compound was found that blocked it, and the lab results showed it hit cancer cells and spared normal ones. The trial then tested a specific prediction, which is why a small group of patients could answer the question so cleanly.

Notice also who carried the project forward. The compound belonged to a company, but the push to test it came from a scientist who understood the mechanism well enough to know what the drug should do. A drug that works only against one known protein can also be tested on exactly the patients who have that protein, so the first answers can arrive quickly and clearly.

[[lessons]]
$$**Find the specific cause of a problem before you design the fix, then aim your effort exactly there.** In a business this might mean working out which step in a sales process loses the customers, or which handoff produces the delays, instead of improving everything a little. Once you can name the mechanism, you can predict what the right intervention should do, and then test that prediction on a small and carefully chosen group. A fix aimed at a known cause is easier to defend, easier to measure and far harder to dismiss as a hunch.$$

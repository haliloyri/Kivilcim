[[story]]
Adam Grant's *Hidden Potential* includes the early career of Steve Martin, who started doing stand-up in the 1960s and bombed again and again. He later said he was not naturally talented. One critic called him "the most serious booking error in the history of Los Angeles," and during one show a heckler threw that glass of red wine at him.

His first answer was imitation. He listened to other comedians' material, copied their mannerisms and practised. It did not work. His shows stayed weak, and one stretch ran about twenty minutes without a single laugh.

Writing was the thing he had dismissed, because it did not come naturally to him. He has described it as "hard, so hard." After a few years of struggling he had what he called a "horrible revelation": he would have to write everything himself.

His first attempt at a writing job did not go smoothly. A variety show was looking for young writers, and the material he submitted was rejected. The head writer hired him anyway, after seeing him play the banjo, and paid him out of his own salary. When Martin was then asked to write an introduction, he could not type a single word, so he phoned his roommate and borrowed a joke.

For the next few years he wrote for television by day and performed stand-up by night. His agent told him, "Stick to writing." Writing taught him to strip a joke down to its essentials, to cut everything that was not doing work, a habit that mattered on every page he wrote afterwards. By the late 1970s he was selling out large arenas, had a platinum comedy album, performed stand-up on *Saturday Night Live*, and starred in *The Jerk*, a film that grew out of his writing.

Look at the order of events and what each step actually fixed. Copying other comedians polished his delivery, but nothing was fixing the material, because the material was the part he had been avoiding. The skill he had written off as too hard was exactly the one missing from his act. And the writing did not replace the stage; the stage was where each joke he wrote got its verdict, every night.

[[lessons]]
$$**The skill you keep avoiding because it does not come naturally may be the one your results are waiting on.** A cook who is brilliant at plating but never learned to write a budget will keep wondering why the restaurant struggles, and a manager who can charm a room but dreads writing a clear plan will find the plan's absence costing more each month. Practising what you already do well feels productive, but it can polish a part that was never the limit. Ask what you have been quietly calling "not my thing", and give that one a real, scheduled try.$$

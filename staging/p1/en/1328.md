"This won't work for me." Five words, said before anything has been tried. They sound like a prediction. Follow them into the very next action, though, and they start to look more like an instruction: try a little, not too hard, and be ready to stop.

[[story]]
In *The Success Principles*, Jack Canfield argues that what you believe is possible shapes three things: whether you try at all, how hard you try, and how long you keep going. None of those is the result itself. But results depend on all three.

Take that limiting belief, "this won't work for me." Canfield traces what it does. The belief comes first, before any action. It leads to half-hearted action, because putting full effort into something you expect to fail feels pointless. Half-hearted action produces weak results. And the weak results then seem to confirm the belief you started with.

That is the uncomfortable part of the loop. From the inside, it doesn't feel like a belief shaping events. It feels like the evidence came in and proved you right. The shrinking of effort happened quietly, early, and nobody recorded it.

This is why Canfield treats results as feedback about your own thinking and behavior, not only about outside circumstances. A disappointing outcome may say something about the market, the timing, or other people. It may also say something about how much you actually did, and what you expected going in.

His remedy doesn't start with the circumstances either. It's tempting to wait for the world to change before letting yourself believe something different. Canfield turns that around. ##Notice the limiting belief and replace it with one that supports action before the circumstances change, because the belief is what sets the effort.##

It's worth being careful about what he is and isn't saying. This is Canfield's interpretation of how belief influences behavior. He is not claiming that believing something makes it happen, or that belief alone decides outcomes. The claim is narrower and more useful: belief works upstream. It sets the effort and persistence that results are built on.

So two people can face the same situation and walk away with different results. Not necessarily because one was luckier, but because one of them had already decided how it would end, and then acted accordingly.

[[lessons]]
$$**Belief works upstream.** Canfield's "Believe it's possible" isn't about wishful thinking. It's about sequence: what you believe decides whether you try, how hard, and for how long, and every result is built on those three. A different belief doesn't guarantee a different result, but it changes what you're willing to put in.$$

$$**A doubt can manufacture its own proof.** The loop runs from "this won't work for me" to half-hearted action to a weak result that seems to confirm the doubt. The evidence feels objective, but the belief helped produce it by trimming the effort first.$$

$$**Results are information about you, too.** Canfield treats outcomes as feedback on your own thinking and behavior, not just on circumstances. Asking how much you actually did, and what you expected, gives you something to change. Blaming conditions alone leaves you waiting for them.$$

[[reflect]]
- Where have you already decided something won't work before really trying it?
- Can you recall a time a result seemed to prove what you expected all along?
- Looking back, how much effort did you actually give that attempt?

&&Before your next attempt at something you've been doubting, what belief are you carrying into it, and how might it change how much you actually do?&&

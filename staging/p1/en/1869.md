One city's Black population climbs from about 6,000 to about 120,000 in under twenty years. Behind that leap there is no single decision, no single train, no single call. There are the separate decisions of well over a hundred thousand people, each made about their own life.

[[story]]
Isabel Wilkerson's *The Warmth of Other Suns* tells the Great Migration as the journeys of individual people, not of crowds. This story looks instead at what those journeys add up to, the numbers. The migration ran from about 1910 to 1970, and by common estimates about six million Black Americans left the rural South.

A total like that is hard to grasp on its own. City by city, the change becomes easier to see. Detroit's Black population was about 6,000 in 1910 and about 120,000 by 1929. In Chicago it rose from 278,000 to 813,000 between 1940 and 1960. That is an increase of more than half a million in twenty years, in a single city. Not all population growth comes from migration, but a leap of that size shows what newcomers contributed. When a city grows that fast within the same streets, the strain falls on housing, jobs and schools.

On a national scale the shift is sharper still. In 1900 about 740,000 Black Americans lived outside the South, around 8 percent of the Black population. By 1970 more than 10.6 million did, which is 47 percent. The geography of a whole people was close to turned around within seventy years.

None of these figures has a single cause. The demand for factory workers in wartime, the gap in wages between South and North and discrimination itself all pressed at once. Yet every move was one person's or one family's decision about their own life.

What stands out is that a very large event is made of very small units. A movement of six million is not one wave of a crowd but the accumulation of many separate decisions. The totals for Chicago and Detroit read the same way: their growth reflects the decisions of many people to change where they lived. To understand a change like this, look first at the total and then at the single decisions that make it up.

The same caution applies to the numbers themselves. A total shows scale, but it says nothing about why any one person left, or what that person hoped to find. That is why a book like Wilkerson's goes down to individual travellers: the sum tells you how big it was, and only the single case tells you what it was like. A figure of 813,000 is one answer. A single person's reason for boarding a train is another, and a useful reading of this history needs both.

[[lessons]]
$$**If you want to understand or create a large change, find the single decision it is built from.** A team's culture, a customer's satisfaction or a region's population is usually the sum of small choices repeated day after day. Meetings that go badly, for instance, are made up of the individual choices about agenda, length and who gets to speak in each one. Once you have named the unit, you have something you can change, and an abstract goal turns into a step you can take today. Pick the unit that repeats most often and that you control, and start there.$$

Thick fog has settled over a small mountain airport. The control tower cannot see the runway, and there is no ground radar to show it. At one end of the strip a huge jet swings round and its engines begin to rise. Somewhere in the white, another jet is still rolling slowly towards it.

[[story]]
Ori and Rom Brafman's *Sway* opens with the Tenerife airport disaster, and this is how that day unfolded. On 27 March 1977 two Boeing 747s, one flown by KLM and one by Pan Am, were diverted to Los Rodeos airport on Tenerife. A bomb had exploded at the airport on Gran Canaria, where they had meant to land, and the flights were redirected. Los Rodeos was small, and it quickly filled with parked aircraft. The fog arrived later.

Because the taxiway was blocked, planes waiting to depart had to taxi along the runway itself. KLM flight 4805 was commanded by Jacob Veldhuyzen van Zanten, the airline's chief flight instructor and one of its most senior pilots. According to published accounts, his crew was concerned about crew duty-hour limits that had just been tightened in the Netherlands, and he chose to refuel fully on Tenerife. The refuelling took about 35 minutes, and by the time it ended the fog had rolled in.

The tower had no ground radar and could not see either aircraft. At the end of the runway the KLM jet turned. The first officer, Klaas Meurs, pointed out that they had no clearance from air traffic control. Van Zanten said he knew and told him to ask. The tower then read out route instructions that used the word "takeoff" without actually clearing them to take off. The first officer began reading them back and said, "We are now at takeoff." The captain said, "We're going."

"OK," came the answer from the tower, and then, "Stand by for takeoff, I will call you." At that very moment the Pan Am crew radioed that they were still taxiing along the runway, and the two transmissions overlapped. In the KLM cockpit this came through as a shrill whistle. The flight engineer asked, in Dutch, whether the Pan Am was clear of the runway. The captain answered emphatically that it was. The takeoff continued.

The KLM jet struck the Pan Am aircraft in the fog. All 248 people on the KLM plane died, and 335 of the 396 on the Pan Am plane, a total of 583 deaths. The Spanish investigation named the captain's takeoff without clearance as the main cause, with the fog and the radio interference as major contributing factors.

Look at the shape of the morning. Each decision was small and sensible by itself: refuel here, line up, start rolling. But each one made the next harder to reverse, and once the engines were rising, a question from the flight engineer had to push against all of that momentum. The question was the last chance to stop, and the answer it received was a confident one.

Aviation changed because of that day. Standard phrases became mandatory, "takeoff" is now used only for a real takeoff clearance, and "OK" no longer counts as an acknowledgment. The accident also contributed to crew resource management training, which teaches junior crew to challenge a captain.

[[lessons]]
$$**Agree in advance who may call a stop, and treat their question as an order to pause.** Once a plan is moving, every step makes the next one feel more natural, and a doubt from the side can sound like noise. In a launch, an operation, a negotiation or a long drive, the person who senses the problem is often junior or quiet. Before you begin, say aloud: if anyone asks whether it is safe to continue, we stop and check, whatever our rank or schedule. A pause that costs two minutes is cheap next to a step that cannot be taken back.$$

Think back over the last month and ask a simple question: in which moments were you genuinely happy? For most people, the list is short. A few conversations, a few hours doing something they love, a meal with the right people. Richard Koch thinks that short list may be one of the most important things you can know about yourself.

[[story]]
In *The 80/20 Principle*, Richard Koch spends most of his time on business: customers, products, profits. But he also turns the principle toward life itself, and toward one of the hardest questions of all: what makes us happy?

His suggestion is that happiness, like most things, is unevenly spread. A large share of our happiness tends to come from a small share of our time. There are certain moments, activities, places and people that reliably lift us. And there are others that reliably drag us down. Much of the rest of life sits somewhere in between.

To make this practical, Koch proposes a simple exercise.

First, identify your **happiness islands**. These are the small periods of time, and the particular activities or situations, in which you are consistently happiest. Perhaps it's early mornings with a book, time with a certain friend, working on a particular kind of project, being outdoors, playing with your children.

Then, identify your **unhappiness islands**. These are the times, places, people or tasks that reliably make you feel worst. A certain meeting, a particular commute, a draining relationship, a type of work you dread.

##Once you can see both clearly, Koch's advice is straightforward: expand the first, and shrink the second.##

Spend more time on your happiness islands. Protect them. Build more of your week around them. At the same time, look hard at your unhappiness islands. Some can be avoided entirely. Others can be reduced, changed or handled differently.

Koch points out that many people accept their unhappiness islands without question. They've always had that draining weekly obligation, that dreaded task, that relationship that leaves them exhausted, and it doesn't occur to them that things could be different. Often, they could.

He isn't suggesting that life can be made entirely happy, or that every unpleasant duty can be escaped. Some can't. But he argues that happiness is, at least in part, a matter of design. If you know where your happiness actually comes from, you can deliberately arrange more of your life around it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Happiness is concentrated.** Koch suggests that a large share of our happiness comes from a small share of our time. That makes a short list of happy moments extremely valuable. Knowing exactly what lifts you gives you a map for where to invest more of your limited hours.$$

$$**Expand your happiness islands.** Once you know your happiest activities and people, you can deliberately make more room for them. Many people leave these moments to chance. Protecting and expanding them, on purpose, is one of the simplest ways to feel better about your weeks.$$

$$**Shrink your unhappiness islands.** Many draining situations persist only out of habit or unexamined obligation. Koch encourages questioning them. Some can be removed; others reduced or changed. Even small reductions in what reliably makes you miserable can noticeably improve your overall happiness. Small, reliable sources of joy add up over time.$$

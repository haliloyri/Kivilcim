Sixty-one versions of one soda go out to three thousand consumers. Each is sweetened a little differently from the one before, and the gaps between them are tiny. Nobody is trying to make the sweetest drink. The aim is to find the single spot on that ladder where people like it most.

[[story]]
The man behind that test was Howard Moskowitz, a researcher whom Michael Moss calls a legend in the food industry. Moskowitz helped coin the term "the bliss point": the perfect amount of sugar in a food or drink, the amount that appeals to us most. Moss had him explain how it works using a new flavor line for Dr Pepper.

For that soda, Moskowitz concocted 61 different versions of sweetness, each slightly different from its neighbor, and had them tried by 3,000 consumers. The reactions were run through regression analysis, a statistical method for finding the pattern in a large pile of answers. The target was one point on a curve.

The curve is the interesting part, and Moss explains it with coffee. A little sugar makes the cup better, and for a while each spoonful makes you like it more. Then you pass a point, and every additional spoonful makes you like it less. Pleasure rises, peaks and falls, and the bliss point is the peak.

Nobody can guess where that peak sits by tasting a single sample. It hides between neighboring formulas that differ only slightly, and it shows up only when many people have rated many versions. That is why the number 61 mattered, and why the number 3,000 mattered.

There is a practical edge to this. A bliss point is not a vague preference; it is a number a company can write down, repeat and defend. Of two neighboring formulas, the one nearer the peak is the one people like more, and the other sits a small step off it. In a business that sells sweetness, a small step is exactly the kind of difference that a test of 3,000 people can detect and a conference-room debate cannot.

Moss argues that the idea travels far beyond soda. Breads, pasta sauces and low-fat yogurt, he says, have been made sweeter. The logic is the same each time: find the amount that people like best, and treat every other amount as a miss.

What stands out is the method as much as the sugar. Moskowitz did not rely on one expert palate or one confident opinion. He built a ladder of small differences, let thousands of people react to it, and read the answer off the data. The result was less a flash of taste than a measurement, and measurements can be repeated for the next product.

[[lessons]]
$$**When you cannot guess the best setting, build a ladder of small versions and let many people react to it.** A meeting gives you one opinion at a time, which is one point on the curve, while five slightly different drafts show you its shape. This works for the length of a newsletter, the price of a workshop or the order of an agenda, anything where people can tell you which version they like. Ask what the smallest difference is that your audience could still notice, and place your versions that far apart. It turns an argument about taste into a short test that anyone can read.$$

A basketball team plays an entire season on one idea: a shot from far away is worth more than the shot next to the basket, so take more of them. The idea works well enough to win 65 games. Then, on the biggest night, the ball stops going in, and the whole idea is on trial.

[[story]]
In the 2017-18 season, the Houston Rockets, built around analytics and led in the front office by general manager Daryl Morey, did something no NBA team had done before. They attempted 3,470 three-pointers and 3,436 two-pointers. They were the first team in the league's history to take more shots from three than from two. For a sport that had long treated the mid-range and the paint as home, it was a loud break with habit.

The logic is simple arithmetic. A three-pointer is worth one point more than a two-pointer, so if you make enough of them, they are worth more than the easier shot. The Rockets decided to follow the numbers further than anyone else had. The results were strong. They won 65 games, which is the most in the franchise's history.

Then came Game 7 of the Western Conference finals against the Golden State Warriors. The Rockets missed 27 three-pointers in a row. Shots that had carried them through the whole season stopped falling, for long enough that a game and a series slipped away. Their strategy did not change, since it could not be changed in the middle of the game, and the ball simply did not go in.

Here the story becomes interesting, and a little uncomfortable for fans of both camps, because two easy conclusions are available and both are wrong. One says the miss streak proves the whole approach was foolish. The other says it was just bad luck and so proves nothing. A cold night can come from variance, but it can also hide a real weakness, such as a defence that has learned where the shots are coming from.

A bet built on odds is judged by the odds, and by a single night only weakly. The Rockets' season is evidence that the plan could win 65 times. Game 7 is evidence that a plan built on high volume carries large swings. You can hold both facts and still not know which one will matter next. The honest response is to keep the strategy and keep asking what would show it was wrong. That is harder than either cheering or panic, because it asks you to hold a plan firmly and loosely at once.

[[lessons]]
$$**Judge a bet by the odds you took before the result, and keep checking whether those odds still hold.** One bad night does not refute a good plan, and one good season does not prove it, so neither should end your thinking. Write down, before you act, which result would make you change course, and look at the evidence in batches rather than one outcome at a time. Treat it as a habit you repeat each season, not a one-off audit. This protects you from dropping a sound approach after one bad day, and from clinging to a weak one because it once worked.$$

A summer-camp team has just won a game, and its members go over to tell the losers they played fairly. The adult in charge of their group, the senior counsellor, has a word for them. The word is "soft".

[[story]]
In the summer of 1953 the psychologist Muzafer Sherif ran a camp for boys at Middle Grove, New York. Parents had been told it was a study of leadership skills. In fact Sherif was studying how conflict arises between two groups of boys, and some of the counsellors were his own observers. Written instructions told the staff to keep a "professional distance".

It did not go as planned. The boys had made friends in the first days, and when the groups were split the friends resented it. A winning team that praised its opponents' sportsmanship was told by its senior counsellor that it was "soft". Both groups ended up accusing the staff of bias, and according to the writer Gina Perry, one boy found a staff notebook that gave the game away. Sherif wrote the whole thing off as a failure.

Perry spent years in Sherif's archives and wrote about it in *The Psychologist* in 2014 and in her book *The Lost Boys*. She points out that Sherif's money was nearly gone when he tried again the next summer, in 1954, at Robbers Cave State Park in Oklahoma. This time one of his colleagues, O. J. Harvey, chose athletic boys from Oklahoma City playgrounds, reasoning that they would be more competitive. Twenty-two boys aged eleven and twelve spent about three weeks there, split into two groups called the Rattlers and the Eagles.

The staff stayed hands-off. Food fights and name-calling went unchecked, the canteen cooks threatened to quit over it, and staff supplied the matches when the boys burned a flag. Perry reports that scores were sometimes manipulated to sharpen the rivalry, and Harvey later said of one contest: "So we deliberately let them win the competition."

The result became one of the best-known studies in social psychology: hostility rose when the groups competed for prizes only one could win, and it eased when they had to work together on shared tasks. The psychologist Michael Billig pointed out in 1976 that the staff were in effect a third group in the camp. The rivalry the textbooks describe was shaped by adults, and in 1953 the boys were already treating those adults as the problem.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before blaming people for a rivalry, look at how the contest was set up.** If two teams in a company fight over leads, or two siblings fight over the front seat, the rules often reward winning over cooperating, and some adult or manager put those rules in place. Ask who chose the prize, who keeps the score and who benefits when the groups keep their distance. Once you can see the setup, you can change a rule, for instance by giving both sides a shared target, instead of lecturing the people inside it, and you may find the quarrel fades with it.$$

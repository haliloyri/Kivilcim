Twenty-five British warships patrol the Mediterranean, and two German ones are slipping away to the east. A British rear admiral holds his orders in his hand. One phrase in them sounds perfectly clear until the moment he has to apply it, and that moment is only days away.

[[story]]
Barbara Tuchman gives a chapter of *The Guns of August* to the hunt for the German battlecruiser *Goeben* in August 1914. Under Rear Admiral Wilhelm Souchon, she sailed with a smaller consort, the light cruiser *Breslau*. On 4 August, with war just breaking out, Souchon bombarded the Algerian ports of Bône and Philippeville, from which French colonial troops were being shipped across to France, and then turned away east.

The British Mediterranean fleet had, on paper, every advantage: three battlecruisers, four armoured cruisers, four light cruisers and fourteen destroyers against two German ships. Britain had not yet declared war, and Churchill's order to engage was cancelled after a Cabinet decision. The fleet's commander, Admiral Sir Archibald Berkeley Milne, was slow to see that the Germans were heading east.

The orders were the other problem. Churchill had told the commanders to avoid being brought to action against "superior forces". Rear Admiral Ernest Troubridge, whose cruiser squadron lay across the Germans' likely path, had been told not to get seriously engaged with such forces. Nobody had defined the word. Was *Goeben*, one powerful ship, "superior" to a squadron of four older cruisers? On 7 August Troubridge gave up the chase.

Only HMS *Gloucester*, a light cruiser, kept shadowing the Germans, and she briefly fought *Breslau* before she could do no more. On 10 August the German ships reached the Dardanelles. Under German pressure the Ottoman government let them through to Constantinople, and on 16 August both were handed over to the Ottoman navy and renamed *Yavuz Sultan Selim* and *Midilli*. They kept their German crews and Souchon.

The ships' presence helped to pull the Ottoman Empire into the war. On 29 October they took part in the Black Sea raid on Russian ports. In Britain, Milne was recalled and never held another active command. Troubridge was court-martialled in November on a charge that was not proved.

What the episode shows is how a single undefined word travels down a chain of command, and how little it takes for a fleet with every material advantage to come away empty-handed. Churchill wrote it, Milne passed it on, Troubridge read it, and at each step the unspoken answer to "what counts as superior?" was filled in by the most careful instinct available.

[[lessons]]
$$**An order that leaves its key word undefined hands the real decision to the most cautious person who reads it.** A manager who tells a new hire to "avoid risky purchases" has given no rule at all, because to one person risky means anything expensive and to another it means any new supplier. Both will act in good faith and reach opposite results, and the manager will only find out after the fact. When you give or receive an instruction with a judgment word in it, such as risky, urgent, big or reasonable, ask for one concrete example of each side of the line before anyone moves.$$

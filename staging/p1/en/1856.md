For thirty days a doctor takes about eighty percent of his food from packets, boxes and advertised brands. Every morning he steps on the scales, and he also slides into a brain scanner. By the end of the month the scales have told only half the story.

[[story]]
Chris van Tulleken, the author of *Ultra-Processed People*, is a British doctor. In 2021 he ran an experiment on himself for the BBC documentary "What Are We Feeding Our Kids?". Ultra-processed food normally made up about thirty percent of what he ate. For a month he raised that share to about eighty percent.

The experiment ran for roughly thirty days, and it could be measured. The scales, hormone levels and a brain scan gave him records to set beside how he felt. That made it possible to look at what his body did instead of settling for "I feel different".

The weight showed first. Van Tulleken gained more than six kilograms in the month. Reports give slightly different figures, but all of them are above six. His body mass index rose as well, his sleep got worse and he became constipated.

The hormones were more interesting. Hormones linked to hunger went up and the ones that signal fullness went down. In plain terms, his body was sending stronger messages to eat more and weaker messages that he had had enough.

The brain scan was the most striking part. It showed new connections between regions involved in reward and in habit. That suggests the distance between wanting something and doing it automatically may have shrunk, though one scan of one person after one month cannot establish a mechanism.

The strength and the weakness of the experiment sit in the same place. It was one person, one month and no control group. Even so, a doctor recording the change in his own body makes a concrete case for the idea that what you eat can alter your weight and also what you want. The weight is an outcome. The thing that moved is the route by which wanting arises.

It also stops looking like a matter of willpower alone. Hormones and brain connections shift before a craving arrives. The person who walks into the kitchen after a month of this is a slightly different person from the one who walked in a month earlier.

[[lessons]]
$$**After a stretch of eating the same way, look at which of your cravings have changed.** Someone who grabs something ready-made at midday and a packaged dinner for a month may find, without noticing, that the hand goes to sweets and snacks more often. Blaming your willpower leads nowhere useful, whereas asking whether your food has shaped your wants gives you a smaller and more realistic place to start. Once you can see where a craving comes from, you can rearrange the kitchen and the routine around it instead of fighting the craving head-on.$$

Patients are moved out of asylum wards and city psychiatric hospitals so that thousands of beds stand empty. Officials expect them to fill with people hurt by bombs, and with people whose minds have given way under the noise and fear.

[[story]]
Before the Second World War began, the British government prepared for a particular kind of disaster. Planners assumed that air raids would cause panic and a wave of mental breakdown. According to the historian Edgar Jones, writing in *The Lancet Psychiatry* in 2021, the government estimated that 1.2 million civilians would be injured in a sixty-day bombing campaign. In September 1939 the Ministry of Health moved patients out of five urban psychiatric hospitals and cleared asylum wards, which freed 36,000 beds.

The bombing came in September 1940. Rutger Bregman cites the Blitz as a case where people met disaster better than the experts expected. The medical records of the time support part of that. In February 1941 the Ministry of Health found that only 5% of air-raid admissions were for "nervous shock," and most of those people recovered within two weeks. Felix Brown, writing in 1941, called raid-induced psychoneuroses in people with no earlier history "comparatively rare," and P. E. Vernon reported fewer cases of neurosis than clinicians had expected. Jones and his colleagues, reviewing the evidence in 2004, found "little evidence" that admissions for formal mental illness rose appreciably.

The picture was not tidy. In December 1940 the research group Mass-Observation, commissioned to survey morale in heavily bombed towns, reported "depressive and defeatist feelings, which at present exist only in embryo." In Coventry its observers saw "open signs of hysteria, terror and neurosis." The Ministry of Home Security dismissed these findings as "defeatist and mischievous gossip" and withdrew the reports from ministers. Psychosomatic illness did rise, especially stomach ulcers among emergency workers.

So two stories stand side by side. Jones summed up one of them at a British Psychological Society conference in 2015: "there was no real evidence for a total collapse in morale." The other says that fear and breakdown were real and were played down in official accounts. Historians still disagree, partly because a war that lasted so long supplies examples of both.

What the record does show is that the forecast of mass panic was wrong. People did not collapse in the numbers planners had prepared for. Many coped by taking a job in civil defence, in the fire service, the police or the Home Guard.

[[lessons]]
$$**Test a worst-case forecast against the first real data, and keep the report that disagrees with you.** A manager who predicts that staff will revolt after a reorganisation, or a parent who expects a child to fall apart at a new school, often behaves as if the prediction were already true. Look at what actually happens in the first weeks, and write down both the reassuring signs and the troubling ones instead of filing the second kind away. That habit lets you correct a plan early, and it lets you do so without pretending that everything went well or that everything went badly.$$

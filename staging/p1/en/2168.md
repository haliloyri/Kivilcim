[[story]]
Edward O. Thorp, author of *A Man for All Markets*, built a wearable roulette computer with Claude Shannon, and this account follows Thorp's own 1998 paper about it. The device was the size of a pack of cigarettes and held twelve transistors. The wearer worked two microswitches in his shoes with his big toes. One switch started the computer, the other timed the rotor and the ball of the roulette wheel.

Once the rotor had been timed, the computer played a musical scale of eight tones, each marking a rotor octant passing the reference mark. When the ball was timed for the second time, the tones stopped, and the last tone heard named the octant to bet on. The sound came through a tiny loudspeaker in one ear canal. They painted the wires to match skin and hair and stuck them on with spirit gum. The wires were hair-thin steel and fragile. The first time Thorp was fully wired up for a rehearsal, Shannon cocked his head and asked with a twinkle, "What makes you tick?"

In the lab the expected gain was about 44 percent when betting on the best octant. In the summer of 1961 the Shannons and the Thorps met in Las Vegas to test it in a casino, using ten-cent chips. Shannon stood by the wheel and timed the ball, writing down numbers like just another "system" player for camouflage. Thorp bet at the far end of the layout and paid little attention to the ball and rotor. The two acted as if they were strangers, while the wives watched whether the casino suspected anything.

Once a lady next to Thorp looked over in horror. He left the table quickly and found the speaker peering from his ear canal, in his words, like an alien insect. The wires broke often, and each break meant tedious repairs and rewiring. When the computer worked, it worked very well, and the predictions matched the expected gain from the lab. But the wires kept them from serious betting on that trip.

Thorp thought thicker wires, hair grown over the ears (a conspicuous style at the time) or talking their reluctant wives into "wiring up" could fix it. The project stopped instead. In June 1961 an attractive offer took Thorp away from MIT, and the difficulty of working at a distance and the cost of their other activities kept them from continuing. They kept the method and the computer secret until 1966. On 30 May 1985 Nevada signed a law banning devices that predict outcomes or track cards, aimed at blackjack and roulette devices; the descendants of that cigarette pack were by then powerful enough to be outlawed.

[[lessons]]
$$**Test a system under real conditions and look for its weakest part first.** A cook whose new menu is perfect at home may meet a failure he never saw in practice the first time he serves a crowded lunch, perhaps one stove too few or a pass where plates pile up. The part that gives way under real conditions is often the thinnest and most ordinary one, not the clever calculation at the center. A small field trial therefore tells you, cheaply and early, which detail to strengthen before the real work starts and the first real day brings its surprises.$$

Over breakfast in Texas, a man sits across from a 93-year-old former teacher and feels slightly afraid of her. Then a friend walks in and greets her warmly. The woman who had seemed so stern turns, in front of his eyes, into someone he had not met yet.

[[story]]
The man was the writer David Brooks, and the woman was LaRue Dorsey, a 93-year-old former teacher in Waco, Texas. Brooks tells the story himself in an interview with the Templeton Foundation, so what follows is his account of that breakfast.

He says he found her intimidating. That detail matters, because it names the starting point honestly. Being afraid of a stern former teacher is a very ordinary first impression, the kind most of us form in a few seconds and then act on without noticing.

Then a friend of Brooks walked in and greeted her warmly. Brooks says that in front of him she changed. The stern figure became, in his words, a bright-eyed girl. Nothing about the room had altered, and neither had her age or her history. What had changed was how she was being looked at, and she responded to it at once.

Brooks draws a conclusion from this: the quality of attention we give to other people is a moral act. When we look at someone, he suggests, we are not just observing them. We are choosing which part of them to treat as real. The person tends to answer the version of themselves that we are prepared to see.

Consider what his friend did. By Brooks's account it was not elaborate. There was no speech and no grand gesture. He greeted her warmly, and in that greeting he addressed the whole of her and not only the sternness that had kept Brooks at a distance.

The first impression Brooks had formed was real, but it was partial. Age and authority are the parts of a person that show first, and they can hide almost everything else. Warmth is one of the ways the rest comes out. His friend, who evidently knew her, used it without effort. For Brooks, watching, the moment showed how much of a person he could miss simply by trusting the first thing he saw.

It also shows who was responsible for the change. It was not the woman, who was the same person throughout. It was the person doing the looking, and that is the only part any of us can control.

[[lessons]]
$$**Greet people with warmth before you decide who they are, because the way you look at them shapes which part of them answers.** Think of the person who seems stern, shy or guarded in your office, your family or your street. The first impression is rarely the whole of them, and a warm, specific greeting is an invitation for more of them to show up. Try it with someone who intimidates you this week, and notice what changes in the room, and in you, when your first words come from curiosity instead of caution. The warmth costs you nothing, and it may be the only way you ever meet the rest of them.$$

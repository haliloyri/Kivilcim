A group of women sit in a quiet room. Each of them lives with chronic PTSD, and each has already tried other treatments that didn't bring relief. Now the instruction is almost absurdly small: notice your breath. Notice where your feet meet the floor. Bessel van der Kolk thought this simple request might reach something that explanations hadn't.

[[story]]
The starting point is an argument that runs through *The Body Keeps the Score*. Van der Kolk contends that understanding why you feel threatened does not, by itself, stop your body from reacting as though you are under threat.

That is an uncomfortable idea for anyone who puts faith in insight. A person can sit in talk therapy, trace a fear back to its source, understand exactly where it came from, and still have a racing heart and a clenched chest the next time something sets it off. The explanation is correct. The nervous system simply doesn't act on it.

So van der Kolk's Trauma Center tested a different route. They studied trauma-sensitive yoga for women with chronic PTSD who had not improved with other treatments. One group attended a weekly yoga class. A control group did not get the yoga. The researchers compared how their symptoms changed.

The yoga group showed significantly greater reductions in PTSD symptoms than the control group.

Van der Kolk is specific about why he thinks it worked, and it has little to do with flexibility or fitness. The classes trained a few quiet abilities. One was noticing internal sensations safely, a capacity researchers call interoception. Another was regulating the breath. A third was tolerating feelings in the body without being overwhelmed by them.

For someone whose body has become a source of alarm, just paying attention to it can feel risky. The classes made that attention something to practise, a little at a time, week after week.

##What changed things wasn't a new explanation of the trauma; it was repeated practice at feeling the body without alarm.##

He also measured heart rate variability, a sign of how flexibly the nervous system responds to changing conditions. He presents yoga as one way to improve it: a body that can shift gears, rather than one stuck on high alert.

Van der Kolk doesn't offer yoga as a single cure. The book describes a range of body-based and action-based approaches, including theater programs, neurofeedback and EMDR. What ties them together is the thread that started with the women in that room. Knowing why you feel unsafe is one kind of change. Teaching your body that it is safe now is another, and it seems to need practice rather than persuasion.

[[lessons]]
$$**Insight and calm are different kinds of change.** Van der Kolk argues that understanding the cause of a fear doesn't switch off the body's alarm. The women in the study had tried other treatments without relief. Knowing why you react and reacting less turn out to be separate problems, and the second may need its own approach.$$

$$**Noticing your body safely is a skill.** The yoga classes centred on interoception, breathing and staying with sensations without being flooded. For people whose bodies had become a source of threat, that attention had to be built up gradually, like any other skill.$$

$$**Repetition did what a single realisation couldn't.** The improvement came from a weekly class, not a breakthrough moment. Recovery in this study looked less like finding the right words and more like returning, again and again, to a small practice.$$

[[reflect]]

- Where in your body do you tend to notice stress first?
- What do you usually do in the first minute after tension builds?

[[open]]
A peacock drags a tail of shimmering eyespots across the ground, a load that is bright, heavy and impossible to hide. A fox would see it from a long way off, and so would any hungry bird of prey. The peahen watching him seems to want exactly that burden.

[[story]]
Amotz Zahavi, an Israeli biologist born in 1928, looked at that tail and saw an argument, not a decoration. In 1975 he published a paper called "Mate selection: a selection for a handicap" in the *Journal of Theoretical Biology*. His idea, now called the handicap principle, is the kind of puzzle that Richard Dawkins's *The Selfish Gene* (1976) made popular: why would evolution favour a trait that seems to hurt its owner?

Zahavi's answer was that the cost is the point. Anyone can announce that they are strong. A signal is believable only if a weak individual could not afford to send it. A male that carries a large, conspicuous tail and still survives has shown, in the one language that cannot be faked, that he has strength to spare. The tail is honest because it is expensive. The principle predicts that the more a trait costs, the more reliable it is, which is why a trait that costs nothing could never be trusted this way.

The logic fits more than peacocks. It turns the question around: instead of asking why animals do not waste resources, ask what waste can prove. Zahavi also spent years studying the Arabian babbler, a social bird, and read its behaviour with the same lens, as signalling that has to be costly to be trusted.

Biologists did not accept this at once. John Maynard Smith was an early critic, and a 1976 analysis by Davis and O'Donald questioned Zahavi's model. In 1990 Alan Grafen produced a mathematical model in which a costly signal could be honest, because the cost is lower for higher-quality individuals, and that gave the idea a formal footing. Some later models showed that honest signals need not be costly, and in 2019 Penn and Számadó reported that they found no empirical evidence for selection favouring wasteful signals and proposed abandoning the principle.

Zahavi died in 2017, more than forty years after the paper, and the argument was still going. So the story has no clean ending. The idea is elegant, it has survived for decades, and it is still disputed. What remains useful is the question it teaches you to ask. When someone claims a quality, you can ask how much it cost them to say so, and whether a person without the quality could have said the same at the same price.

[[lessons]]
$$**Weigh a claim by what it cost the person to make it.** A job applicant who says they are quick learners has spent nothing, while one who arrives with a small working sample built over a weekend has paid in time and effort that someone less capable would struggle to match. Cheap claims are still worth hearing, but you can ask for something that costs a little more before you rely on them. The same test applies to your own words, and it is easy to run: a promise with some real effort behind it is more believable than a promise that costs you nothing.$$

A steel boss walks through his mill at lunchtime and finds a group of workers smoking directly under a large "No Smoking" sign. They see him. They know they're caught. What he does next is the opposite of what they expect, and it works better than any reprimand could.

[[story]]
Dale Carnegie tells this story in *How to Win Friends and Influence People*, in a section about how to change people's behavior without arousing resentment.

The boss was Charles Schwab, one of the most successful industrialists of his time. Walking through one of his steel mills one day at noon, he came upon some employees smoking. Right above their heads hung a sign that said **No Smoking**.

Schwab could have pointed at the sign and asked, sarcastically, whether they could read. He could have taken their names. Instead, he walked over to the men, took out some cigars and handed one to each of them.

> I'll appreciate it, boys, if you will smoke these on the outside.

##They knew they had broken a rule. And they admired him because he didn't say a word about it.##

Carnegie explains why this worked so well. Schwab didn't embarrass anyone. He didn't make them defend themselves. He called attention to the mistake indirectly, and gave them a small gift at the same time. The men got the message completely, and they went away liking their boss more, not less. It's hard to resent someone who treats you like that.

Carnegie gives a related example. The journalist Ida Tarbell once spent time with Owen D. Young, a prominent business leader. She said she never heard him give anyone a direct order. He made suggestions instead. He would say things like "You might consider this," or "Do you think that would work?" When he dictated a letter and then read it back, he might ask, "What do you think of this?" He let people do things themselves, and let them learn from their mistakes.

This, Carnegie argues, is one of the quietest and most effective ways to change behavior. Direct orders and criticism make people defensive. They trigger pride, and people dig in. A question, on the other hand, invites the other person to think. It lets them reach the right conclusion on their own. And people are far more committed to decisions they feel they made themselves.

He sums it up in two principles. **Call attention to people's mistakes indirectly.** And **ask questions instead of giving direct orders.**

The approach takes more patience than barking an instruction. But it produces something an order rarely does: cooperation that people actually mean.

[[lessons]]
$$**Indirect correction protects pride.** Schwab's workers knew exactly what they'd done wrong. By handing them cigars instead of pointing at the sign, he let them fix it without being humiliated. People who keep their dignity usually cooperate. People who lose it in front of others usually resent the person who took it.$$

$$**Questions let people find the answer.** Owen Young asked "Do you think that would work?" instead of giving orders. Questions invite thinking rather than resistance. Often the other person spots the problem themselves, and a problem you find yourself is much easier to accept than one someone points out to you.$$

$$**People keep the decisions they make.** Carnegie's point is that ownership drives commitment. When someone reaches a conclusion through their own thinking, they defend and follow it. When they're ordered, they comply at best, and often only while someone is watching.$$

[[reflect]]
- When did you last correct someone directly, and how did they react?
- Which instruction you give regularly could you turn into a question?
- How do you respond when someone corrects you gently, compared with bluntly?

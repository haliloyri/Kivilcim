In a small desert town in Arizona, a mother is annoyed by the local school district's dropout rate, and she hands her three sons a college-prep reading list of her own. One of them will read his way through a literature degree, a biology degree and a philosophy degree, and still end up in an operating room.

[[story]]
In *When Breath Becomes Air*, Paul Kalanithi describes how he got from that reading list to a scalpel. When he was ten, his father moved the family from a suburb north of New York City to Kingman, Arizona. His mother was unhappy that the local high-school dropout rate, as he recalls it, was somewhere north of thirty percent, so she gave her sons a reading list of her own. He came out of it with a love of books.

At Stanford he followed two threads at once. One was literature, where language seemed to him a near-supernatural force connecting people. The other was biology, which drew him in after a mediocre novel made him wonder how the brain produces the mind. He finished with degrees in English literature and human biology and a master's in English, and his thesis looked at how Walt Whitman tried to describe what he called the "Physiological-Spiritual Man." Underneath both ran one question: what makes human life meaningful?

On the advice of his professors he went to Cambridge for a degree in the history and philosophy of science and medicine. This is where the question turned into a decision. After a lot of theoretical discussion, he concluded that direct experience of life-and-death questions was needed before anyone could form serious moral opinions about them. In his words, moral speculation was puny compared to moral action. He chose medicine.

From Cambridge he went to Yale School of Medicine, where he graduated cum laude in 2007. He then returned to Stanford for a residency in neurological surgery and a research fellowship in neuroscience.

The sequence is easy to flatten into a neat story, so it helps to stay with the reasoning he gave: he saw that he could talk about meaning for years and still not stand where meaning is tested. A hospital was a place where the questions he had read about were happening to the people in the room.

Looking back, the move was a lateral one. He did not leave literature behind. He took it into a profession that is full of decisions about who lives and who does not, and he would later use it to write his own account of being on the other side of them.

[[lessons]]
$$**Take a question you have only been discussing to the place where it is actually decided, and spend some time there.** A person who keeps debating what good teaching looks like, or whether a family business is worth saving, can read and argue for years and end up with polished opinions that nothing has tested. Spending a few days in the classroom, or in the shop on a bad week, tests the opinions against what actually happens in practice. You come back with fewer claims to defend and a clearer sense of which ones you can honestly stand behind in the next debate.$$

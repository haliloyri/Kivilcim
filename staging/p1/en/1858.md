Brazil's official dietary guidelines give citizens a plain piece of advice: avoid ultra-processed products. The advice looks at how a food was made rather than at what it holds. Behind it stands a public health professor who asked about food in a different way from everyone before him.

[[story]]
Behind the term "ultra-processed" in Chris van Tulleken's *Ultra-Processed People* stands a Brazilian epidemiologist, Carlos Monteiro of the School of Public Health at the University of São Paulo. Knowing where the term came from helps explain why it is more than a fashionable word.

For a long time nutrition science judged food by what was inside it: how much fat, sugar, protein, vitamin. Monteiro asked something else. How much has this food been processed, and for what purpose? Around 2009 he coined the term "ultra-processed" and proposed a new way of sorting foods by their degree of processing.

That classification is known as NOVA, and it has four groups. Group one holds unprocessed and minimally processed foods. Group two holds processed culinary ingredients, such as the oil, salt and sugar a cook uses. Group three is processed foods, and group four is ultra-processed products, which sit at the far end of the scale.

The sorting has one feature that is easy to overlook. A label tells you what a product is made of, whereas the degree of processing tells you what road the product travelled to reach you. The two kinds of information do not always arrive together, because a number printed on a label says nothing about the production chain.

What sets this view apart is that it approaches a product through how it was made before it looks at what it contains. Two products with the same calories and sugar can fall into different groups. The same logic appeared in a ward experiment where the plates were matched on nutrients and the volunteers still ate different amounts.

Brazil did not leave the classification on the shelf. In 2014 the country's dietary guidelines were published with the advice to avoid ultra-processed products, and Monteiro coordinated them. One academic's question became the spine of a national recommendation.

Sources differ somewhat on the dates, so read the years as approximate. The order does not change: first a different question, then a classification, then a nationwide guide. Once the question changed, so did the place people looked, and the new place gave public health a tool it could hand to ordinary shoppers.

[[lessons]]
$$**To get to know a food, ask how it was made before you read what is in it.** Two products can carry similar labels while one is built from ingredients you know from a kitchen and the other has passed through a very different factory route. When you pick up a breakfast item, reading the first few ingredients and asking whether you could make something like it at home is a good place to begin. That lets you set the number comparison aside and decide for yourself which group a product belongs to, a decision that takes a few seconds in any shop.$$

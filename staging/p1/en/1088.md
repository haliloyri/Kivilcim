[[open]]
Picture a guy with a big idea around 1999: people should buy shoes on the internet. The obvious next step is money, meaning a warehouse, boxes of stock, and a shipping operation. He didn't take that step. He walked into a local shoe store with a camera and asked if he could take pictures of the shoes on their shelves. Those shoes weren't his.

[[story]]
This one comes from Eric Ries's *The Lean Startup*, and it's about a man named Nick Swinmurn.

Around 1999, Swinmurn wanted to sell shoes online. The whole idea rested on one question that nobody could answer for him:

> Will people actually buy shoes they can't try on?

That was the real risk. The web design, the logistics and the branding all came second. If customers wouldn't buy shoes without slipping them on first, nothing else he built would matter.

The conventional move would have been to build the business first and find out later. You raise money, lease a warehouse, stock inventory in every size, set up distribution, launch, and hope. If the answer turned out to be "no," you'd find out only after spending everything.

Swinmurn skipped all of that. He went to local shoe stores and asked if he could photograph their inventory. Then he put those photos up on a simple website. There was no warehouse and no stock. When an order came in, he would go back to the store, buy that pair at full retail price, and ship it to the customer himself.

Think about how odd that looks as a business. He bought at the same price anyone walking in off the street would pay. That isn't how you build a profitable shoe company, and he wasn't trying to build one yet. He was trying to answer the one question.

##And the answer came back: customers placed orders.##

Real people typed in real payment details for shoes they'd only seen in a photo. So there was genuine demand for buying shoes online. Swinmurn had evidence from what people did, which is stronger than anyone's opinion about what they might do.

Ries points out that this experiment told Swinmurn far more than a survey could have. A survey might have said people were "interested." Actually serving customers showed him how they behaved. He saw how they paid, and he saw what happened when shoes didn't fit and came back as returns. Those details only show up once you're dealing with actual customers and actual boxes.

Ries uses the story to illustrate what he calls validated learning. The idea is that you treat a startup as a series of experiments, each one built to test a key hypothesis, and you let the results decide what to build next.

The company was Zappos. It grew into a large business, and in 2009 Amazon acquired it in a deal valued at roughly $1.2 billion. It started with photos of someone else's shoes.

[[lessons]]
$$**Test the assumption that could sink everything.** Swinmurn's riskiest assumption was that people would buy shoes they couldn't try on. He didn't spend on warehouses or inventory before answering that. Photographing a store's shelves was cheap, fast, and aimed straight at the question that mattered most.$$

$$**Trust what people do over what they say.** The proof was the orders placed on a bare-bones website, not anyone's enthusiasm. A person handing over money for shoes they've only seen in a photo is evidence you can build on. Polite encouragement isn't.$$

$$**An experiment can be a bad business and still succeed.** Buying every pair at full retail price made no commercial sense, and it didn't need to. The goal was an answer, not a margin. By that measure it worked, and it also showed him how customers paid and how returns played out, which no plan or survey would have surfaced.$$

[[reflect]]
- In your current idea, what's the equivalent of "will people buy shoes they can't try on?" Which assumption, if wrong, would make everything else irrelevant?
- Is there something you're planning in detail that you could simply try by hand, at a small scale, the way Swinmurn fulfilled orders himself?
- What would count as real evidence for you, like an order, a payment, or a signup, rather than someone saying "sounds great"?
- Where are you holding a test to the standard of a finished business, when it only needs to answer one question?

&&What's the smallest, cheapest experiment you could run this week to learn whether your idea's core assumption holds?&&

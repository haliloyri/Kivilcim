Ask anyone to name a failure that turned out to be useful and they'll find one within seconds. A job they lost, a plan that collapsed, a rejection that pushed them somewhere better. Now ask how many of them said so while it was happening. That gap is where Shirzad Chamine wants you to work.

[[story]]
In *Positive Intelligence*, Shirzad Chamine describes two ways the mind can respond when something goes wrong.

The first belongs to what he calls the Saboteurs, the automatic patterns that hijack our thinking under pressure. They treat a setback as a threat. Someone must be blamed. Something must be feared. The mind fills with anxiety, regret or anger, and those feelings make it harder to think clearly about what to do next.

The second belongs to what he calls the **Sage**, the calmer, wiser part of the mind. The Sage starts from a very different assumption.

##Every outcome and circumstance, the Sage says, can be turned into a gift and an opportunity.##

Chamine is careful about what this does and doesn't mean. It isn't pretending the bad thing is good. It isn't the forced cheerfulness of "everything happens for a reason." The Sage fully accepts that something unwanted has happened. It just doesn't stop there. Once the loss is acknowledged, it asks a more useful question: *given that this happened, how can I make the most of it?*

He points out something most of us already know from experience. Looking back, we can usually find the gifts in past difficulties. The painful breakup taught us what we really wanted. The failed project exposed a weakness we fixed. The rejection sent us down a better road. Hindsight makes these gifts easy to see.

The Sage's aim is to see them earlier, while the difficulty is still happening, when it can still change how you feel and what you do.

To make that concrete, Chamine offers a simple exercise. When something bad happens, don't just react. Sit down and come up with at least three ways it could turn into a gift or an opportunity. They don't have to be certain. They just have to be possible.

At first this can feel strained, even a little absurd. But the act of looking changes the frame. A lost client might become the push to diversify. A harsh piece of feedback might be the most useful thing anyone has said to you this year. A cancelled plan might free up exactly the time you needed.

Chamine argues that this ability gets stronger with practice, and that as it does, both performance and wellbeing improve.

[[lessons]]
$$**Don't wait for hindsight.** Most of us find the gift in a hardship years later. Chamine's Sage looks for it while the difficulty is still unfolding. That's when it's most useful, because it can still shape your mood, your decisions and your next move, instead of just making a good story afterwards.$$

$$**Reframing starts with accepting.** The Sage perspective isn't denial. It begins by admitting that something unwanted happened. Only then does it ask how to make the most of it. Skipping the acceptance step turns reframing into fake positivity, which people can feel and rarely trust.$$

$$**Three gifts is a practical tool.** Asking yourself for three possible gifts or opportunities forces your mind off the threat and onto options. They don't need to be certain. The exercise works because searching for them breaks the Saboteurs' grip long enough for clearer thinking to return.$$

[[reflect]]
- Which past difficulty are you now genuinely glad happened?
- How long did it take you to see its gift, and what might have changed if you'd seen it sooner?
- What current problem have you only been treating as a threat?

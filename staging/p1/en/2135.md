[[open]]
A video of a talent-show audition ends. A small, sour restlessness settles in, the kind that was not there ten minutes ago, and it has a very simple cure. A thumb is already hovering over the next clip, and the next one is already loading.

[[story]]
Anna Lembke, a Stanford psychiatrist, explains the idea at the center of *Dopamine Nation* with a habit of her own: watching *American Idol* clips on YouTube. Each clip, she says, releases dopamine and tips the balance of the brain toward pleasure. Then comes the part people rarely connect to the clip. The brain adapts almost at once, turning down its dopamine signaling and reducing its dopamine receptors, and what follows is a comedown, something like a hangover, together with the urge to watch one more.

To describe this, she uses a picture of a seesaw. Pleasure and pain are handled in overlapping regions of the brain, and the brain's default is to return to level, which neuroscientists call homeostasis. Whenever the plank tips toward pleasure, little gremlins hop onto the pain side to bring it back.

The gremlins do not stop at level. They stay on until the pain side has dipped as far below as the pleasure side rose above. That is the comedown. If she waits, Lembke says, the gremlins eventually leave and the feeling passes.

If she keeps watching instead, more gremlins climb on, and they pile up until they fill a whole room. Her baseline for enjoying anything shifts. She now needs the clips just to feel normal, and when she stops, her brain protests with withdrawal: anxiety, irritability, insomnia, a flat and unhappy mood, and an intense craving to go back.

Her choice of example is telling. A talent-show clip is about as harmless as a pleasure gets, with no drug and no drink involved, and she still uses it to show the whole sequence from a rise in reward to a comedown to an urge for more.

Notice the timing, too. The gremlins arrive after the pleasure, at a moment when nothing seems wrong with the video itself and the only apparent fix is a bit more of it. The urge to keep watching comes from the same system that has just produced the enjoyment, so it feels like a wish for more of the good thing.

That is why waiting matters in her account. The step that lets the gremlins leave is the one that feels least natural: doing nothing at all for a while.

[[lessons]]
$$**When the urge for one more arrives, treat it as a wave that tends to leave if you give it time.** Picture someone who finishes an episode, feels a sour pull toward the next and a restless itch to keep going. Naming that pull as the comedown, rather than as a real wish for the next episode, lets you stand it for a few minutes without acting on it, and then check whether it has changed. In Lembke's own account, waiting is exactly what lets the gremlins go, and the price is a short stretch of discomfort, which you can spend on something small and ordinary.$$

The head of a young government agency signs an order that ends the use of a chemical across the country. In the same order he gives farmers more than six months before it takes effect. The date is not a loophole. It is a bridge, built so that the people who have to change can cross it.

[[story]]
DDT had been used on a vast scale. According to the US Environmental Protection Agency, about 675,000 tons were applied in the United States over thirty years, with use peaking in 1959 at nearly 80 million pounds. By 1971 it had fallen to about 13 million pounds, mostly on cotton. The EPA lists several reasons for the decline: insect resistance, better alternatives, environmental worries and government restrictions since 1969.

The decision itself came after three years of government inquiries. In January 1971, following a court order in a suit brought by the Environmental Defense Fund, EPA had issued notices of intent to cancel the remaining federal registrations of DDT products. According to the Environmental Law Reporter, the hearing examiner who reviewed the case then recommended that all the cancellations be withdrawn. William Ruckelshaus, the EPA Administrator, reviewed the case himself, overruled the examiner and, on 14 June 1972, ordered the cancellation of nearly all remaining DDT registrations.

He concluded that continued heavy use posed unacceptable risks to the environment and potential harm to human health. But the order was shaped with care. It did not take effect at once. The EPA delayed it until 31 December 1972 so that farmers and others could move to substitute pesticides in an orderly way, and the US Department of Agriculture helped develop a programme to teach farmers how to use the substitutes safely. Public health uses, quarantine uses, a few minor crop uses and exports were left outside the ban.

Manufacturers did not accept it quietly. They filed suits in the US Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia and in the federal district court for the Northern District of Mississippi. When EPA published its press release on 31 December 1972, neither court had ruled.

The result was a ban that arrived nearly ten years after *Silent Spring* appeared, aimed at a chemical the book had put under a spotlight. It was neither sudden nor total. It had a date at the end of the year, a list of exceptions and a training scheme attached.

Three decisions sit inside that order. The first was to act despite a recommendation to retreat. The second was to give users a long enough runway. The third was to keep exceptions narrow and named instead of leaving them to argument afterwards.

[[lessons]]
$$**When you make a hard decision, attach a runway to it: a date, a list of exceptions and help for the people who must change.** An order that stops something overnight pushes the cost onto those with the least room to adapt, and they will resist it. Setting an effective date months ahead, naming what stays allowed and offering practical training turns "no" into "not like this, but here is how". Use this when you retire a tool, change a policy or end an arrangement at home, and say the date and the exceptions in the same breath as the decision.$$

A hall in Philadelphia is full of men who have just become American citizens. The president steps up to speak, and every reporter in the room is waiting for one word. Three days earlier a liner went down with more than a hundred Americans on it. The president does not say its name.

[[story]]
Erik Larson's *Dead Wake* sets the Lusitania's crossing against the lives of the people ashore, and one of them is Woodrow Wilson. The publisher's description calls him a man lost to grief, dreading the widening war yet captivated by the prospect of new love. The facts behind that sentence are simple. His first wife had died the year before. In March 1915 his cousin Helen Bones introduced him at the White House to Edith Bolling Galt, a widow. They would marry on 18 December 1915 at her home in Washington, with about forty guests.

Then came 7 May, when the Lusitania was sunk and 128 Americans were among the dead. On 10 May Wilson spoke in Philadelphia's Convention Hall at a ceremony for newly naturalized citizens. The speech was titled "Americanism and the Foreign Born," and he did not mention the ship. But he said, "There is such a thing as a man being too proud to fight." Listeners took it as an oblique answer to the question of whether the country would go to war, and the phrase stuck to him.

What followed was slower and harder. Wilson sent diplomatic notes to Germany demanding reparations and an end to attacks that endangered Americans at sea. A much stronger second note, drafted with Robert Lansing, went out on 8 June, calling the sinking illegal and demanding that Germany give up submarine attacks on unarmed merchant ships. The next day Secretary of State William Jennings Bryan resigned in protest. Bryan felt Wilson was too ready to threaten force, and had also wanted a note to Britain about its blockade, which Wilson refused. Lansing took his place.

Wilson did not take the country to war that year. In September 1915 Germany limited its submarine attacks, and passenger liners were no longer to be attacked. It was only in January 1917, when Germany announced full unrestricted submarine warfare, that the road ran out, and the United States declared war on 6 April 1917. Larson has said in an interview that he thinks Wilson was doing the right thing for his country, and that most Americans did not want war after the sinking.

The episode is worth looking at because of what Wilson refused to do in public that week. Faced with a demand for a verdict, he gave a speech that answered nothing outright and named nothing, and then spent weeks on notes whose wording he could defend. That is a decision about when to speak. It cost him a resignation, and it also kept a door open.

[[lessons]]
$$**When everyone demands an answer now, give the part you can stand behind and keep the rest open.** Pressure makes a crisis feel as if it needs a verdict by evening, but many crises only need a first step you will not regret. In a customer complaint, a team conflict or a family argument, say what you know, name what you are checking and say when you will report back. Wilson's silence on the ship's name bought him time to choose his words, though it came at a price in goodwill. Time bought this way is worth having only if you use it to prepare the real answer.$$

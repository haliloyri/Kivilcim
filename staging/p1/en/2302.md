[[open]]
A young doctor is given a ward of children who have leukemia. In those years almost none of them survives, and the standard treatment, one drug at a time, buys only a short remission before the disease returns. The ward leaves one question open: what else is there to try?

[[story]]
Emil "Jay" Freireich joined the new National Cancer Institute in 1955, and the ward was his assignment. Published accounts agree on the basic picture: the children he looked after had acute lymphoblastic leukemia, and doctors could usually push it back with a single drug for a while but not defeat it. When the disease returned, the doctors were back where they had started.

Freireich had an idea that other researchers found hard to accept. If one drug fails because the cells adapt, he reasoned, use several at once, each attacking a different weakness. Around 1962 he proposed a four-drug combination of vincristine, methotrexate, 6-mercaptopurine and prednisone, later known by the initials VAMP. Gladwell discusses him in *David and Goliath*, and his refusal to accept the usual rules is what makes him a fit for a book about people who fight from a weaker position.

His colleagues did not welcome it. According to published accounts, the leukemia group initially refused to fund the proposal until their current trials were finished, and the trial was eventually run separately from the group's other work. Combining several strong drugs in sick children was a serious step, so the doubters were not simply being obstinate.

Combination chemotherapy changed the field. The approach that began in childhood leukemia spread to other cancers, and the regimen that came out of it led, in turn, to the MOPP combination for Hodgkin's disease. In 1972 Freireich and his colleague Emil Frei shared the Lasker Award. Freireich is now credited as a founder of combination chemotherapy for acute lymphoblastic leukemia. He died in 2021 at the age of 93.

Look at what he did in the face of institutional resistance. He did not wait for the group to agree. He insisted that the idea be tested, even though it meant a run of arguments and a trial set apart from the rest. The strength of the idea lay in its logic, and the willingness to fight for it was the other half. A single drug lets the disease adapt around it; several drugs at once leave far less room to do that, which is the reasoning behind the whole approach. It is also a plan that can be checked, because a trial gives an answer whoever believes in it.

[[lessons]]
$$**When you hold a well-reasoned idea that colleagues resist, make it cheap to test instead of trying to win the argument.** Freireich could not persuade the whole group, so his proposal was run as its own trial and the result did the persuading. At work, the same approach helps with a new process, a pricing idea or a way of running meetings: ask for a small, separate test with a clear measure, agreed in advance. People who refuse an opinion will often accept an experiment. And if the results are poor, you will have learned that before anyone else is exposed.$$

As you read this sentence, notice something: is there a voice in your head saying each word? For most people, there is. Jim Kwik argues that this quiet voice, along with a couple of other childhood habits, may be setting your reading speed limit.

[[story]]
In *Limitless*, Jim Kwik points out something we rarely think about. Most of us learned to read as small children, sounding out words, often aloud, one at a time. And then, for most people, reading technique simply stopped developing. We got more fluent, but we kept the same basic method we had at six or seven years old.

He identifies a few habits that slow us down.

The first is **subvocalization**: silently pronouncing each word in your head as you read. It's a leftover from reading aloud as a child. Kwik argues that if you "say" every word, even silently, your reading speed tends to stay close to your speaking speed.

The second is **regression**: the habit of letting your eyes jump back to reread words or lines you've already read. Often we do it not because we didn't understand, but out of habit or a moment of lost focus. Each jump costs time and breaks the flow.

##Kwik's main remedy is surprisingly simple: use your finger.##

He recommends using a **visual pacer**, a finger, a pen or a card, moving smoothly under each line as you read. Children are often told to stop pointing at words, but Kwik argues it actually helps. The eyes naturally follow movement, so a pacer guides them steadily forward, reduces regression and helps maintain focus. He suggests practicing with the pacer moving slightly faster than feels comfortable, pushing your eyes to keep up, and then checking how much you understood.

Many people who try this notice they read more smoothly and stay focused longer.

It's worth adding a note of balance. Reading researchers have questioned some of the bigger claims made for speed reading generally. Their studies suggest that very large jumps in speed usually come at a cost to comprehension, and that some inner voice is a normal part of understanding what we read, especially complex material. In other words, faster isn't always better.

A sensible way to use Kwik's ideas, then, is not to race through everything, but to update habits that no longer serve you: reduce needless rereading, use a pacer to stay focused, and match your speed to the material. Skim what deserves skimming. Slow down for what deserves depth. And always check that you've actually understood.

[[lessons]]
$$**Reading habits can be updated.** Most people still read with the basic method they learned as young children. Kwik's point is that reading is a skill, and skills can be improved. Simply noticing how you read, your inner voice and your eye movements, is the first step toward reading better.$$

$$**A pacer keeps your eyes moving.** Moving a finger or pen under the line guides your eyes, reduces unnecessary rereading and helps you stay focused. It's a small, almost childish-seeming tool, yet many people find it makes reading smoother and less tiring, especially with long or dry material.$$

$$**Balance speed with understanding.** Research suggests big speed gains often cost comprehension, and some inner speech helps understanding. The goal isn't to read everything as fast as possible. It's to choose the right speed for each text and to check, honestly, that you've understood what you read.$$

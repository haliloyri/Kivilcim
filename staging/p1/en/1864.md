Near the Bavarian town of Landsberg lay a complex of eleven camps, and the question of who reached it first has no single answer. The dates drift across several days, and two units each have a claim. In a place like that, the most honest work is to write what is known and what is not, side by side and in plain view.

[[story]]
Stephen E. Ambrose's 1992 book *Band of Brothers* follows Easy Company, part of the 506th Parachute Infantry Regiment of the 101st Airborne Division. In late April 1945, in the last weeks of the war, the division came to a place near the town of Landsberg and the village of Hurlach in Bavaria. It was part of the Kaufering camp complex.

Kaufering was a group of sub-camps of Dachau, eleven camps in all. According to the sources, soldiers of the 12th Armored Division found one of them on 27 April. The 101st arrived on 28 April. The dates vary between 27 and 29 April depending on the source, so no single day is settled.

At Camp IV, before leaving, the SS burned the barracks, and hundreds of prisoners were killed. That sentence is heavy enough that it needs no further detail beside it. The sources do not agree on one count of the dead, and to print a number here would be to claim knowledge we do not have. Nor do we describe what the soldiers saw, because we have no solid record of it for this account.

Then there is the question of who liberated the place. The Airborne Museum credits the 506th Parachute Infantry Regiment with the liberation of Landsberg. Other accounts put the 12th Armored's soldiers in front. The credit is contested, and both sides point to something real, so the page records the dispute itself.

This tangle need not be read as a flaw. On the same days, in places close together, different units could have entered different camps, and that may explain why the records do not fit neatly. What matters is being able to say what each account rests on. A camp's name, a range of dates and which unit appears in which source make up the skeleton of what is certain.

In this story, bearing witness is a duty, and the duty includes writing the certain as certain and the uncertain as uncertain. Eleven camps, the link to Dachau, the SS burning the barracks and hundreds dead belong on the page. Who arrived first stays open until the sources agree. The value of an accurate record lies less in how much it says than in having a source under everything it does say.

[[lessons]]
$$**Write what you know and mark where your sources differ.** Presenting the certain as certain and the uncertain as uncertain protects an account from exaggeration and from silence alike. A person writing up a workplace accident who records what they saw and what they only heard on two separate lines lets every later reader see where to place their trust. That care lets you keep an honest, usable record even on a contested subject. The report gets a little shorter and much sturdier, and whoever reads it after you understands why you left a gap.$$

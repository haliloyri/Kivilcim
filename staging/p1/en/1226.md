A young man, frustrated and unhappy, visits an old philosopher to argue with him. He lists his problems: work, money, relationships, himself. The philosopher listens, then offers an answer the young man finds almost insulting in its simplicity. The trouble, he says, lies in a wish most of us share: the wish to be liked.

[[story]]
*The Courage to Be Disliked*, by Ichiro Kishimi and Fumitake Koga, is written as a conversation. Over five nights, a skeptical young man debates a philosopher who teaches the ideas of the psychologist Alfred Adler. The young man arrives convinced that happiness depends on circumstances. The philosopher gently takes that belief apart.

One of his first claims is sweeping: all problems are interpersonal relationship problems. Even worries that seem private, about our looks, our status, our achievements, are really about how we stand in relation to other people.

From there, he points to a trap. Many of us want recognition. We want others to approve of us, praise us, think well of us. It seems harmless, even natural. But the philosopher argues that if you live seeking recognition, you end up living according to other people's expectations. You shape your choices around what will please them. And then you're no longer living your own life.

##To escape this trap, the philosopher offers a tool: the separation of tasks.##

For any situation, he says, ask a simple question: whose task is this? The way to tell is to ask who will ultimately bear the consequences of the choice. If you decide how to spend your career, that's your task. How your parents feel about it is their task. If you work hard on a presentation, that's your task. Whether your boss likes it is your boss's task.

The principle is to take care of your own tasks fully, and not to intrude on other people's, or let them intrude on yours. You can't control what others think of you. Trying to is like trying to live their part of the relationship for them.

This leads to the idea that gives the book its title. The philosopher tells the young man that freedom is being disliked by other people. He doesn't mean you should try to be disliked, or act without care for others. He means that as long as you need everyone to like you, you can never be free. Some people will disapprove of any honest choice you make. Accepting that, having the courage to be disliked, is the price of living as yourself.

The young man resists this for a long time. It's not an easy idea to accept.

[[lessons]]
$$**Seeking approval means living someone else's life.** The philosopher argues that constantly seeking recognition ties your choices to other people's expectations. It feels like harmless wanting to be liked. In practice, it can quietly hand control of your life to whoever you most want to impress.$$

$$**Separate your tasks from theirs.** Asking "whose task is this?" and "who bears the consequences?" clears up a lot of anxiety. Your effort and choices are your task. Others' opinions and reactions are theirs. You can do your part well without trying to manage feelings that aren't yours to manage.$$

$$**Freedom includes being disliked.** Not everyone will approve of your honest choices. The courage to be disliked isn't about being careless or cruel. It's accepting that some disapproval is the cost of living as yourself, and deciding that cost is worth paying. It gets easier with practice.$$

A man arrived in a small Arkansas town with his family and, barely a week later, owned a variety store on its square. He held a one-day remodeling sale, hung his own name over the door, and set the shelves so that customers walked up and took things themselves.

[[story]]
Sam Walton's 1992 memoir *Made in America*, written with John Huey, covers what he did after Newport took his first store away. On 1 May 1950 he moved to Bentonville, Arkansas, a town less than half the size of Newport. By 9 May, with his father-in-law's help, he had bought the Harrison Variety store.

He opened it after a one-day remodeling sale and called it Walton's 5 & 10. According to the Encyclopedia of Arkansas, it was the third self-service variety store in the country. The store ran to about 4,000 square feet, large for a town that size, and had a single checkout at the front.

Self-service sounds ordinary now, and in 1950 it was not. In a traditional variety store the clerk stood behind the counter and brought each item to the customer. In a self-service store the customer walked the aisles, picked things off the shelves and paid once, at the front. The counter stopped being a wall between buyer and goods.

That detail is worth keeping in view. Walton had just lost a store over a clause he could not change. In Bentonville he could still choose how the shop worked, and he chose a format that, by that Encyclopedia's count, only two other variety stores in the whole country were using. He did not copy the way his old store had run; he put a newer idea inside a store with his own name over the door.

Bentonville had its own pull for him. According to published summaries of the book, it was closer to his wife Helen's family, and it offered quail hunting, one of his lifelong interests. Two years later, in 1952, he added a Ben Franklin store in nearby Fayetteville, so Bentonville was the start of something, not a one-off.

Notice what carried over from Newport and what did not. In Newport he had watched a variety store grow to $250,000 in annual sales, and Bentonville was less than half that town's size, so the figures he carried in his head were an argument for what a small-town shop could do. The craft carried over: buying, pricing, stocking, knowing how a variety store makes money. The method did not. Self-service meant the customer's own hands did the fetching that a clerk used to do.

[[lessons]]
$$**When a setback hands you a clean sheet, spend it on the newest idea that fits what you already know how to do.** You do not have to rebuild the old thing smaller, or prove that the old plan was right. Keep the craft you have earned, such as pricing, stocking or knowing your customers, and change the one part of the method that you could never justify changing while the old setup was still running. A forced restart removes the habits that would have kept you doing it the old way, and an experiment costs the least on day one.$$

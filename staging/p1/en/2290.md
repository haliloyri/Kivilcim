A man at an office desk presses the telephone to his ear and cups his free hand around the mouthpiece. He drops his voice so the person at the next desk cannot hear. Nobody has ever banned the hand. A small cup that does the same job, and leaves the hand free, was for years in a telephone company's sights.

[[story]]
In Tim Wu's *The Master Switch*, AT&T's wish to control whatever touched its telephone network is a recurring theme, and the Hush-A-Phone was an early, small test of it. The Hush-A-Phone was a cup-shaped attachment that fitted over the mouthpiece. It kept a speaker's voice from reaching the people nearby, and it made the sound a little clearer for the person listening. According to published accounts, it had been sold for about twenty years without trouble.

AT&T treated it as a "foreign attachment," a device the company had not supplied and had not approved for its network. The dispute went to the Federal Communications Commission, and in 1951 the commission sided with AT&T. Its reasoning was that unrestricted use could damage the quality of telephone service.

The argument looked reasonable at first glance, because the owner of a network answers for the quality of its service. What stood out was that it asked for no proof. The rule did not check whether a device did any harm. It checked whether the device had come from the company.

Hush-A-Phone appealed to the federal appeals court in Washington, D.C. The case was argued on 4 October 1956, and Judge Bazelon wrote the decision, issued on 8 November 1956.

The court's logic was plain. By the commission's own finding, the device caused only a negligible loss of intelligibility when it was not pressed against the face. The telephone companies suggested a different route to privacy: the subscriber could cup a hand between the mouthpiece and his mouth and speak softly. The court found it unreasonable to allow the hand and ban a device that left the hand free. The device did no physical damage to the company's equipment. It changed only the user's own conversation.

The court set aside the commission's order and sent the case back. In summary, the holding was that the tariffs interfered unreasonably with a subscriber's right to use his telephone in ways that were privately beneficial without being publicly harmful. On 6 February 1957 the commission directed AT&T and the Bell companies to let subscribers use the Hush-A-Phone and similar devices.

The ruling did not deny AT&T the right to protect its network. It insisted that protection rest on an actual harm, and it set that claimed harm beside a habit the company already tolerated. The question moved from "did this come from us?" to "does it really hurt anyone?"

[[lessons]]
$$**Set the stated reason for a ban beside the nearest thing that is already allowed.** When someone blocks a small improvement of yours by citing the need for protection, the word "harm" often stays vague, and a vague harm is hard to argue with. Ask what similar behaviour is permitted today, and the side that cannot name a difference has to explain its position. At work, when a person rejects a new tool or routine, reminding them of the comparable method already in use turns the objection into a concrete question and keeps the talk calm. It also gives the other person an easy way to change their mind without losing face.$$

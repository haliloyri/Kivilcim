[[open]]
Imagine you could ask someone who spent a career inside one of the world's most effective organizations what its secret is. You'd expect something big: a brutal selection process, a legendary training program, a doctrine refined over generations. Now imagine the answer is about lunch. It isn't a metaphor for lunch. It's actual lunch, and the question of who gets fed first.

[[story]]
In *Leaders Eat Last*, Simon Sinek tells of a conversation with retired Lieutenant General George Flynn of the US Marine Corps. Sinek's question, in essence, was simple: what makes the Marines so effective?

It's the kind of question that invites a grand answer. The Marines have a reputation for toughness, cohesion and performance under pressure. Surely the explanation would be something you could put in a brochure: how they select recruits, how hard they train, some hard-won method passed down from officer to officer. Sinek was looking for the big secret.

Flynn pointed him somewhere much smaller. His answer came down to three words:

> Officers eat last.

Picture the mess line at mealtime. The most junior Marines step up and get served first. Rank by rank, the line moves up the hierarchy, and the most senior people in the room are served last. If the food runs short, it runs short for them.

##No one orders this, and no regulation requires it. It is simply how Marines do things.##

That detail is what makes the custom so striking. A rule can be enforced from above and followed grudgingly. A habit that nobody has to enforce is different. It tells you what people inside the organization already believe about themselves. In this case the belief is that the people with the most authority are the ones responsible for everyone else. Being in charge doesn't earn you the first plate.

Sinek builds his argument from there. Leadership, he says, is a responsibility, not a rank. It means giving up privileges and comfort for the sake of the people in your care. The lunch line is a daily, visible reminder of that deal.

He ties it to an idea he calls the **Circle of Safety**. When people feel safe inside a group, they spend their energy facing outward, on the real threats and challenges the group exists to meet. When they don't feel safe, that energy turns inward. They protect themselves from each other. They hide mistakes. They shift blame.

Sinek is careful about one point. People in an unsafe culture aren't lazy or malicious. They often work very hard. The trouble is where the effort goes. Much of it is spent on self-protection inside the organization, not on the work the organization is supposed to be doing.

So the answer Sinek got wasn't a program at all. It was an unwritten habit about who gets fed first. Behind that habit sits a whole theory of why some groups turn their strength outward while others spend it guarding themselves from one another.

[[lessons]]
$$**Rank collects, responsibility gives away.** "Officers eat last" works because it flips the usual logic of seniority. If leadership is a rank, it gathers privilege: the best seat, the first plate, the least exposure. If leadership is a responsibility, it hands those things back. You eat last, you leave last, and you answer first when something goes wrong. The Marine mess line turns that choice into something anyone can see, three times a day.$$

$$**Unwritten habits tell the truth.** The most revealing part of Flynn's answer is that no regulation requires the custom. Written policies say what an organization wants to be seen as. Habits that everyone follows without being told show what it actually is. If you want to understand a group, don't read its mission statement. Watch what happens at the lunch line, in the meeting after a failure, or at the moment when there isn't enough of something to go around.$$

$$**Safety decides where the energy goes.** The Circle of Safety explains why the custom matters beyond good manners. When people trust that leaders will protect them, their effort goes outward, toward real challenges. When they don't, that same hard work turns inward into hiding mistakes and shifting blame. Leaders build that safety through concrete acts. They take the blame outwardly, protect whoever brings bad news, and stay predictable when pressure rises.$$

[[reflect]]
- On your team, who gets the comfort first, and who absorbs the cost when things go badly?
- Think of one unwritten habit where you work, maybe who speaks first, who stays late, or who gets credited. What does it say about your values that no poster on the wall would admit?
- Look at last week honestly. How much of your team's effort went toward the outside challenge, and how much went into covering itself internally?
- If you lead anyone, what is your version of eating last, and do people actually see you do it?

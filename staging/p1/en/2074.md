[[open]]
A crew lays glass fibre in a trench, mile after mile, through five states. On the map the route looks drawn with a ruler. It bends only where a lake, a highway or a row of houses forces it to. Nobody out there is trying to carry more data, only to shave a few thousandths of a second off a distance.

[[story]]
Michael Lewis's *Flash Boys* (2014) is about high-speed trading, and this story is about a cable built for it. The idea is credited to Dan Spivey. According to published accounts it came to him in 2007, while he was working with a New York hedge fund on a low-latency arbitrage strategy.

The company built around that idea was Spread Networks, backed by James Barksdale, the former Netscape chief, and run by his son David. Its plan was a dark-fibre line between Chicago and Carteret, New Jersey, roughly 825 miles long and drawn as straight as lakes, highways and houses would allow. Rights of way had to be secured in five states. Spread was approached about two and a half years before it announced the line, and much of that time went on those rights of way and on laying the fibre. According to published accounts, the build was kept quiet so that competitors would not learn of it.

When the line was unveiled in June 2010, Spread advertised a round trip of 13.33 milliseconds. The shortest times Light Reading found from competitors were 15.9 and 17.1 milliseconds. The company never disclosed the price of the build, and press accounts later put it at about $300 million. Its customers were mainly high-frequency trading firms, who were paying for a lead of a couple of milliseconds. Against the nearest rival, the advertised gap came to about two and a half.

That lead did not stay private for long. In October 2012 Spread trimmed its own estimate from 13.1 to 12.98 milliseconds. According to Wired, microwave links through the air were by then reaching roughly 8.2 to 8.5 milliseconds, close to the physical minimum of about 8. In November 2017 Zayo agreed to buy Spread for $127 million in cash.

Look at what the glass line actually improved. It did not make the signal travel faster, because light in a fibre goes at the speed it goes. The builders took out the bends instead. Everyone before them had treated the route as a given, and Spivey treated it as the thing to change.

A straight line of glass looks like the end of the road until someone notices that the air above it is a shorter road still.

[[lessons]]
$$**Look for the detours in a route before you work on the speed of each step.** A small shop that keeps buying faster equipment may lose most of its days to the walk between the counter, the storeroom and the owner's desk, where every order waits for a signature. Drawing the path your work actually takes, with a mark at every place it stops or turns, often shows a saving that no amount of hurrying would find. It also tells you which delays are really yours to remove and which belong to someone else's schedule. A single page and one note beside the longest wait are enough for a first step.$$

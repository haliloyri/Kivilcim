In a photograph from the days around the unveiling, two women stand beside a wall of cables and switches. The caption names the engineers who designed the machine. It leaves out the women, and it leaves out the six people who had worked out how to make the machine do anything at all.

[[story]]
Before ENIAC, the Army's firing tables were worked out by hand at the University of Pennsylvania's Moore School. During the Second World War, more than eighty women there worked as "computers", calculating the paths of shells with desk calculators. When the engineers finished the giant electronic machine, six of those women were picked to make it run: Kay McNulty, Jean Jennings, Betty Snyder, Marlyn Wescoff, Frances Bilas and Ruth Lichterman. Walter Isaacson's *The Innovators* tells their story.

There was no manual, because nothing like ENIAC had ever existed. At first the Army would not give the women security clearance to see the machine at all. They worked from blueprints and wiring diagrams and went to the engineers with questions. Programming meant plugging cables and setting rows of switches, and they worked out for themselves what each connection did.

The test chosen for the public showing in February 1946 was a ballistic trajectory, the very job they had once done by hand. Betty Snyder, later Betty Holberton, and Jean Jennings, later Bartik, wrote that program. According to published accounts, a trajectory that would have taken a person about forty hours took ENIAC around twenty seconds. Bartik remembered it as calculating the trajectory faster than it took the shell to travel.

The audience saw the speed on the machine, and the praise went to John Mauchly and J. Presper Eckert, who had designed the machine. The women appear in the photographs from the period, but the captions did not name them. By one account none of them was invited to the celebration dinner afterward. Years later some people looked at those pictures and assumed the women had been hired as models.

Nothing in the demonstration was faked, and nothing about it was easy. The machine did calculate the shell's flight, and it did so because six people had learned its wiring from drawings and then wrote a program that ran in front of the public. That is the part that stayed off the page, and the result everybody applauded depended on a layer of work that looked, from the outside, like a person standing next to a machine and plugging in a cable.

[[lessons]]
$$**Name the people behind a result while the applause is still going.** The ENIAC demonstration put a machine on display and left its programmers out of the caption, so for years the work looked like the machine's own. If your team ships a smooth launch, the quiet coordinator who wrote the schedule and the colleague who tested it at night are the ones who most often vanish from the thank-you email. Say their names and what they did in the first message and in front of the people who decide about promotions, because credit that waits until later rarely arrives at all.$$

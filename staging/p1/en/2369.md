A new engineer sits down at a desk that has already been set up for them, on the very first morning of a new job. The first email in the inbox is from the chief executive of the company, and it tells the engineer about a plow.

[[story]]
John Deere is a company that makes farm machinery, and Chip Heath has used its way of welcoming new hires as an example of how to design a first day. He described it in a 2018 interview with Knowledge at Wharton and again in a talk at Stanford, so the details below come from the author's own telling.

According to Heath, the first day begins before the new engineer arrives. A colleague with whom the newcomer has already been in touch is there to greet them, bringing a favorite drink. The workspace is ready and carries the new person's name. In the Stanford talk, he described a banner strung over the cubicle area so that colleagues can see that new people have come in, and a lunch with coworkers.

Then comes the email. The first message in the inbox is from the head of the company, and it includes a short video. It tells part of the company's history and links it to a patented plow from the firm's earliest days. In the interview, Heath also mentioned a model of that plow sitting on the new employee's desk. The accounts differ slightly in detail, but they agree on the main idea: the company chose to meet a newcomer with a story, not a stack of forms.

Think about what that does. A new job begins with doubt. The person does not yet know whether they belong, whether anyone is expecting them, or whether the organization has anything to say beyond a job description. A greeting from a friendly face answers the first two questions in minutes. The note from the chief executive answers the third by placing the newcomer inside a history far older than they are.

Heath's point is that a first day happens once. If nobody designs it, it becomes whatever the schedule produces: forms, passwords and a tour of the building. If someone designs it, the same eight hours can leave a person feeling expected, which is a very different start.

None of the elements is expensive. A drink, a banner, a prepared desk, a short video. What they share is that someone thought about the newcomer's morning in advance.

[[lessons]]
$$**Design the first day of a newcomer in advance, because it happens only once and sets the tone for everything after it.** New people arrive unsure whether they belong, and small, planned gestures answer that question faster than any policy. Before your next hire, volunteer or guest arrives, decide who will greet them, what will be ready for them, and what one sentence tells them why their work matters. None of this takes much money, only a little forethought and someone willing to own it. The newcomer will remember the welcome long after they forget the orientation slides, and will repeat it to friends.$$

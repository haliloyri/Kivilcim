[[story]]
Paul Kalanithi, the neurosurgeon who wrote *When Breath Becomes Air*, spent years on the other side of this conversation. When patients asked him how long they had, he gave them ranges, such as days to a few weeks, or weeks to a few months, and he kept his answers honest but vague. He avoided detailed statistics. He also told patients not to go looking for survival numbers online.

Then he became the patient, with metastatic lung cancer, diagnosed in May 2013. At his first visit with his oncologist, Emma, he asked to see the Kaplan-Meier curves for his type of cancer, the graphs that show what share of patients are still alive as time passes. She put him off. She said the first step was to work out his values, whatever the timeline turned out to be.

In his essay "How Long Have I Got Left?", published in *The New York Times* in January 2014, Kalanithi wrote about the odd position this put him in. He wanted the number his patients had always wanted, and the doctor in front of him always avoided being pinned to one. He asked why physicians hold back when they know so much. And he gave his own earlier answer. A curve, he wrote, cannot tell an individual patient where she stands on it. The curves describe a whole group of people, and no curve says which of them you will turn out to be.

What he wanted from a number was a way to plan. Without it, the way forward was unclear, so he spelled out his two versions of the future in one sentence: if he had two years, he would write, and if he had ten, he would go back to surgery and science.

Nobody could give him the number. He went back to the operating room for a time, and he wrote the book. Counting from the diagnosis in May 2013 to his death on 9 March 2015, he lived about twenty-two months.

The two answers he proposed are worth a second look. Each was a plan that began only after he supposed a length of time, and the oncologist's refusal kept pushing him back to the question that had to be answered first, which was what he valued most.

[[lessons]]
$$**When you cannot get the number, decide what you would do under each plausible number, and begin with what holds in all of them.** Someone waiting on a layoff decision, a loan approval or a doctor's call tends to freeze until the figure arrives. Sketching two or three versions of the next stretch, and writing down what you would do in each, shows you the steps that appear in every version, so you can start those today. You stop waiting for certainty and still make progress on what matters, and the next conversation about the number goes better for it.$$

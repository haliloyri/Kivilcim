"Find your passion." It's one of the most common pieces of advice given to young people. It sounds encouraging. But Angela Duckworth thinks it quietly sets people up to feel like failures, because it suggests passion is lying somewhere, fully formed, waiting to be dug up.

[[story]]
In *Grit*, Angela Duckworth spends a lot of time on the "passion" half of her definition, passion and perseverance for long-term goals. And she found that passion rarely arrives the way people expect.

Many people picture a moment of discovery. You try something, a light goes on, and you know this is what you were meant to do. Some people do have stories like that. But many of the gritty high achievers Duckworth interviewed described something much slower and messier. They spent years trying different things. They followed curiosities that faded and others that didn't. Only gradually did one interest pull them in deeper than the rest.

##Passion, in Duckworth's view, is usually not discovered. It's developed.##

She describes how it tends to begin. Interests are triggered by interaction with the outside world, often by chance: a class, a book, a conversation, a job you took for other reasons. At first, that interest is fragile. It needs time, encouragement and repeated contact before it grows into anything more. That's why telling people to "find" their passion can backfire. They wait for certainty, feel nothing that dramatic, and conclude something is wrong with them.

Duckworth goes further. She describes four psychological assets that people with grit tend to develop over time, roughly in order.

The first is **interest**. You genuinely enjoy what you do, or at least find it intriguing.

The second is **practice**. You develop the discipline to work at getting better, day after day, including on the parts that aren't fun.

The third is **purpose**. You come to believe your work matters, not just to you, but to other people.

The fourth is **hope**. You believe you can keep going and improve, even when things get hard.

The important thing is that these build on each other. Very few people start with purpose. They start with a small interest, stick with it long enough to practice, and discover the purpose later. Many people stop at the first stage, waiting for interest to feel like destiny. Others abandon an early interest the first time practice gets boring.

The passion people admire in others is usually the end of that long process, not the start of it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Passion is grown, not found.** Duckworth's high achievers mostly didn't have a single moment of revelation. They had interests that were triggered, often by chance, and then deepened over years. If you're waiting for a lightning bolt, you may be overlooking a small interest that could become something much bigger with time.$$

$$**Early interests are fragile.** A new curiosity needs time and encouragement before it can take hold. Dropping it because it doesn't immediately feel like a calling is like pulling up a seedling for not being a tree. Give it contact and repetition before you judge it.$$

$$**The stages build on each other.** Interest, practice, purpose and hope tend to develop in order. Purpose usually comes later, after you've practiced long enough to see how your work helps others. Knowing that makes it easier to keep going through the practice stage, where many people give up.$$

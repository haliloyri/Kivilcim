Listen to how people describe the things they didn't want to do but did anyway. "I couldn't say no." "I had no choice." "I should have gone." Susan Jeffers paid close attention to sentences like these. She believed the words themselves were part of the trap.

[[story]]
In *Feel the Fear and Do It Anyway*, Susan Jeffers argues that the way we talk to ourselves shapes how powerful or powerless we feel. Certain words, used again and again, quietly tell us that we're victims of circumstance. Others remind us that we're the ones making choices.

She calls the first set a **vocabulary of pain** and the second a **vocabulary of power**, and she gives concrete pairs to swap.

The first, and perhaps the most important, is **"I can't" versus "I won't."**

Think about how often "I can't" is used. "I can't say no to my boss." "I can't go to the gym tonight." "I can't turn down my mother." Very often, what's really meant is *I won't*: I'm choosing not to, perhaps because saying no feels uncomfortable or risky. "I can't" hides the choice. It makes the situation sound like something that happened to you.

##"I won't" hands the choice back to you, where it belonged all along.##

That can feel uncomfortable at first. It means taking responsibility. But it also means you have power. If you *won't*, you could decide differently. If you *can't*, you're stuck.

Jeffers offers other pairs. Replace **"I should"** with **"I could."** "Should" carries guilt and pressure; it implies you're failing if you don't. "Could" turns the same thing into an option you're free to take or leave. Replace **"It's not my fault"** with **"I'm totally responsible,"** not to blame yourself, but to reclaim the ability to change your situation. Replace **"It's a problem"** with **"It's an opportunity."** And replace **"Life's a struggle"** with **"Life's an adventure."**

These shifts apply especially to saying no. Many people who struggle to refuse requests describe it as something outside their control: they "just couldn't." Jeffers's language invites a more honest version. You *could* say no. You've been choosing yes, perhaps out of fear of disappointing someone, or of being disliked.

Seen that way, a no stops being a rude impossibility. It becomes one of two options you're entitled to choose between. And the yeses you do give become real choices too, rather than things squeezed out of you by fear.

Jeffers doesn't claim that changing words changes everything overnight. But she argues that the language you use every day slowly trains how you see yourself: as someone things happen to, or as someone who chooses.

[[lessons]]
$$**"I can't" often hides "I won't."** Many things we say we can't do are things we're choosing not to do, usually to avoid discomfort. Jeffers's swap makes the choice visible. It can feel less comfortable at first, but it also reminds you that you have power in the situation, and that you could choose differently.$$

$$**"Should" brings guilt; "could" brings options.** A list of shoulds feels like a list of failures waiting to happen. Rewritten as coulds, the same list becomes a menu of possibilities. That small change in wording often lowers pressure enough for you to actually choose, and do, the things that matter most.$$

$$**Owning your choices gives you back power.** "I'm totally responsible" isn't about blaming yourself. It's about seeing that your choices shape your situation, which means different choices could change it. For people who struggle to say no, that's the first step toward a no they can say calmly.$$

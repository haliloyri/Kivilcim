At a border crossing, dogs circle a car. In the boot, under blankets, lies the man they are looking for. Inside the car is a baby, and the baby's dirty nappy is enough to throw the dogs off the scent.

[[story]]
Ben Macintyre's *The Spy and the Traitor* is about Oleg Gordievsky, a Soviet intelligence officer who spied for Britain. This story is about his escape from the Soviet Union, on 19 July 1985. By then the KGB was suspicious of him and watching him, and every day he stayed in the country was more dangerous than the one before.

British intelligence, MI6, had kept an escape plan ready for years. It was called Pimlico. A few days earlier the plan had been put before Prime Minister Margaret Thatcher, and she had approved it. Gordievsky slipped away from those watching him and took a train to Leningrad. From there he made his way to a rendezvous, agreed in advance, south of Vyborg.

Two British diplomats and their wives were waiting for him. He hid in the boot of a Ford Sierra. One of the couples had a baby daughter, and she was in the car too. As the car approached the Finnish border, Soviet guards began searching vehicles with dogs. The dogs circled the car. At that moment the baby's dirty nappy distracted them.

The car crossed the border. Gordievsky was later flown to Britain.

What stands out is how plain the plan was. There was no secret tunnel, no forged identity, no elaborate operation. There was a train ticket, a car boot and a meeting time rehearsed for years. Nothing was left to luck, but nothing was made cleverer than it had to be. The one unexpected element, the dogs and a baby's nappy, came from outside the plan, and the plan survived it because it was simple enough to survive a surprise.

There is a second thing to see. A plan this plain can be explained to everyone involved in a minute, and each of them can carry out their part without improvising. The drivers did not need to be spies. They needed to be on time, calm and ready to do exactly what had been agreed.

[[lessons]]
$$**Do a hard job with a plain plan prepared years ahead and rehearsed many times, not with a clever but fragile one.** On a hard day, whether it is a launch, a lost customer or a system outage, what works is usually what is simple and already tested. In a complicated plan every extra step is another point of failure. Ask of each step in your plan: what happens if this one breaks, and who else knows what to do then? When something unexpected turns up, at your own "dirty nappy" moment, the thing still standing is the thing that saves you.$$

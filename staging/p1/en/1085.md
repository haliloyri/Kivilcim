[[open]]
Picture two teams racing for the same sky in the same year. One has government money, a famous institution behind it and reporters watching every move. The other has a bicycle shop in Ohio and a belief it can't let go of. If you'd had to bet your own money in 1903, you'd have known which team to back. And you'd have lost.

[[story]]
Around 1903, several people in America were trying to build the first powered, piloted airplane. In *Start With Why*, Simon Sinek focuses on two of them: Samuel Pierpont Langley, and the brothers Wilbur and Orville Wright.

On paper, this wasn't a fair fight. Langley was a senior officer at the Smithsonian Institution, a prominent scientist with serious connections. The U.S. War Department gave him $50,000 to build his flying machine. He had money, a strong team and the press following his progress. If anyone looked set to make history, it was him.

The Wrights had none of that. They ran a bicycle shop in Dayton, Ohio. Neither brother had a college degree. They had no outside funding and no newspapers tracking them. They paid for their experiments with what the shop brought in.

What they did have, Sinek argues, was a reason. The people around them weren't there for a paycheck or a headline. They were held together by a shared belief that flight would change the world. Sinek points to one detail that shows how that team thought. Each day, the brothers took several sets of parts out with them, because they expected to crash several times before they came home.

##They didn't plan for success. They planned to keep going.##

Then came December 1903. On the 8th, Langley's Great Aerodrome was launched from a houseboat on the Potomac River. It went straight into the water. The press, which had followed him so closely, now mocked him.

Nine days later, on 17 December, at Kitty Hawk, the Wrights achieved the first sustained, controlled, powered flight in history.

Sinek's reading of the story is simple. Langley was chasing a result, the fame and fortune that would come from being first. The Wrights were driven by a *why*. When results are what you're after, a public failure hits hard, and Langley did not carry on once the Wrights had succeeded. The Wrights and their team, meanwhile, had already been failing and coming back for years.

One detail is worth getting right. The order of events matters here. Langley's crash came first and the Wrights' flight came after. He didn't give up because they flew. His failure had already happened, in public, before anyone knew the bicycle mechanics from Dayton had beaten him.

[[lessons]]
$$**Resources don't buy the breakthrough.** Langley had $50,000 from the War Department, a Smithsonian title and a strong team. The Wrights had a bicycle shop and its earnings. Everything you could measure favored Langley, and it still wasn't enough. Budgets and credentials help, but they can't stand in for the thing that makes people keep working on a problem when nobody is paying attention.$$

$$**A shared belief turns failure into routine.** Packing several sets of parts a day means you've already accepted the crash before it happens. For the Wrights' team, a broken wing wasn't a verdict. It was Tuesday. When people are united by what they believe, a failure becomes one step in the work instead of the end of it.$$

$$**Chasing the result makes you fragile.** Langley's crash on 8 December happened in front of the press he had courted, and his project didn't recover. If fame is the goal, a public failure takes away the very thing you were after. Keep the dates straight, though: his collapse came before the Wrights flew on 17 December, not because of it. The fragility was already in the project.$$

[[reflect]]
- If your funding or recognition disappeared tomorrow, would you still be working on this?
- Think of your last visible failure. Did you pack "spare parts" for it, or did it catch you with nothing to fall back on?
- Does your team come back after a setback because they're paid to, or because they believe in where you're going?
- Where are you measuring yourself by someone else's budget instead of by your own reason?

&&What is the belief behind your work that would make you show up again the day after a crash?&&

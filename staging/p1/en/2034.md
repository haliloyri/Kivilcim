A prize committee praises a formula for something rare in economics: it escaped the lecture hall and went to work. Traders use it daily. Two of the men behind it collect the honour, and in the same season their names also appear on the letterhead of a young investment partnership that borrows heavily.

[[story]]
The Nobel committee's own words, as a University of Chicago magazine reported them, were these: "Nowadays, thousands of traders and investors use the formula every day to value stock options." The committee added that such rapid and widespread application of a theoretical result was new to economics.

That is the prize Myron Scholes and Robert Merton won on Tuesday, 14 October 1997, for a method of valuing derivatives. A third name belonged on the list: according to Stanford's announcement, Fischer Black, who worked on the method with them, had died in 1995, and the prize is not awarded after death.

The same magazine noted that Scholes was still working with Long-Term Capital Management in Greenwich, Connecticut, a firm he had helped to found with Merton. The Federal Reserve's own history says John Meriwether founded LTCM in February 1994 and that its team included two Nobel laureates, a pedigree that is central to the story Roger Lowenstein tells in *When Genius Failed*.

Be careful about what the prize covered. It honoured a way of pricing options. According to the President's Working Group report, LTCM sought profit from several strategies, among them convergence trades and dynamic hedging, and the report says the fund's standing in the hedge-fund world rested on the reputation of its principals in the theory of financial markets, along with its large initial capital.

About ten months after the prize, in August 1998, the fund lost 1.8 billion dollars in a single month. The Working Group's later verdict was that some of the risk models used by LTCM and its creditors were flawed: they had been estimated in stable periods and did not expect markets to be hit together. Alan Greenspan told Congress that abnormal returns are sustained only through abnormal exposure to risk.

The prize and the collapse do not contradict each other, because they measured different things. The prize confirmed that an idea was right and useful in its own field. Whether the fund would survive depended on other questions: how much debt it carried, which assumptions it leaned on, and what the markets would do in a bad month. No committee could answer that for the fund; its own risk arithmetic had to, and August tested it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask under which conditions a proven method works, separately from asking whether it has succeeded.** A prize or a strong track record shows that a method is sound in its own field, but it does not show where its limits lie in your work, such as how much leverage you use or what you assume about calm and storm. When an adviser or a tool arrives with an impressive past, ask exactly where it works and whether your situation fits. Writing the answer down separates confidence from knowledge and shows what you still do not know before you decide, which is the moment you can still change course.$$

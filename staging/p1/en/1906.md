For about ten years, a small team keeps trying to get one author onto one television show, and the door stays shut. Phone calls go out and nothing comes back. Then, one day, a producer calls back with a single question about the schedule: can you fill an hour?

[[story]]
Gary Chapman's *The 5 Love Languages* has sold more than 20 million copies. It started nowhere near that. According to Christianity Today, the book sold 8,500 copies in the first year after its 1992 release. In the second year, sales more than doubled. By the fourth year it was selling 137,000 copies.

Set those three numbers side by side and a direction appears. Taken alone, 8,500 is a small number, and an author could easily have given up on it. But the doubling in year two showed that something was not standing still. By year four, the book was selling more than sixteen times its first-year figure. Plotted on a chart, the curve would hug the floor in year one and then bend upward.

Chapman's team was knocking on another door in those same years. Christianity Today says they spent about ten years trying to get him on Oprah's show. In the end a callback came. A producer asked whether they could fill an hour of *Oprah's Lifeclass*, a prime-time program on Winfrey's own cable network, around Valentine's Day 2013.

On the air, Winfrey said the book never seemed to leave the *New York Times* bestseller list. Her wardrobe manager said it had transformed her marriage. The team had knocked on one door for a decade, and it opened onto an hour of broadcast. Winfrey's remark suggests the book had been collecting readers quietly all along. The show did not create it. It caught up with readers who were already there.

Today, by the same article, the book has sold more than a million copies in Spanish alone and has been printed in 57 languages. Moody, the publisher, reports about 2.5 million monthly visitors to the book's website, many of them taking its quiz.

Two clocks were running at once, the one for sales and the one for the television door. Both were slow, and both made sense only in hindsight.

What stands out is how misleading the first number was. The 8,500 copies said nothing about where the book was headed. The direction showed only in the figures for year two and year four, and the ten-year wait ended in a single phone call.

[[lessons]]
$$**Judge a slow start by the direction it is moving before you judge it by its size.** A new product, a blog or a small business can draw little attention in its first months, but if each period reaches somewhat more people than the one before, that is a sign something is working. Rather than calling the first number too big or too small, put two periods side by side and look at the ratio. That way you decide whether to quit by the numbers you hold, and not by the mood of the day, whether that is enthusiasm or disappointment.$$

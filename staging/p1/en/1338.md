[[open]]
Two people walk into the same meeting room. One stops just inside the door, hands on hips, and takes in the space. The other edges along the wall, shoulders drawn in, hands tucked out of sight. Neither has said a word. The room has already decided something about each of them.

[[story]]
Joe Navarro, the former FBI agent behind *What Every BODY Is Saying*, is interested in exactly that silent verdict. In his account, the way we hold ourselves and use space works as a display of comfort and confidence. Other people read it, often without realizing they're doing it.

There's a popular version of this idea that goes much further. It promises that holding a bold pose for a couple of minutes will shift your hormones and, with them, how you feel. That claim doesn't come from Navarro's book, and its hormonal findings failed to hold up in later research. What Navarro describes is narrower: what your posture tells everyone else.

Start at the expansive end. He treats territorial displays, such as standing with hands on hips or spreading out to claim room, as signs of confidence or dominance. Hands on hips with elbows out, sometimes called standing "arms akimbo," takes up space on purpose. Whether that reads as assured or as overbearing depends on who's doing it, and where.

Then there are the hands. Navarro picks out steepling, fingertips touching in a spire shape, as a high-confidence gesture. It's a small movement and easy to overlook. In his reading, it signals someone who feels sure of their ground.

##Low confidence tends to show as the body making itself smaller.## Discomfort pulls things inward: arms drawn close, a withdrawing posture, hands tucked away or thumbs hidden. Put that next to steepling and the contrast is striking. The same pair of hands can signal certainty or doubt, depending on whether they're out in the open.

As elsewhere in the book, he warns against treating any of this as a fixed code. A posture means something in context, and against how that particular person usually carries themselves. Someone who always sits with their hands in their lap isn't necessarily nervous today. The signal lies in what stands out from their usual way of sitting and standing.

So, back to the two people at the door. Before either speaks, the room has formed an impression from the space they claim and where their hands are. Navarro doesn't offer this as a technique for changing how you feel inside. He offers it as a way of seeing what your body is already telling people.

[[lessons]]
$$**The room reads you first.** Before you say anything, people form an impression from your posture and how much space you take up. Navarro describes these as displays others read, often without noticing they're reading them. Your opening line arrives after that impression, not before it.$$

$$**Watch your hands.** Small gestures carry strong signals in both directions. Steepled fingertips read as high confidence, while hands pulled out of sight or hidden thumbs read as low confidence. If you want a quick check on what you're projecting, your hands are the easiest place to look.$$

$$**Big can read as bossy.** Hands on hips and space-claiming are territorial displays. In one setting they look assured. In another, especially standing over someone, they look like dominance. Taking up more room isn't automatically better, so it's worth noticing whether your stance fits what the moment needs.$$

[[reflect]]
- When you're unsure of yourself in a group, what do your hands and shoulders tend to do?
- Think of someone who seems confident the moment they walk in. How do they occupy the space?
- Is there a setting where your usual stance might come across as more dominant than you intend?

&&At your next meeting or presentation, could you notice where your hands are and how much room you take up, and whether people respond to you any differently?&&

A nineteen-year-old lies hurt after a car crash in a foreign country, not knowing how badly he's injured. In the confusion, three simple questions rise in his mind. They weren't about his plans or his future. They were about whether his life so far had counted.

[[story]]
Brendon Burchard has told this story many times. At nineteen, he was in the Dominican Republic when the car he was riding in, driven by a friend, crashed. It was serious. In the aftermath, bloodied and shaken, he didn't know whether he'd survive.

He has described how, in those moments, three questions came to him with sudden clarity.

> Did I live? Did I love? Did I matter?

##He realized he wasn't sure he could honestly answer yes to any of them.##

Burchard survived. And he describes those three questions as the turning point of his life. They stayed with him long after his injuries healed. He began to ask whether he was truly living fully, not just going through the motions. Whether he was loving openly and generously. Whether his life was making any real difference to others.

Those questions eventually shaped his career. He became a coach and author, working on what helps people live, and perform, at their best. In *High Performance Habits*, he draws on his research and his years of coaching to ask why some people succeed consistently over the long term while others burn out, plateau or never quite get started.

His answer is that the difference usually isn't talent or luck. It's a set of habits. He identifies six.

High performers **seek clarity**: they keep asking who they want to be, how they want to interact with others, and what matters most. They **generate energy**: they deliberately manage their physical and mental energy instead of running on empty. They **raise necessity**: they feel that their performance truly matters, to themselves and to others. They **increase productivity** on the outputs that matter most, rather than just staying busy. They **develop influence** with others. And they **demonstrate courage**: they speak up, take risks and act despite fear.

Burchard's point is that none of these are fixed personality traits. They're habits, which means they can be learned and practiced.

But underneath the six habits, the three questions from that crash still run through his work. Clarity, energy and courage are, in the end, ways of making sure that when you ask whether you lived, loved and mattered, you can answer yes.

[[lessons]]
$$**Big questions cut through the noise.** "Did I live? Did I love? Did I matter?" are simple, but they reveal priorities instantly. Burchard only faced them because of a crash. You don't need a crisis to ask them. Asking them on an ordinary day can show you where your life is drifting away from what you care about.$$

$$**A near miss can become a compass.** Burchard turned a frightening moment into questions that shaped his whole career. Painful experiences don't automatically lead anywhere good. But reflecting on what they revealed, rather than just moving past them, can give you a lasting sense of direction.$$

$$**Performance comes from habits.** The six habits, clarity, energy, necessity, productivity, influence and courage, are practices, not gifts. Burchard's research suggests that people who succeed over the long term aren't simply more talented. They've built routines that keep them clear, energized and brave.$$

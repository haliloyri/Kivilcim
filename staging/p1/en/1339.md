[[open]]
April 2007. Arianna Huffington is two years into building The Huffington Post, working 18-hour days, and the company is growing fast. Then she collapses at her desk. Her head hits it on the way down. When she comes to, she is lying in a pool of blood with a broken cheekbone. What caused it would take a string of doctors to find.

[[story]]
By every outward measure, this was success. Huffington had co-founded The Huffington Post about two years earlier, and it was growing quickly. She was pouring everything into it. Eighteen-hour days were simply how she worked.

The collapse didn't come after a failure or a setback. It came in the middle of the climb, at exactly the point where everything looked like it was going right.

A fall like that seems to demand an explanation. A founder at the top of her game doesn't just drop at her desk and break her cheekbone for no reason. So she went looking for the reason. Appointment followed appointment as she went from doctor to doctor, searching for the underlying illness that would account for what had happened.

##There was no hidden disease. The cause was exhaustion.##

That answer was harder to fix than a diagnosis would have been, because it pointed straight back at how she was living. It made her question whether this was really what success looked like. The episode also started what became a deep interest in sleep, and years later she opened *The Sleep Revolution* with this story.

In the book she turns to the culture she had been part of. Sleep deprivation, she argues, had become a badge of honor at work. People boast about how little sleep they get by on, as if a short night proved how committed they were. Meanwhile, research links sleep loss to worse judgment, worse mood and worse health.

Put those two things side by side and the problem is plain. The qualities a demanding job relies on, clear judgment above all, are the ones that lack of sleep wears down. The long days that looked like proof of dedication were also what put her on the office floor.

Her story works because it runs against the usual script. Nobody would have looked at her calendar that spring and seen a warning. They would have seen someone succeeding. The warning only became visible when she woke up bleeding next to her desk, and the doctors, one after another, found nothing wrong except the pace itself.

[[lessons]]
$$**Success can hide the bill.** Huffington collapsed two years into building a fast-growing company, not after a failure. From the outside, her schedule looked like it was working. The cost was real, but it didn't show up in any business result until her body collected it.$$

$$**Bragging turns warnings into trophies.** When people boast about how little they sleep, a sign of strain starts to look like a sign of dedication. Huffington calls this a badge of honor in work culture. It makes the warning harder to hear, both for the person bragging and for everyone who copies them.$$

$$**Sometimes the diagnosis is the schedule.** Doctor after doctor looked for a hidden illness and found exhaustion instead. It's tempting to hunt for one specific problem to fix. Occasionally the problem is simply the way the days are being lived.$$

[[reflect]]
- Have you ever heard someone say they got by on very little sleep with a hint of pride? How did you react?
- What does your own schedule suggest you believe about rest and success?
- When did you last treat tiredness as a problem to push through rather than information?

&&The next time you're about to trade sleep for one more hour of work, what would the next afternoon have to say about that hour?&&

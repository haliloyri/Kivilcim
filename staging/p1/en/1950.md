A student sits at a table with a sheet of ten hard general-knowledge questions, all of them written by the student's own hand. Across the table, another student frowns at the first one and says they do not know. A few observers watch from the side, and chance alone decided who got which seat.

[[story]]
In the 1970s, the psychologist Lee Ross and his colleagues Teresa Amabile and Julia Steinmetz ran a quiz game at Stanford that became one of the best-known experiments in social psychology. It speaks directly to *Difficult Conversations*, whose authors Douglas Stone, Bruce Patton and Sheila Heen show how quickly we turn another person's behavior into a verdict on the person.

The setup was simple. Students were assigned by chance to one of two roles, questioner or contestant. The questioner wrote a set of hard general-knowledge questions, drawing on whatever they happened to know, and put them to the contestant. A third group, the observers, watched the exchange without taking part.

Look at what the roles did. The questioner chose the ground. They could pick a painter, a river or a grammar rule from their own corner of knowledge and be sure of the answer in advance. The contestant had to walk across territory somebody else had picked, so a string of "I don't know" was close to built in, and it said nothing about how much the contestant knew in general.

Afterwards, everyone rated the general knowledge of both players. The contestants rated the questioners as more knowledgeable than themselves. The observers did the same. Only the questioners held back, and did not rate themselves above the contestants.

Nobody had asked the observers to size up anyone's character; they were asked only to rate general knowledge, and the verdict came out lopsided anyway. So two groups looked at one exchange and concluded that one student was simply better informed. They had been handed a role and read it as a trait, even though the roles were assigned at random and, as the study is usually described, the observers knew that.

The same move sits underneath many hard conversations. A colleague misses a deadline, a friend does not call, a boss gives a curt answer, and we take the act as a portrait of the person. The one who acted usually sees the setting around it: the other deadlines, the bad week, the question they had been handed. We see the person. They see the position, and a conversation that starts from only one of those views usually goes badly.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask what position the other person was in before you judge what kind of person they are.** A neighbor who never answers the group chat may be the one who gets the awkward questions all day at work, and a manager who sounds sharp may be the one holding the whole schedule. When you see only the behavior, you tend to describe it as a trait, while the other person feels the setting that produced it. Add one sentence to your own account, such as "Given what was on their desk that week, what would I have done?" and the conversation can start from the situation instead of from a charge.$$

[[story]]
Tara Westover's memoir *Educated* tells how she grew up in an Idaho survivalist family and entered a classroom for the first time as a teenager. This story is from her first semester at Brigham Young University, in a lecture on Western art. The professor was showing slides, and one of them carried a caption with a word she did not know.

She asked what it meant. The room went silent, and the professor answered, "Thanks for that," then went back to his notes. Afterwards a classmate named Vanessa told her, "You shouldn't make fun of that. It's not a joke." She had meant no joke and had no idea why the question had landed so badly.

She went to the computer lab and looked up the word. It was "Holocaust." What she read shook her, and what shook her almost as much was how little she knew. She had vaguely believed that Jews were killed long ago, in a small conflict, something like the Boston Massacre her father liked to discuss. She recalled that her mother might once have mentioned it, and she felt a sudden wish to shout at her mother, which frightened her.

Then she found Vanessa and apologised for the joke, promising not to do it again. She gave no explanation, since she had none she could put into words. To keep her promise she stopped raising her hand. She stayed silent for the rest of the semester.

Look at how the moment worked. A professor moved on with his lecture, and a classmate defended what she took to be a sensitive subject. Neither needed to be unkind for the question to hurt, because the gap was invisible to everyone except Westover. From the front of the room it looked like mockery, and from her seat it was simply a word she had never met.

What she did next is the part to study. She closed the gap on her own, at a computer, within hours of the lecture. It was quick, and it had a price: she kept a promise by going quiet, and for the rest of the semester the questions she might have asked stayed unasked.

[[lessons]]
$$**When a question lands badly, find out what the room knows that you do not before you decide you were wrong to ask.** Westover read the silence as ridicule, but it was a reaction to a gap she could not yet see. In your own work the same thing happens when a new colleague asks about a basic term and the room goes quiet. Go and look the answer up quickly, then tell one trusted person what you found and how it felt. A gap closed in private is still a gap, and a semester of silence costs more than the question did.$$

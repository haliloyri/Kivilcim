[[open]]
Picture a report card. Next to one required course, where you'd expect a failing grade, there are two words instead: *Not Yet.* The student missed the standard either way. Nothing about the work has changed. But read those two lines, one with an F and one with "Not Yet," and notice how differently each one lands. This episode is about how much weight one label carries, and which way it pushes you.

[[story]]
Carol Dweck, the Stanford psychologist behind *Mindset*, tells a story about a high school in Chicago. It's an example she came across, not an experiment she ran, and she uses it to make a simple point clear.

At this school, students had to pass certain courses to graduate. When a student didn't pass one of them, they didn't get a failing grade. They got the grade "Not Yet."

That's the whole policy. It sounds almost too small to matter. Dweck argues that it matters a great deal, because of what each label tells the student about where they stand.

> A failing grade says: you are nowhere.

> "Not Yet" says: you are on a learning curve, and there is a path forward.

Think about what it's like to be sixteen and see that F. It doesn't read as a note about your essay or your problem set. It reads as a verdict on you. The door feels shut. "Not Yet" describes the same gap between where you are and where you need to be, but it puts you *on the way* somewhere. The distance is still real. The door is still open.

Dweck doesn't present outcome numbers for this school, and neither will I. What makes the anecdote worth telling is how neatly it fits what her own research had found in separate studies.

In that research, she and her colleagues looked at how students react after they fail. Some of them held what she calls a fixed mindset: the belief that ability is a set trait, something you either have or don't. When those students hit a failure, their answers were revealing. Some said that next time they might cheat. Others said they would look for someone who had done worse than them, so they could feel better about themselves.

Look at what's missing from both answers. Neither one involves learning the material. Both are ways to get out from under the failure, either by going around it or by shrinking it next to someone else's.

##Students who saw failure as a verdict on who they were looked for escape routes, and students who saw it as part of a process looked at their mistakes.##

That second group held a growth mindset, the belief that ability can be developed. In Dweck's studies, these students engaged more deeply with their errors. They processed what had gone wrong and worked on correcting it instead of looking away.

This is the core of Dweck's argument. When you frame ability as something you build through a process, rather than something that defines your identity, you change how people respond when things get hard. A failure stops being a statement about who you are. It becomes information about where you are.

The Chicago school put that whole idea into two words on a report card.

[[lessons]]
$$**Identity labels invite hiding; process labels invite effort.** The Chicago school didn't lower its standard. Students still had to pass the course. What changed was the message. "Failing" judges the person and implies there's nowhere left to go. "Not Yet" describes a stage and implies a next step. When a label tells you *what you are*, the natural move is to protect yourself. When it tells you *where you are*, the natural move is to keep going.$$

$$**Watch for escape routes after a setback.** Dweck's fixed-mindset students didn't say they would study harder. They said they might cheat, or they would find someone who did worse. Both responses deal with the pain of failing and leave the problem untouched. If you notice yourself reaching for an excuse, a shortcut, or a flattering comparison after a miss, that's often a sign you've taken the failure as a verdict on yourself.$$

$$**A mistake is data, not a sentence.** In Dweck's studies, growth-mindset students did the uncomfortable thing: they looked straight at their errors and worked through them. That only makes sense if a mistake is information you can use. If you treat it as a judgment on your worth, you'll want to hide from it. If you treat it as a clue, you'll want to study it.$$

[[reflect]]
- Name one area where you currently say "I'm bad at this." What would the process version of that sentence sound like?
- Think back to your last real setback. Did you learn from it, or did you find a way around it, such as an excuse, a shortcut, or someone who did worse?
- When someone you lead or love falls short, what does your first reaction tell them: "you're nowhere" or "you're on the way"?
- Which mistake from this month have you avoided looking at closely?

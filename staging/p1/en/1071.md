[[open]]
Picture the most generous professor at one of the world's top business schools. He's the one who answers questions, helps students, and makes time for colleagues. Now picture his email sending you an automatic reply that says, in effect, *not now, and not for a few days.* Those two pictures are of the same man, and he isn't being a hypocrite. The auto-reply is part of how he manages to be both.

[[story]]
The man is Adam Grant. In *Deep Work*, Cal Newport presents him as the youngest full professor at the Wharton School. That kind of promotion comes from output, and Grant's was unusually high. He kept publishing papers in the top academic journals, where getting in even once is hard.

Newport wanted to know how one person produced that much. The obvious guess is that Grant simply worked more hours than everyone else. Newport points somewhere else. What set Grant apart, he argues, was how he arranged his hard work. He didn't spread it thinly across his days. He packed it into long, uninterrupted blocks.

The packing happened at more than one scale.

The first scale was the whole semester. Grant put all his teaching into one semester. For those months he was fully a teacher, with classes, students, and everything that goes with them. That left the other semester free for focused research. He didn't have to keep switching between lecture prep and serious thinking every week.

The second scale was days. During his research periods, Grant would withdraw for several days at a time. He turned on an out-of-office auto-responder, so anyone who emailed him learned right away that he wasn't reading messages. He cut himself off from students and colleagues and worked alone. For those stretches, the demanding work had all of his attention.

##The out-of-office reply wasn't Grant walling himself off from people. It was him scheduling when people could reach him.##

This is what makes the story more than a productivity tip. Grant is also known for being generous and helpful to others. Newport notes that outside those retreats, Grant kept his door open. The isolation had a start and an end, and it was planned. Students and colleagues weren't shut out for good. They were asked to wait for a while, and they were told plainly that they were waiting.

Newport uses Grant to illustrate a simple formula:

> High-quality work produced = time spent × intensity of focus.

Read that way, Grant's schedule makes sense. Nearly everyone has a hard ceiling on the first factor, since there are only so many hours in a week and a semester. The second factor has much more room to grow. An hour of research broken up by emails, office drop-ins, and class prep produces less than an hour with nothing competing for attention. Grant's choices about semesters and days were all aimed at raising that second number. A whole teaching-free semester, followed by several days behind an auto-responder, is about as much focus intensity as an academic calendar allows.

So the paradox from the start comes apart. The generous professor and the unreachable professor were the same person at different times. Grant didn't try to be available and deeply focused in the same hour. He gave each one its own block and kept them apart.

[[lessons]]
$$**Focus multiplies time.** Newport's formula, work produced = time spent × intensity of focus, explains Grant better than a story about long hours would. Grant's advantage came from the multiplier. A few days of research with the auto-responder on meant every hour got his full attention, so the same amount of time went further than it would have in a day full of interruptions.$$

$$**Batch at more than one scale.** Grant didn't stop at blocking off a morning. He grouped a whole semester's worth of teaching together so the other semester could go to research. Then, inside the research time, he grouped days into retreats. Each level protected the one below it. If you only protect an hour but your week is scattered, that hour usually gets taken too. Look for the biggest unit you can batch, whether that's a season, a week, or a day, and then work down from there.$$

$$**Being unavailable can be done openly and politely.** The auto-responder came from a man known for helping people. It told anyone who wrote to him exactly where things stood, so nobody was left guessing. Because his door was open at other times, the absence came across as a scheduled pause. It didn't read as a refusal. Being clear about when you're away is what keeps that time away from looking like indifference.$$

[[reflect]]
- Think about the hardest kind of work you do. Is it done in long stretches, or in fifteen-minute pieces between other things?
- Grant's unit was a semester and then several days. What's the biggest block you could realistically protect: a morning, a full day, a week each quarter?
- Consider who would actually be hurt if you were unreachable for that block. Is it really them, or is it your worry about looking unhelpful?
- Where could a clear, polite notice, like an auto-reply or a status or a calendar entry, do the explaining so you don't have to?

&&If your results come from time multiplied by focus, which of the two have you actually been trying to increase?&&

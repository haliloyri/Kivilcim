Two nine-year-olds asked a businessman to teach them how to make money. He put them to work dusting shelves in one of his stores for a tiny wage. When one of them complained, he did not raise the pay. He cut it to nothing. It was, Robert Kiyosaki says, the start of his real education about money.

[[story]]
Robert Kiyosaki's *Rich Dad Poor Dad* (1997) is built around two father figures. One was his own father, highly educated, working in Hawaii's state education system. The other was the father of his best friend, Mike. That man had left school early, but he was building businesses.

Their advice pointed in opposite directions. His real father said to study hard and get a secure job. Mike's father taught that the rich don't work for money; they make money work for them. The book calls the first man "poor dad" and the second "rich dad."

When Robert was about nine, he and Mike went to the rich dad and asked him to teach them how to make money. His answer was a job. The boys dusted shelves in one of his stores for a very small wage. Robert eventually complained. Rich dad's response was to take the wage away entirely and have them keep working for nothing.

Kiyosaki tells this as a lesson, not an injustice. As he explains it, fear and desire keep people chasing paychecks. The rich dad wanted him to stop asking for a raise and start looking for opportunities instead.

And the boys did find one. The store was throwing away comic books. They took them and started a comic-book reading library for other kids. ##The library brought them income while they were not personally working in it.## For the first time, money was coming in without their hours going out.

That small business is the seed of the book's core definitions. An asset, Kiyosaki writes, is something that puts money in your pocket. A liability is something that takes money out. The definitions are blunt on purpose, and they lead to his most argued-about claim: by this test, the home you live in is usually a liability, not an asset.

The book follows the two men into later life. By its account, the rich dad became one of the wealthiest men in Hawaii, while the educated dad struggled financially later on. That outcome runs against almost everything children are taught about school, effort and success, which is exactly why Kiyosaki puts it at the center.

One caveat is worth knowing. Critics have questioned whether the rich dad was a single real person. The book presents him as real, and this account follows the book. Either way, the dusty shelves and the discarded comics make the distinction easy to remember: one kind of money stops when you stop, and the other keeps coming.

[[lessons]]
$$**Hours for pay is one game; owning is another.** Dusting shelves paid the boys only while they were dusting. The comic-book library brought in money while they were somewhere else. Kiyosaki's whole book grows from that difference between working for money and owning something that works for you.$$

$$**Judge things by cash flow, not by their label.** His test is simple: does it put money in your pocket or take money out? Run the home you live in through that test and, by his definition, it usually lands on the liability side. Calling something an investment doesn't change which way the money moves each month.$$

$$**Losing the wage opened their eyes.** The comic books were being thrown away the whole time. The boys spotted them only after the pay was gone and they started looking for opportunities instead. Sometimes a complaint about pay stands between you and something lying in plain sight.$$

Fourteen bouts are behind him: seven wins, seven losses. The fifteenth and last will decide whether his rank rises or falls for the next tournament. Across the ring stands an opponent who already has his eight wins and has far less riding on this one.

[[story]]
In sumo, the eighth win of a fifteen-bout tournament is worth about four times as much as any other. A wrestler with a winning record moves up the ranking, and the eighth win alone lifts him about eleven places, while each other win is worth about three. Rank sets his pay, and by the rough estimate of Mark Duggan and Steven Levitt, co-author of *Freakonomics*, one place was worth about $3,000 a year. At the bottom, wrestlers rise early to clean the stable, while those in the top forty have servants of their own. A wrestler at seven wins has much more to gain on the last day than a rival with eight has to lose.

The two economists went through almost every top-division match from January 1989 to January 2000, more than 32,000 bouts. If nothing odd were going on, about as many wrestlers should finish with exactly seven wins as with exactly eight, around 19.6 percent each. In the data, 26.0 percent finished with eight and only 12.2 percent with seven.

On the last days, wrestlers who needed one more win to reach eight won 12 to 16 percentage points more often than expected. Pure effort might explain that, since the prize is big, so the authors looked at what happened the next time the same two men met. The wrestler who had won on the margin then lost about seven percentage points more often than expected. The wrestler who had lost on the margin showed no such pattern. Effort has no reason to behave that way. A favor repaid at the next meeting does, and the pattern was gone by the meeting after that.

Then came the quiet. In the tournaments around the time a former wrestler named Itai made allegations of match-rigging that the media widely covered, the extra wins disappeared. The authors also found that some stables seemed to trade favors with each other.

The Japanese Sumo Association dismissed such allegations as inventions of disgraced former wrestlers, and by the time of the paper, no wrestler had been formally disciplined. Duggan and Levitt's evidence is statistical. It shows a pattern across thousands of bouts and says nothing about what happened in any one of them. It is also the kind of pattern that shows up only when you know where the reward jumps.

[[lessons]]
$$**Find the point where a reward jumps and watch what people do just below it.** A sales team that earns its bonus only at one exact quarterly target will often see deals pulled forward or pushed back in the last days before the cutoff, and the totals will bunch just above the line. The same happens wherever a small step in effort brings a large step in payoff, such as a grade boundary or a free-shipping threshold. Once you see the jump in a reward, you can look for odd bunching in your own numbers and choose whether to smooth the reward or to look more closely at what happens at the edge.$$

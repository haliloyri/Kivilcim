Weeks before a book goes on sale, a publisher and two magazines are told that they may soon find themselves in court. The warning does not point to a wrong figure or a mistaken sentence. It points to the plan to print the book at all. The book is about what a poison does once it leaves the field where it was sprayed.

[[story]]
In June 1962 The New Yorker began publishing *Silent Spring* in three parts, in its issues of 16, 23 and 30 June. The book itself followed from Houghton Mifflin on 27 September. Between the two dates, the chemical industry answered, and the answers took three quite different forms.

The first was a threat. According to Rachel Carson's biographer Linda Lear, the Velsicol Chemical Company threatened legal action against Houghton Mifflin, The New Yorker and Audubon Magazine unless the planned features were cancelled. This happened in the weeks before publication. The threat was aimed at the people who would print Carson. It said nothing about whether her claims were right or wrong. A book publisher, a weekly magazine and a conservation magazine were all named, so the threat covered every planned route to readers at once.

The second was a joke with a grim punchline. Monsanto published a parody called "The Desolate Year", about 5,000 copies of it, according to an article in the journal American Entomologist. It pictured a world of famine and disease after pesticides were banned. Its argument was a disaster in a hypothetical future, painted vividly enough to read like a forecast.

The third was a slogan. Robert White-Stevens, a biochemist at American Cyanamid, said that if people followed Carson's teachings, "we would return to the Dark Ages," as a Frontline web article reports. The line is short and memorable, and it gives a listener nothing to check.

Look at what the three replies share. A lawsuit threat talks about printing, a parody talks about a future, and a slogan talks about the Dark Ages. As reported, none of them is a measurement, a sample or a named study. Each one answered a version of Carson that was easier to oppose than the one on the page, and each tried to move the discussion from the evidence to the stakes.

The reaction tells you one more simple thing. Companies rarely threaten, parody and sloganise about a book they expect nobody to read, so the effort they spent shows how seriously they took it. The question, however, stayed where it started: what happens to the birds, the soil and the water when a poison is spread across a whole landscape?

[[lessons]]
$$**When criticism arrives, sort the replies into those that answer your actual claim and those that answer a bigger, easier version of it.** A threat, a joke and a slogan can all feel like an argument because they are loud, and they cost the person who hears them time and calm. Write your claim in one plain sentence, then hold each reply against it and ask which ones touch it. Respond to the ones that do, with evidence, and let the rest pass, since a caricature does not need a defence and a loud tone is not a reason to doubt yourself.$$

[[story]]
Jeremy Brown is one of the main characters of Michael Lewis's *Moneyball*, and this story is how he came to be drafted in June 2002. The Oakland Athletics held extra picks that year because they had lost Jason Giambi. With one of them, 35th overall, they took Brown, the University of Alabama's catcher. According to Baseball America he signed for 350,000 dollars, and he had not been ranked among the draft's top 250 players beforehand.

Oakland's own scouts did not like the choice. Sports Illustrated reported that they mocked his build, and one called it "a soft body, a fleshy kind of body." Most clubs thought Brown should not have been drafted at all, according to the same account. General manager Billy Beane saw something else, a hitter who produced and drew walks.

The same draft produced other names. Nick Swisher went 16th and was Beane's top target. Joe Blanton went 24th, John McCurdy 26th and Mark Teahen 39th. Years later the picture looked very different from player to player. By 2011 Swisher was the Yankees' starting right fielder with 185 home runs and an .827 OPS, the best of the group. Blanton had a 73-62 record and a 4.33 ERA. McCurdy never played above Double-A. McCurdy ended his minor league career in 2006 with 33 home runs and a .259/.308/.374 line. Teahen never played for Oakland, because he was traded to the Royals in 2004, and his career total of 67 home runs was 361 fewer than Giambi's.

Brown played five games for Oakland, all at the end of the 2006 season, and the first came on 3 September. He hit .300 with two doubles, mostly as a designated hitter. In February 2008 he announced his retirement and went home to Hueytown, Alabama, with ten major league at-bats to his name, three of them hits.

So the draft pick Beane was most mocked for left the smallest line in the record. The same draft class held a Swisher and a Blanton as well, so judging it by Brown alone would be reading one row of a table. A choice has to be judged by the evidence it was built on, and by the whole class it came from.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you judge a person, write down which evidence shows they can do the job, then check what their appearance is adding to your view.** Say the owner of a corner shop is picking a new cashier and leans toward the candidate who looks tidier and talks more. The shop actually needs someone who does not miscount and does not keep customers waiting, which shows up in what that person has done in earlier jobs. If you write the test down first, you can tell whether you are turning someone down for something that matters or for something you simply dislike. You also stop mixing up a decision's quality with one result's luck.$$

A man who drives a London taxi for a living lies still in a brain scanner. Another man, who has never driven one, goes through the same scan. Later, researchers lay the pictures side by side and look at one small structure, shaped like a seahorse, near the middle of each brain.

[[story]]
The neuroscientist Eleanor Maguire and her colleagues at University College London wanted to know what a demanding spatial skill does to the brain. London taxi drivers were an obvious group to ask. To get a licence they have to learn the city's streets and routes, a course known as "the Knowledge", which took the drivers in this study about two years on average, from ten months to three and a half years.

Sixteen right-handed men who were licensed drivers took part. Their average age was 44, and they had been driving taxis for 14.3 years on average, from a year and a half to 42 years. Their brain scans were compared with those of fifty right-handed men who did not drive taxis. For one of the measurements, sixteen of those controls, matched for age, were used.

The back part of the hippocampus, on both sides, was significantly larger in the drivers. The front part showed the opposite, relatively larger in the controls. Total hippocampal volume did not differ between the groups, so what separated them was where the tissue sat. Among the drivers, more years on the job went with a larger right posterior region, a correlation of about 0.6, and a smaller right anterior one.

That the total stayed the same matters, because it makes the picture less simple than "drivers have a bigger hippocampus". Tissue that grows in one place and shrinks in another could be a redistribution, which is how the authors read it. Heavy specialisation may ask the brain for room, and the room may come from somewhere else.

The authors suggested that the posterior hippocampus holds a spatial map of the city and can grow with use, a sign of local plasticity in the adult brain.

The study has a clear limit. The drivers were scanned once, in the middle of their careers, so it cannot separate the effect of driving from the possibility that people with such brains drift into the job. Commentators noted that scans taken before and after people reach spatial expertise would be much stronger evidence.

That is what a later study, by Katherine Woollett and Maguire in 2011, set out to do. It followed adults over four years as they trained for the licence. In those who qualified, gray matter in the posterior hippocampus increased, while no structural changes appeared in trainees who failed to qualify or in controls.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask what a long specialisation may be taking from somewhere else in you.** In the drivers' scans the back of the hippocampus was larger and the front relatively smaller, with the total unchanged, and the authors read that as tissue moving toward the work. A nurse who spends years on night shifts may become quick at sizing up an emergency and notice she no longer reads the long novels she used to love. Seeing a trade like that lets you decide which skill to protect, and give it a small regular slot before it fades, perhaps one evening a month.$$

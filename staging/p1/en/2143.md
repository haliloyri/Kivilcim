[[open]]
A small beeper clips to a belt and goes off at a random moment of the afternoon. Its owner has to stop whatever she is doing and answer a short list of questions. Does she want something right now, anything at all? Is she trying not to?

[[story]]
Wilhelm Hofmann and colleagues, among them Baumeister, wanted to know how often ordinary people feel a desire, and what they do about it. Their study, published in 2012, is one that *Willpower* describes. They gave 205 adults in Germany a beeper for a week. It sounded at random about seven times a day, and each time the person said whether a desire was there, whether it clashed with a goal, and whether they resisted it. The study collected 7,827 reports of desires.

The book's summary of the results is plain. About half the time the beeper went off, the person was feeling some desire. Another quarter had felt one in the last few minutes. And people spent roughly a quarter of their waking hours resisting an urge, which the authors work out as at least four hours a day.

The most resisted desires came in a clear order: eating, sleeping, a break from work, sex. After those came social media, email, web surfing, music and television. The most common method of resistance was not iron will. People looked for a distraction or began a different activity, and some simply tried to push the urge away or put up with it.

They succeeded about half the time. People did well at resisting sleep, sex and spending. They did badly with television, the web and the general pull to relax instead of work.

The authors also compared the forces behind a desire. Personality, such as how reward-seeking or self-controlled a person was, mattered most for how strong a desire was and whether it clashed with a goal. The situation mattered more for the later steps, whether the person resisted and whether they gave in.

The paper adds detail that sounds less like a lecture on character. Desires were moderated by situation: drinking alcohol, the mere presence of other people, and the presence of others who were already acting on the same desire. A person who faces a plate of biscuits alone is in a different position from one beside a colleague who is already eating.

The figures come from people reporting on themselves, and the study describes everyday life rather than testing a theory of depleted willpower. As a portrait of ordinary temptation it is unusually concrete.

[[lessons]]
$$**Plan around the room you will be in, not just the resolve you hope to have.** When others around you are already acting on a desire, resisting it is harder than when you are alone, so the setting does part of the work. Someone trying to cut down on late-night snacking may find it easier to keep nothing in the cupboard than to refuse whatever the household offers. Choosing where you spend time, and who is there, is a form of self-control you can use before the urge arrives. It also takes less effort than winning the same argument with yourself again and again.$$

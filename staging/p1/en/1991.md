Three of the biggest names in American retail opened discount stores in a single year. A fourth opening that year was a plain building in a small Arkansas town, and its owner was forty-four. The three big names had the money, the cities and the head start, and the man in Arkansas had fifteen-odd variety stores and a hunch.

[[story]]
Sam Walton's whole chain, about fifteen variety stores, sold $1.4 million in 1960. Published summaries of *Made in America*, the 1992 memoir he wrote with John Huey, note that an early discount store could do up to two million dollars on its own. One building outsold his entire network. Then the discounters arrived, and his next move was to open one himself.

The date was 2 July 1962. Walton, aged 44, opened the first Wal-Mart in Rogers, Arkansas. According to TIME, the same year S. S. Kresge launched K Mart, F. W. Woolworth started Woolco and Dayton Hudson began its Target chain. Walton was not early to an empty field. He joined a race that three large companies were starting in the same year, with far deeper pockets.

The summaries add that many people thought he was making a mistake. The conventional wisdom of the time held that discount retailing worked only in larger cities. Within five years, they report, K Mart had 250 stores and $800 million in sales, so the big players had the cities and the money. Walton had the towns they had written off.

Rogers was one of those towns, and so were the many places he went to next, one small town after another. Wal-Mart was incorporated in 1969 and had eighteen stores by the start of 1970, along with fourteen Ben Franklin variety stores, according to the Encyclopedia of Arkansas. He sold shares to the public that year. The old variety stores faded out slowly, and by 1976 he had closed every Ben Franklin. By the end of 1980 there were 330 Wal-Mart stores, and in 1991 the company became the nation's largest retailer, with 1,700 stores.

Look at where Walton put the idea. He took the same format, low prices and high volume, and set it down in towns that the leaders had decided were not worth the trouble. The big companies had the cities, and he did not have to fight them there. In Rogers and the towns after it, the rule that discounting needed a big city simply went untested for years. The rule everyone repeated turned out to describe where the big companies were looking, not where customers were.

[[lessons]]
$$**When everyone agrees a race can only be run in one place, check whether the rule describes the customers or only the competitors.** Conventional wisdom often reflects where the big players happen to be standing, which is not the same as where demand lives. Ask which group of people is being skipped by everyone's current plan, such as a smaller city, an older client, a night shift or a neglected product line. Then test the idea there, at a small scale first, because a crowded field and an empty field can sell exactly the same product at the same price.$$

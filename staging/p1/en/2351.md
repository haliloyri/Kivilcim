A few dozen villagers stand around a homemade tower of poles in the dust. A boy tightens the last connection. The wind picks up, the blades turn, and a small bulb comes on. Some of the people watching had been calling him crazy.

[[story]]
Behind that evening lay a scrap yard. According to published accounts of William Kamkwamba's story, he went to the yard across from his old school and found a rusted tractor cooling fan, a broken bicycle with one wheel and an old shock absorber. This was around 2002, and he was fourteen.

The fan blades alone were not enough. He heated plastic pipes and flattened them into longer blades. From his father's maize-mill machinery he took a ball bearing, and he used bottle caps as washers. The hardest part to get was the bicycle dynamo that would turn motion into light, which he obtained only after months of saving money and with help from a friend.

For the tower he used blue gum poles, lashed and nailed together into a frame roughly five metres tall. His cousin and a friend helped. In the village, some people called him *misala*, which means crazy.

Look at what the materials were. A fan made to cool a tractor. A bicycle made to be ridden. A shock absorber made to soften bumps. None of it was designed for wind, and every part had been thrown away by someone who thought its job was finished. What William brought was not a new part but a new question: what else could this do?

Most people define an obstacle by counting what is missing. No money, no workshop, no instructor. He first counted what was lying within reach. That small shift in the order of counting moved the problem from lack to inventory and made it solvable.

Of course a pile of parts in a scrap yard is not a working machine. A tower, blades, a dynamo and a turning axis had to meet in one arrangement. The yard was only a warehouse, and what turned it into a device was the ability to look at those pieces and picture a whole.

That is why even the smallest detail here teaches something. A bottle cap can be a washer. A pole from a tree can be a tower. Cleverness in this story is less about inventing anything new than about renaming what already exists.

[[lessons]]
$$**Take stock of what you already have before you write the list of what you lack.** A project with no budget, a team that is too small or an ageing tool tends to frame the problem around what has been lost. An inventory in which you ask each resource what else it could do reveals combinations nobody had thought of. A small first version built from what is on the table moves you forward far faster than waiting for perfect materials, and it trains you to see the resources that were part of the solution all along.$$

In a typical brainstorming session the whiteboard is blank, the marker is in someone's hand, and the boss speaks first. The most junior person in the room has an idea, glances at the faces around the table, and decides it can wait for another day.

[[story]]
The humorist Dave Barry once joked that the reason humanity never reaches its full potential is meetings. Adam Grant's *Hidden Potential* takes the joke seriously in a chapter on brainstorming, and his verdict is blunt: groups that brainstorm fall short of their potential. Many good ideas get lost in the room, and few are gained.

The evidence he points to is extensive. People working alone produce a greater volume and variety of ideas than people working in a group. That includes more brilliant ideas and also more poor ones, so a group's judgement is still needed, but later.

Grant names three obstacles that operate inside the meeting. The first is ego threat, the fear of looking foolish. The second is noise, because people cannot all talk at once. The third is the pressure to conform, which usually means agreeing with the boss. Together they push out diversity of thought and produce groupthink. They weigh most on the people with the least power: the most junior person in the room, the only woman of colour on a team, an introvert among extraverts.

His alternative is brainwriting. Members first come up with ideas alone. Then they pool them anonymously. Each person evaluates the pooled ideas independently, and only after that does the group choose the best ones and refine them.

Look at what each step takes out of the room. Working alone removes the noise. Pooling anonymously leaves the boss's face and the fear of looking foolish outside the process. Evaluating independently means no one knows yet which idea the loudest voice prefers. The group still meets, but at the stage where its judgement helps rather than where its habits get in the way.

Grant also draws on the research of Anita Woolley and colleagues, which found that balanced participation is a key driver of collective intelligence. Brainwriting, he says, works especially well for groups that struggle to achieve that balance.

He cites one more detail, from a law professor who studied corporate boardrooms in Norway. After women joined the boards, members were more likely to read the meeting materials beforehand, and the men followed suit so as not to be the unprepared ones. A change in who sat at the table changed how everyone arrived.

[[lessons]]
$$**Let each person's idea exist on paper before it meets the room's rank and volume.** A family choosing a holiday, or a small team picking next quarter's priority, usually ends up with whatever the most confident person said first, because everyone else adjusts to it within seconds. If everyone writes a few ideas privately and hands them in unsigned, the quiet ideas get the same reading as the loud ones. Then you can discuss the list as a list, pick on merit, and keep the conversation lively, because the talking now starts from a pile of ideas that already exists.$$

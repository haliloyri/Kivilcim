A king slips out of his capital on a winter night and crosses the sea to exile. Behind him the throne is empty, and the people who invited his rival now face a plain question. They have the power to name the next ruler, so what should they demand before the crown changes heads?

[[story]]
On 5 November 1688 William of Orange landed with an army at Torbay, in Devon. His father-in-law, King James II, watched his own army fall apart as William advanced on London, and on 23 December 1688 James went into exile in France. England had changed rulers, but the authors of *Why Nations Fail* argue that this was no ordinary palace switch.

The reason lies in what came before. Through the seventeenth century, Parliament and the Crown had fought over who could tax, who could make law and who could grant monopolies. Acemoglu and Robinson describe a broad coalition, including merchants and landowners, that had grown strong enough to challenge the monarch. For them the Glorious Revolution is the moment when that coalition got to write the terms.

In April 1689 a Convention made William and Mary joint monarchs. The settlement that followed, usually known as the Bill of Rights of 1689, spelled out limits on the Crown: the monarch could not suspend laws or raise taxes without Parliament's consent, elections were to be free, and members of Parliament could speak freely in debate. In the authors' reading, power did not vanish. It moved, and it was spread across more hands than before.

Why does that matter for prosperity? A ruler who can change the law, seize property or hand out monopolies at will makes every investment risky. If Parliament must agree, a merchant or a farmer can count on rules that outlast a single king. The authors argue that this security helped open the way to economic growth in Britain over the following century.

The easy mistake is to think the lesson is about who sits on the throne. Kings changed in 1688 and again later, and the lasting change was elsewhere. The new ruler accepted limits, and the people who had arranged the change were the ones who held the pen. A settlement that is written while those who feel the weight of power are in the room is far harder for the next ruler to undo.

Notice also what the authors do not claim. They do not say the men of 1689 were saints, or that the new system was fair to everyone, since the franchise was narrow. Their point is smaller and sturdier: once enough people can block a ruler, the ruler has to bargain, and bargaining slowly widens who counts.

[[lessons]]
$$**When you can choose who holds power, use that moment to write down its limits.** A new manager, a new partner or a new committee chair is usually accepted on trust, and the terms get vaguer the longer you wait. Write the boundaries while your say still matters: who decides about money, who can overrule whom, and how a mistake gets corrected. A limit agreed at the start feels like part of the deal, while the same limit proposed later feels like an accusation, and it keeps the next disagreement from turning into a fight about trust.$$

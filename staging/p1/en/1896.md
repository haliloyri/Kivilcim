A surgeon is willing to scratch a child's arm, but only if strangers watch. He sets one condition before he touches her: witnesses must be present and must see exactly what happens. The mother agrees. Outside, in the city streets, an epidemic of smallpox is spreading.

[[story]]
Lady Mary Wortley Montagu had seen the procedure before. In Constantinople, where her husband was the British ambassador, her young son had been inoculated against smallpox by an old Greek practitioner, with the embassy's surgeon, Charles Maitland, assisting. Maitland had come out as surgeon to the ambassador in 1717. A little of the matter from a mild case of the disease was introduced into a small cut, and the patient caught the disease in a milder form.

In 1721 the family was back in London and smallpox was raging. Lady Mary asked Maitland to inoculate her daughter. Maitland agreed, on one condition: there had to be observers. In April 1721 he performed the inoculation before witnesses.

One child, however well watched, is a single case. Within months a larger test followed.

In August 1721 six prisoners in Newgate prison, condemned to death, were offered a pardon if they would take part. Maitland was reluctant at first and agreed after Sir Hans Sloane, the physician, spoke to him. He inoculated all six. Five of them came down with mild smallpox, and the sixth showed no pustules at all. The man later said he had already had smallpox six months earlier. The prisoners were duly pardoned.

Six people is a small number, and nobody at the time could have called the test a proof. But five mild cases and one unexplained blank, among people who had every reason to take the risk, was a sight worth showing others. The experiment was judged a success. In April 1722 two daughters of the Prince of Wales, the future George II, were inoculated as well, and neither suffered ill effects. A practice that began in a room in Constantinople had reached the royal family.

Two details stand out. The first is the sixth prisoner. The test turned up a man who could not be counted as a result either way, and His own explanation went into the record. A report that explains its odd case is more convincing than one that has none.

The second is the condition set on the first day. Maitland did not ask anyone to trust him. He asked them to watch. Every step after that, from the daughter to the prisoners to the princesses, widened the circle of people who had seen with their own eyes and could say so.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you ask people to trust something new, ask some of them to come and watch.** A cafe owner who wants to change the opening hours will get further by inviting a few regulars to see a week of the new schedule than by announcing it and defending it afterwards. Witnesses turn your claim into something they saw, and what they saw travels further than what you told them. Start with a small circle, show the odd result along with the good ones, and widen it step by step. People who have watched you handle the awkward case honestly will vouch for the rest.$$

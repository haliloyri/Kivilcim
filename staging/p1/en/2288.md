You make something, read it back and wince. You can see exactly what is wrong with it, down to the sentence, and the sight is so clear that you wonder whether you should be doing this at all. The wince, it turns out, says nothing about a lack of talent. It marks a stage that comes before the talent shows up.

[[story]]
Ira Glass, the host of the public radio programme *This American Life*, gave a short talk on storytelling that spread across the internet as a video. The part people share most is about beginners, and it begins with a complaint: nobody tells this to people who are starting out, and he wishes someone had told him.

What nobody tells them, Glass says, is that you get into creative work because you have good taste. Your taste is the reason you wanted to make things at all. You have watched enough, read enough and listened enough to know what a good piece feels like. Then you start making your own and it is not good. It is close to what you admire, but it falls short, and that shortfall hurts.

Glass names it the gap. Your taste is ahead of your ability, and for a while your work is not as good as what you can recognise. Your taste, in other words, is the very reason your own work disappoints you. He adds that many people who do interesting creative work went through years of this.

His remedy is plain. The gap closes only by going through a volume of work. Thinking, waiting for a better idea or buying better tools will not close it. Producing a lot, imperfectly, lets the ability climb toward the taste. That is also why the advice is hard to follow: producing a volume means producing a lot of work you already know is not good enough.

Notice what this does to the feeling of disappointment. If the disappointment shows that your standards are high, it becomes a normal stage, and the sensible response is to keep going. If you read it as proof that you have no talent, the sensible response is to stop. The same feeling can end a craft or sustain it, depending on which sentence you tell yourself about it.

Newport's argument about skill and passion has the same shape. Skill is built by long, unglamorous work, and the satisfaction tends to arrive late. Glass describes the stretch before it arrives from the inside: the part where you can see the problem clearly and cannot yet fix it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Treat the pain of your own early work as the taste you are developing.** When your output disappoints you, you are measuring it against a standard you have already learned to see, and that means the distance is closing from one side. Decide in advance how much you will produce, for example one piece every week for three months, and judge the stretch by quantity before quality. Show the work to one person who can tell you what is wrong. Keep the pile, because later you will read the early pieces and see how far you came, which is the kind of evidence a bad mood cannot argue with.$$

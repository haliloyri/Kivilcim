Picture a small rider perched on top of a six-ton elephant. The rider holds the reins and appears to be in control. But if the elephant decides to go somewhere else, the rider can't stop it. Chip and Dan Heath believe this is what happens in your head every time you try to change.

[[story]]
In *Switch*, Chip and Dan Heath borrow a metaphor from psychologist Jonathan Haidt. Our minds, they explain, have two sides. The emotional, instinctive side is the **Elephant**. The rational, reflective side is the **Rider**.

The Rider thinks ahead, makes plans and knows what we "should" do. The Elephant provides the energy and drive, but it wants comfort and quick rewards. When they agree, we move easily. When they disagree for long, the Elephant usually wins. That's why we know we should exercise, save money or finish the project, and still find ourselves on the couch.

Each side also has its weaknesses. The Elephant is impatient and easily discouraged. The Rider, surprisingly, has its own flaw: it tends to overanalyze, to spin its wheels, to get lost in options and ambiguity.

The Heaths add a third element: the **Path**, the environment the Elephant and Rider travel through. Sometimes the easiest way to change behavior isn't to change the person at all, but to change the situation.

##So their framework for change has three parts: direct the Rider, motivate the Elephant, shape the Path.##

One of their most memorable examples of directing the Rider comes from West Virginia. Two health researchers, including Steve Booth-Butterfield, wanted people to eat more healthily. But "eat healthier" is vague. It leaves the Rider with too many choices and no clear starting point.

So the researchers focused on one very specific change. They noticed that whole milk was one of the largest sources of saturated fat in many people's diets. Their campaign asked for just one switch: buy 1% milk instead of whole milk. Ads, press events and taste tests all pushed this single, clear behavior.

In the campaign area, the market share of low-fat milk rose significantly.

The lesson the Heaths draw is striking: what looks like resistance is often a lack of clarity. People didn't refuse to eat healthier. They just didn't know exactly what to do. Give the Rider a specific, concrete step, and change becomes much easier.

The same logic applies to the other two parts. Give the Elephant a reason to care, something that makes people feel the need for change. And shape the Path so the right behavior is the easy one.

[[lessons]]
$$**Change needs reason and emotion.** The Rider can plan, but the Elephant supplies the energy. If you only appeal to logic, the Elephant may simply refuse to move. Lasting change usually needs both: a clear plan and a feeling that makes you want to follow it.$$

$$**Resistance is often confusion.** The 1% milk campaign worked because it replaced a vague goal with one concrete action. When people seem to resist change, the Heaths suggest checking first whether they actually know what to do. A specific first step often dissolves what looked like stubbornness.$$

$$**Shape the Path.** Sometimes the easiest way to change behavior is to change the environment: remove temptations, add reminders, make the right choice the convenient one. Instead of relying only on willpower, you let the path do some of the work for you. Willpower gets a rest.$$

[[reflect]]
- Where does your Rider know exactly what to do while your Elephant refuses to move?
- Which change you want lacks a clear, specific first step?
- How could you reshape your environment so the right choice becomes the easy one?

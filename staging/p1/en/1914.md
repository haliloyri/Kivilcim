A stack of letters written by a man who tried to take the throne is burned, and the emperor has refused to read a single line of them. Reading them might have shown him who else had stood with the rebel.

[[story]]
Marcus Aurelius is one of the Stoics who anchor Ryan Holiday and Stephen Hanselman's *The Daily Stoic*, and this episode comes from the late years of his reign. The man at its centre is Avidius Cassius, a general Marcus had trusted for years. Cassius had won victories in the Parthian war, taken Seleucia and Ctesiphon, and become governor of Syria in 166.

In mid-175, false reports reached Cassius that Marcus had died. He declared himself emperor, with Egypt, Syria and Arabia behind him and an army of seven legions. The Senate in Rome declared him a public enemy. Marcus, who was then fighting on the northern frontier, broke off his war against the Iazyges and turned east.

According to the historian Cassius Dio, Marcus spoke to his soldiers about the revolt. He said he was saddened by the betrayal of a friend, and that he hoped Cassius would neither die nor take his own life, because he wanted to show him mercy. Plenty of rulers say such things. What matters is what followed.

Cassius never faced him. Marcus's larger army was coming, and rumours said he would strike Egypt first, which alarmed Cassius's own soldiers. In late July 175 a centurion and a decurion from Cassius's army killed him and sent his head to Marcus. Marcus did not want to see it and ordered it buried. Egypt recognised him again on 28 July.

Then came the part that tests a ruler. Marcus asked the Senate for a list of Cassius's supporters, but he wanted to avoid bloodshed. All of Cassius's letters were destroyed, and according to the source, Marcus refused to read any of them, so as to avoid pointless vengeance.

The outcomes were not all gentle. Cassius's son Avidius Maecianus was killed, and another son, Avidius Heliodorus, was sent into exile. Cassius's daughter Avidia and her husband were placed in the care of an uncle.

Notice what Marcus did with the information. He could have read every letter, cross-checked every name and punished at leisure. By destroying the evidence unread, he made it impossible for his own anger to find new targets. He used a simple device: he arranged things so that his worst moment could not make decisions for him later.

[[lessons]]
$$**Make the decision about mercy while you are calm, and remove the evidence that would tempt you later.** A manager who has just been undermined by a trusted colleague can choose, before the hurt sharpens, which facts she will not go digging for. A friend who feels betrayed can delete the old messages instead of rereading them each night to feed the grievance. Anger always asks for one more proof, so the surest way to keep a measured response is to refuse it the material it wants. Once you have done that, you can deal with the person in front of you rather than with a growing file of complaints.$$

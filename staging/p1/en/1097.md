[[open]]
Someone starts telling you about a problem, and halfway through their second sentence you already know what they should do. You're nodding and you're ready. Stephen Covey would say that's the moment you stopped listening. He has a small story about an eye doctor that shows exactly what that moment costs, and why the person across from you so often ends up not hearing a word you say.

[[story]]
Habit 5 in Stephen Covey's 1989 book *The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People* is called "Seek First to Understand, Then to Be Understood." He opens the idea with a scene that sounds almost like a joke.

You go to an optometrist and tell him your eyes are bothering you. He listens for a moment, takes off his own glasses and hands them to you. His point is simple: this pair has worked well for him for years, so you should try them. You put them on, and everything gets worse. Your vision is blurrier than when you walked in.

It was a generous gesture. He gave you something that genuinely worked for him. But it failed because he wrote the prescription before he had examined anything. Covey's phrase for what went wrong is short: **diagnose before you prescribe.**

Then he turns the scene on the rest of us. Most people, he writes, don't listen with the intent to understand. They listen with the intent to reply. While the other person is talking, we're running their words through our own experience and loading up an answer. We believe we're listening. We're really rehearsing.

He gives an example of a parent who comes to him and says:

> "I just can't understand my kid. He just won't listen to me."

Read it twice. The parent can't understand the kid, and the complaint is that the kid won't listen. ##The parent wants to be understood without first doing the work of understanding.## It's the optometrist again, except the glasses are a lecture.

Why does the order matter so much? Covey answers with what he calls "psychological air." Imagine the air being sucked out of the room you're sitting in right now. Nothing else would matter. You wouldn't care about the meeting, the argument or the plan. You would only care about getting air. Next to physical survival, he argues, the greatest human need is psychological survival: to be understood, affirmed, validated and appreciated. When someone feels deeply understood, they get that air. Only then can they turn to solving the problem or being influenced by you.

To show how rarely we give that air, Covey lays out levels of listening. At the bottom is *ignoring*. Then comes *pretending*, the "uh-huh, right" while your mind is elsewhere. Then *selective listening*, where you hear only the parts that interest you. Then *attentive listening*, where you're focused on the words. At the top is *empathic listening*, which means stepping into the other person's frame of reference. Only that last level is listening with the intent to understand.

He also names four "autobiographical responses," the reflexes that drag a conversation back to us. We **evaluate**, agreeing or disagreeing. We **probe**, asking questions from our own frame of reference. We **advise**, handing over our glasses. We **interpret**, explaining the other person by our own motives. Each one feels helpful, and each one fills the gaps in their story with ours.

The second half of the habit does come. Being understood still matters. But it means presenting your ideas clearly and in the context of a deep understanding of the other person's view. You earn your turn.

[[lessons]]
$$**Advice before diagnosis is just your own glasses.** The optometrist wasn't careless or unkind. He offered something that truly worked for him. That's what makes the story sting: most bad advice comes from people sincerely sharing what helped them. The patient's eyes got worse because nobody had looked at them. When you prescribe before you diagnose, the other person has no reason to trust the prescription, however good it is.$$

$$**Your helpful reflexes are autobiographical.** Evaluating, probing, advising and interpreting all feel like engagement. Covey's point is that each one is filtered through your own experience, so the conversation quietly becomes about you. Even a question can do it if it's shaped by where *you* want to go. Empathic listening, the top of his five levels, starts when you notice these reflexes and hold them back long enough to see through the other person's frame.$$

$$**People can't hear you until they have air.** The parent who says "he just won't listen to me" is asking the kid to do something the parent hasn't done. Covey's air metaphor explains why that fails: someone who doesn't feel understood is still gasping, and a gasping person can't take in your ideas. Once they feel understood, they have room for solutions and for your influence. Understanding first isn't a detour on the way to being heard. It's the road there.$$

[[reflect]]
- Think of your last disagreement. Which reflex came out first: evaluating, probing, advising or interpreting?
- Who in your life do you privately describe as someone who "won't listen to me"? Could you state their view in their own words, well enough that they'd sign off on it?
- When did someone last hand you their glasses, meaning advice that worked for them but didn't fit you? How did it feel to receive it?
- Where on Covey's ladder do you usually sit with the people closest to you: pretending, selective, attentive or empathic?

&&In your last important conversation, how much of the time were you trying to understand, and how much were you preparing your answer?&&

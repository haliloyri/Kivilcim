[[story]]
David Quammen's *Spillover* follows viruses that jump from animals to people, and one of its early cases is Hendra virus. It begins on 7 September 1994 in the Brisbane suburb of Hendra, when two horses were moved to a racing stable from a paddock in Cannon Hill, about six kilometres away. One of them was a pregnant mare. She fell ill and died within two days.

A week later, on 14 September, a 40-year-old stablehand came down with an influenza-like illness. The next day the stable's trainer, aged 49, developed similar symptoms. According to the 1995 report by the Australian scientists, he had been exposed to the frothy nasal discharge of the dying mare while feeding her, and he had abrasions on his hands and arms. His first signs looked like Legionnaires' disease, so that was the first suspicion. He needed a ventilator for his failing lungs and died after six days. The stablehand was ill for about six weeks, then slowly recovered.

By 26 September thirteen horses had died. The state's veterinary authorities and the CSIRO Australian Animal Health Laboratory were notified on 22 and 23 September, and the lab worked by elimination. Tests ruled out African horse sickness, equine influenza and an acute equine herpesvirus. They ruled out several bacteria and poisons such as paraquat.

Within three days of inoculating cell cultures with tissue from the sick horses, the cells began fusing into clumps, a sign that a virus was growing. The same virus was isolated from the trainer's kidney tissue. Under the electron microscope it belonged to the paramyxovirus family, and its surface projections looked double-fringed, which the authors considered unique. Sequencing showed it was only distantly related to the morbilliviruses, the group that includes measles. Antisera against measles, canine distemper and rinderpest did not neutralise it.

The team had found a virus nobody had described, first called equine morbillivirus and later renamed Hendra virus after the suburb. Later work pointed to flying foxes as its natural hosts. After that outbreak no further cases appeared in horses or people, and the Australian authorities considered the outbreak over.

The scientists did not begin with a brilliant guess. They began with a list of the known causes and crossed them off, one by one, until what was left could only be something new.

[[lessons]]
$$**Cross off the familiar explanations in writing before you accept a new one.** A shop owner whose sales drop suddenly may blame the weather, the season and a rival down the street, and the quickest way out is to test each of those against the figures. When all the usual causes have been ruled out, the leftover is no longer a vague worry but a defined gap that deserves its own attention. A written list of what you have eliminated lets you say with confidence that the problem is really new, and lets you ask for help in the right place.$$

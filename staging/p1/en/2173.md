[[open]]
A diner counter at seven in the morning. The plate arrives with cold toast, lukewarm coffee and an egg that is still raw in the middle. Every person in the kitchen did their own job well, and the customer still got a bad breakfast.

[[story]]
Andy Grove opens *High Output Management* with a breakfast. His opening scene is a restaurant, with no chip factory or boardroom in sight, and its customer orders a meal of a three-minute boiled egg, buttered toast and hot coffee, all served together. It is deliberately ordinary, because anyone can picture it, and because Grove argues that the logic of a kitchen is the logic of every production process, including the ones that run in offices.

Start with the egg. It needs three minutes, and no amount of effort will make it take two. That makes the egg the limiting step, the one stage that sets the pace for everything else, so the schedule has to hang from it: put the egg in first, then time the toast and the coffee so they finish when the egg does. A cook who starts with the toast looks busy and efficient, yet the customer eats it cold. Activity was never the goal.

Grove also looks at value. Bread becomes toast, and toast, egg and coffee together become a breakfast, which is worth more to the customer than the three items served one after another. Each step in a flow adds value, and the customer pays for the finished whole and never sees how hard each station worked. He then weighs a staffing question: should each item have its own person? A specialist per dish can be faster, but it adds overhead that a small restaurant may not be able to afford.

The example then does its real work. Grove carries the same reasoning over to the organizations most readers actually run. A manager's output, in his view, is the output of the team they lead and the teams they influence. A group can be fully occupied and still deliver cold toast, with a report finished before the decision it was meant to inform, or a design completed while the part it depends on is still weeks away.

So the useful question in any process is a plain one. Which step is the egg, and is everything else timed around it? Most teams have an answer to the first half and never ask the second.

[[lessons]]
$$**Find the one step that cannot be sped up and build the whole schedule around it.** In a project, a hiring round or a product launch, there is usually a single slow stage, such as an approval, a supplier or a test run, that decides when the customer gets the result. Working harder on the fast steps only makes them wait, so start the slow step first and time the others to land beside it. When the whole arrives together, the customer gets a breakfast instead of three separate parts, and you get a plan that someone can actually check against the clock.$$

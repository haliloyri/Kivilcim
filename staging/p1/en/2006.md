Early in November, Mexican cemeteries fill up. Families clean the graves and decorate them with bright orange flowers, and beside each one they set out the food and drink their dead loved one liked best. The custom keeps going year after year as a way of remembering the dead again.

[[story]]
Where did the Day of the Dead come from? Scholars disagree. Some trace it to ancient Aztec rites for honoring the dead, while others read it as a local version of the Catholic season of All Saints brought over by the Spanish. Whichever account is right, the holiday as practiced today carries both Indigenous and Catholic elements, and in 2008 UNESCO placed it on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, "as practised by the indigenous communities of Mexico".

It falls on 1 and 2 November. According to published reviews of Oliver Burkeman's *The Antidote*, he went to Mexico to see the celebrations while exploring how people face death.

Families visit cemeteries and clean and decorate the graves. At home they build small altars called ofrendas, laying out the favorite foods and drinks of the people they have lost. The orange flower of the season, the marigold known as cempasúchil, has a Nahuatl name meaning "twenty flowers", and it is thought to draw the souls of the dead toward the offerings.

Solemnity shares these days with play, and the two sit side by side without either one cancelling the other. Friends exchange candy sugar skulls, families share a sweet bread called pan de muerto, and people write playful verses about the dead and about the living. Nobody whispers about death here and then buries the subject again; it becomes a shared job and a shared table.

Look at what the customs have in common: the dead are named, their dishes are cooked, their graves are decorated. Humor and food make mortality ordinary company instead of a stranger at the door. A topic that might otherwise open once, at a funeral, and then stay shut becomes a task that is taken up again every year, with the whole family holding a piece of it.

No conclusion can be pushed too far. There is no measurement showing that a tradition makes people fear death less. But the tradition does show something: remembering mortality can take the form of a dated, shared, tangible action. An action like that may feel less frightening and more doable than the abstract advice to "think about your mortality."

[[lessons]]
$$**Give remembering a date, a table and a small task.** Cooking a loved one's favorite dish on their birthday, telling a memory about them out loud while looking at their photo, or saying the names of the people you have lost once a year with your family are all examples. When death is tied to a concrete task like these, it stops being a subject you avoid and becomes a memory you share. That in turn can lead you to spend time with the people still here more deliberately, and turns remembering into a small promise you keep once a year.$$

In the city where he grew up, a former Wimbledon champion and Olympic gold medallist walks into a minor-league tournament as the 141st player in the world. Few players in that draw have ever held a major trophy. Most of them are chasing ranking points.

[[story]]
In November 1997 Andre Agassi's ranking dropped to No. 141. A Wimbledon champion from 1992 and Olympic gold medallist from 1996, the man who later told his life in the memoir *Open*, had slid far below where his results had once put him. According to the ATP's account of that period, he considered retiring.

He did not retire. At the end of 1997 he returned to the ATP Challenger Tour, the circuit one level below the main tour, where players fight for points in small events. In his hometown, Las Vegas, he reached the final. In Burbank he won a title. Those results were nowhere near a Grand Slam, and that is exactly what made them useful.

A Challenger draw offers something that a major cannot at that moment: matches he could win. Each win is a small, countable piece of evidence, and each one raises the ranking a little, which in turn earns better draws. For a player at 141, going back down the ladder was a way of re-entering the sport at the rung where winning was realistic again.

The climb that followed is a matter of record. On 5 July 1999 he became world No. 1 again, for the first time in nearly three and a half years. He held the top spot for three weeks, took it back in September 1999 after winning his second US Open title, and finished that year ranked first. Along the way he won the French Open in 1999, the title that completed his career Grand Slam.

Look at the proportions. The Challenger results in Las Vegas and Burbank were a final and a title, and the French Open was a Grand Slam title less than two years later. Nothing links the two events except the order they came in, and it would be wrong to say the small wins caused the large one. What can be said is that the decision to play in front of small crowds, at 141, was the first step on the path that ended with him back at No. 1.

There is also a plain point about ego. A player who has stood at the top of the sport risks a lot by losing in public at a lower level. He went there anyway, and the Las Vegas final and the Burbank title were the first wins he could count.

[[lessons]]
$$**Rebuild at the level where winning is realistic, then count the wins.** When confidence has gone, a fresh start at your old level only produces more evidence against you. A cook who lost a restaurant, or an analyst who was moved off a major account, can take a smaller, easier job on purpose, finish it well and see the results pile up. Small results you can really finish change what you believe you are capable of, and that belief is what a bigger step needs. You do not have to feel ready first, only to pick a place where a win is truly possible and then collect it.$$

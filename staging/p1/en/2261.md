[[story]]
Philip Tetlock and Dan Gardner's *Superforecasting* is about people who put numbers on the future. A story from the history of American intelligence shows what happens when they don't. In March 1951 a National Intelligence Estimate on the chances of an invasion of Yugoslavia reached its final paragraph, and there it said that an attack that year "should be considered a serious possibility." Two words carried the whole judgment.

Sherman Kent, who helped run the office that produced these estimates, told what followed in a 1964 essay for the CIA's in-house journal. A few days after the estimate came out, the chairman of the State Department's Policy Planning Staff asked him, almost in passing, what the phrase had meant and what odds the writers had in mind. Kent answered that his own estimate was on the dark side: around 65 to 35 in favour of an attack.

The chairman was jolted. He and his colleagues had read "serious possibility" as odds very considerably lower. Both sides had read the same two words and walked away with different numbers.

Kent then asked the members of his own board what odds they had had in mind when they agreed to the wording. Each had a different figure. The lowest was about 20 to 80, the highest 80 to 20, and the rest fell somewhere between. He and a fellow board member, Max Foster, were shaken even more by this than by the gap with the readers, because the people who had worked over the text together had not understood each other. This was only the twenty-ninth estimate the office had issued, and they began to wonder how many earlier ones hid the same disagreement.

Kent's answer was a chart. "Almost certain" meant about 93 percent, give or take 6. "Probable" meant 75, give or take 12. "Chances about even" meant 50, give or take 10; "probably not" meant 30, give or take 10; "almost certainly not" meant 7, give or take 5. He never persuaded the community to print it in every estimate.

Some of his colleagues, whom he called the "poets," held that in a speculative field a writer can only hope to convey a broad general sense. Kent said plainly that he sided with the "mathematicians" and that the label was meant to carry some disapproval. By his own account he settled for winning converts one at a time, almost fourteen years after the Yugoslavia estimate.

[[lessons]]
$$**Put a number next to every word that has to carry a decision.** When a foreman says a part will "probably" arrive this week, the shop owner may hear almost certain while the foreman means a lean on a coin flip, and the owner promises a customer on that basis. One extra clause from the foreman, such as "call it seventy percent," exposes the gap in the same conversation, while it can still be fixed. It also gives you a way to check yourself afterwards, because a word like "probably" can never be shown wrong, while a figure can. The next time you type "likely" in an email, add the number you would bet at and watch whether the reply matches it.$$

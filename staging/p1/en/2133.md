[[story]]
In *Dopamine Nation*, Anna Lembke, a psychiatrist at Stanford, describes experiments from the late 1960s. Researchers attached a dog's hind paws to an electrical current and wrote down what the animal did. The first shocks terrified it. It screeched, it thrashed, and the researchers noted the other physical signs of raw fear.

The shocks kept coming, session after session. Slowly the terror wore down. The dog still looked pained, annoyed or anxious, but it no longer shrieked. It whined.

Then came the part Lembke dwells on. When the dog was released at the end of a session, it ran around, jumped on the people nearby and wagged its tail. The researchers had a name for this: "a fit of joy." She adds, without softening it, that such experiments would not be allowed today.

Her reading of the data is plain. With repeated exposure, the pain became shorter and weaker, and the pleasure that followed it became longer and stronger. Nothing about the dog's situation had improved in any way the researchers describe; it was still being shocked. What shifted was how its system answered.

Lembke explains this with a picture she uses throughout the book. Pleasure and pain are handled in overlapping parts of the brain, and they behave like the two ends of a seesaw. Whenever something pushes one end down, the brain works to level the plank again, and it tends to push past level before it settles.

That fits an idea psychologists Richard Solomon and John Corbit proposed in the 1970s, the opponent-process theory. In their 1974 paper, dogs in a harness receiving ten-second shocks appear among the examples of a body that answers a jolt in one direction with a push in the other, and the push grows with repetition.

Lembke carries the same logic over to pleasure, where the push runs the opposite way. A reward tips the brain's balance toward pleasure, and the brain answers by tipping it back toward pain, and that answer also strengthens with repetition. She closes the dog story with a question rather than a verdict. If repeated pain can leave a stronger pleasure behind it, could pressing on the pain side on purpose produce a more lasting kind of satisfaction?

[[lessons]]
$$**Watch the minutes after a pleasure, because the aftertaste shows which way your balance is moving.** Take someone who scrolls videos every evening and notices that the flat, restless feeling when the screen goes dark has been getting heavier. That feeling is information, and it is easy to miss when all your attention goes to the pleasure itself. Name it in a single word each night for a week, and you can tell enjoying something from needing it before the habit starts pushing you around, and you will have a plain, honest signal for when a pause is worth trying.$$

[[story]]
In the autumn of 1954, a Chicago housewife named Dorothy Martin told a small circle of followers that she was receiving messages through automatic writing. The messages said that a great flood would destroy much of the world before dawn on 21 December, and that a visitor would arrive at midnight to take the faithful to a flying saucer. Newspapers printed the prediction.

Three psychologists, Leon Festinger, Henry Riecken and Stanley Schachter, saw the story as a rare chance. They had a group that had publicly committed itself to a date, and they wanted to watch what happened when the date passed. Observers joined the group posing as interested believers, and at least one was in the house on the critical night. The researchers' book, *When Prophecy Fails*, appeared in 1956 and called the group's leader "Marian Keech".

On the evening of 20 December the believers gathered. In the researchers' account they removed the metal from their clothing and waited for the visitor who was meant to come at midnight. No one came. Midnight passed, then the small hours. The prediction was in the newspapers by then, so the group had gone public with a date and left itself little room to retreat.

At about a quarter to five in the morning, Martin received a new message. The group's vigil, it said, had spread so much light that God had spared the world. According to the researchers, the mood changed within hours. A group that had mostly avoided reporters began phoning newspapers and giving interviews, and it tried to recruit.

Festinger drew a theory from this. When people hold a belief, have taken costly and hard-to-undo actions because of it, and have others around them who share it, clear evidence against it can push them to believe harder. Later in the same decade he developed these ideas into his theory of cognitive dissonance.

The book has lost some of its shine. One critic argued that the observers became part of the events they recorded, and another that the shift toward publicity was less sudden than the book's story suggests. In 2025, a paper in the *Journal of the History of the Behavioral Sciences* argued that the researchers encouraged the group's beliefs during moments of doubt and that the book overstated the result. Even its critics do not dispute the plain scene: people who had paid heavily for a belief found a way to keep it.

[[lessons]]
$$**The more you have given up for a decision, the harder it becomes to hear evidence against it, so judge your commitments before the cost mounts.** Someone who has spent a year and a lot of savings on a business idea will tend to explain away each weak sign, because accepting it means admitting the whole spend was a mistake. The price paid works as a vote for staying, whatever the facts say. Decide beforehand which signals would make you stop, write them down, and let a trusted person hold you to them when the moment arrives. That is not distrust of yourself, only a precaution.$$

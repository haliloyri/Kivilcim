The tickets are printed. The announcements are out. Then a letter arrives: the rent for the hall you've booked is about to nearly triple. Most people would be on the phone within minutes, angry and ready to fight. Dale Carnegie did something else, and it worked far better.

[[story]]
In *How to Win Friends and Influence People*, Dale Carnegie tells of renting the grand ballroom of a New York hotel for a series of lectures each season. One season, just before the lectures were due to begin, he was informed that the rent would be almost three times what he'd paid before. The tickets had already been printed and distributed. Everything was set.

Naturally, he didn't want to pay. But he also knew that telling the hotel what *he* wanted wouldn't get him anywhere. The hotel had its own interests.

So a couple of days later, he went to see the manager. He began calmly. He said he'd been a bit shocked by the letter, but he didn't blame the manager at all. If he were in the manager's position, he said, he'd probably have written a similar letter. It was the manager's job to make the hotel as profitable as possible.

Then he took out a sheet of paper and drew a line down the middle.

##On one side he wrote the advantages of raising the rent, and on the other the disadvantages, all from the hotel's point of view.##

The advantage was clear: without Carnegie's lectures, the ballroom would be free to rent out for dances and conventions, which would pay more.

Then came the disadvantages. The hotel would lose the income from Carnegie's lectures entirely, because he couldn't pay the new rate and would have to go elsewhere. And there was something else. Carnegie's courses brought thousands of educated, cultured people into the hotel. That was valuable publicity. The hotel would have had to spend a great deal on advertising to bring in that many people. Now it was getting them for free.

He handed the paper to the manager and asked him to consider both sides and let him know his decision.

The next day, a letter arrived. The rent would go up by 50 percent, not 300.

Carnegie points out what he *didn't* do. He never said a word about what he wanted. He talked the whole time about what the hotel wanted, and how it could get it. Had he stormed in and argued, the manager would have been forced to defend himself, and pride would have made backing down almost impossible.

The principle he draws is simple: try honestly to see things from the other person's point of view.

[[lessons]]
$$**Arguing makes people defend themselves.** If Carnegie had stormed in angry, the manager's pride would have made backing down nearly impossible. By starting with understanding, "I'd probably have done the same," he removed the need for defense and made a real conversation possible.$$

$$**Make your case in their terms.** The two-column sheet was entirely about the hotel's interests: lost income, lost publicity. Carnegie didn't plead his own needs. People are moved by what matters to them. Showing someone how an option serves their goals is far more persuasive than explaining why it serves yours.$$

$$**Let people decide.** Carnegie left the paper and asked the manager to consider it. He didn't demand an answer on the spot. People are much more willing to change their minds when the conclusion feels like their own, reached in their own time. Patience pays.$$

[[reflect]]
- In a disagreement you're facing, what does the other side actually want?
- How could you state your case entirely in terms of their interests?
- When did arguing make someone dig in harder against you?

&&If you drew Carnegie's two columns for your situation, what would you write on their side of the sheet?&&

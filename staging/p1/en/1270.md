Tom had done everything a man is supposed to do after coming home from a war. He had become a lawyer. He had started a family. And every year, one ordinary date on the calendar filled him with dread: the Fourth of July.

[[story]]
Bessel van der Kolk opens *The Body Keeps the Score* with Tom. In 1978, early in his career, van der Kolk was working at the Veterans Administration clinic in Boston, and Tom was one of the Vietnam veterans who came to see him.

On paper, Tom's story looked like a recovery. He had returned to civilian life, built a law career and started a family. Anyone looking from outside would have seen a man who had put the war behind him.

The Fourth of July told a different story. The fireworks, the summer heat, even the lush green of the season pulled him straight back to the war. These weren't things he chose to think about. They were ordinary cues of an American summer, and they set off nightmares and flashbacks that tormented him.

The trouble wasn't limited to one holiday. Tom felt emotionally shut off from his own family. At times he was overwhelmed by a rage he couldn't explain, to them or to himself.

Tom was not the only veteran van der Kolk was trying to understand. He gave many of them the Rorschach test, the set of ambiguous inkblots that people are asked to describe. Many of the veterans saw scenes from the war in the blots. Shapes that could have been anything became the war again.

Others gave a stranger answer. They saw nothing at all. Van der Kolk read this as its own kind of damage, as if these men could no longer imagine anything new. One group filled every empty space with the past; the other had shut down so completely that nothing came through.

From cases like these, van der Kolk drew the central idea of his book.

##Trauma is not only a memory of something that happened; it is re-lived in the body, which keeps reacting as if the danger were still present.##

That explained what Tom's life looked like. The war was years behind him and his circumstances had changed completely. His body, answering fireworks and heat and green leaves, hadn't registered the change.

It also pointed van der Kolk in a new direction. Talking alone often did not change these bodily reactions. A veteran could describe what had happened, and his body would still respond to the next set of fireworks as it always had. That gap between understanding and reacting led him to look at approaches that work through the body itself, a search that shapes the rest of the book.

[[lessons]]
$$**A reaction can outlast the situation that created it.** Tom had a career and a family years after Vietnam, and ordinary summer cues still brought the war back. An outwardly settled life doesn't mean the body has caught up. What someone has built and what they still carry can be very different things.$$

$$**The body can answer a cue before the mind weighs in.** Fireworks, heat and greenery set off reactions Tom couldn't reason his way out of. That is why arguing with a strong reaction in the moment rarely works: the response has already started somewhere logic doesn't reach.$$

$$**Shutting down is a response too.** Some veterans saw war scenes in the inkblots; others saw nothing at all. The quiet, blank reaction is easy to miss, but van der Kolk treated it as seriously as the vivid one. Someone who goes silent may be struggling as much as someone who flares up.$$

[[reflect]]
- Is there a sound, smell or season that changes your mood before you've thought about why?
- Have you seen someone react strongly to something that seemed minor to you?
- When someone near you goes quiet rather than angry, how do you usually read it?

&&Next time someone near you reacts far more strongly than the moment seems to call for, could you ask about it later, calmly, instead of arguing with the reaction as it happens?&&

[[open]]
Two boys sit on the ice of a frozen river, throwing knucklebones. One hands the other a knucklebone from a roebuck, and the other gives back one made of brass. Nobody signs anything and nobody watches, yet the trade is meant to bind them for life.

[[story]]
Jack Weatherford's *Genghis Khan and the Making of the Modern World* follows a boy named Temujin to the head of the Mongol nations. This story stops much earlier, on a river in winter, with a friendship that the *Secret History of the Mongols* records in unusual detail. In Paul Kahn's English adaptation of that thirteenth-century text, Temujin is eleven when he meets a boy named Jamukha.

They play knucklebone dice on the frozen Onan River. Jamukha gives Temujin a knucklebone from a roebuck, and Temujin gives Jamukha one made of brass. With that swap they become *anda*, sworn brothers, and the text says they become so for good.

When spring comes, the two shoot arrows in the forest. Jamukha hands over a whistling arrowhead he made himself from two pieces of calf-horn, bored through and glued together. Temujin answers with an arrow tipped in cypress wood. It looks like a children's game of swapping toys, but each gift is a thing the giver made or owned, and each is given away.

Later, as grown men, the two renew the pledge. They recall what the elders used to say: when two men become anda, their lives become one. Temujin fastens a golden belt, taken from the spoils of a beaten enemy, around Jamukha's waist and gives him a light yellow mare. Jamukha answers with a golden belt of his own, taken from another defeated enemy, and a whitish-tan warhorse.

They swear before the cliffs of Khuldaghar, beneath a great branching tree, and hold a feast. They sleep under one blanket. For a year, the text says, they loved each other. When half of the second year had passed, they agreed to move camp.

That sentence is where the story turns, because the friendship did not last. According to published accounts, Jamukha later became Temujin's enemy, and in 1201 the leaders of thirteen hostile tribes elected him gur-khan. The same sworn brother who once gave away a knucklebone and a war horse ended up at the head of an alliance against him.

What is worth seeing is how the bond was built. It began with small objects, then grew into belts and horses, and every step was witnessed by a place: a river, a forest, a cliff, a tree. The pledge was not a feeling that the two boys had in common. It was a series of acts that each of them could point to. And the second ceremony shows that the oldest bond in the story still needed renewing once the two had become men with followers, enemies and plunder of their own.

[[lessons]]
$$**A bond lasts longer when you renew it in the open, at the size of the life you now lead.** A friendship from school or a first job can quietly turn into a habit of assuming, because nobody has said out loud what they owe each other since things changed. Say a former teammate now runs her own business and you still treat each other as you did years ago: if nothing has been spoken or done in the new circumstances, the old pledge is only a memory. A small, concrete act that fits today, such as an introduction, a visit or an agreed promise, tells you both what the bond still means.$$

Picture a list of everything that makes up your life: your home, your work, your clothes, your name, the people you love. Now imagine someone crossing it out, line by line, with no way for you to stop them. Viktor Frankl didn't have to imagine it. He watched almost the whole list disappear. At the very bottom, though, there was one line nobody could cross out.

[[story]]
Stephen Covey opens Habit 1 of *The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People*, the habit he calls "Be Proactive," with Frankl's story. The way he tells it, it's a story about a man whose training should have told him he had no chance.

Frankl was a psychiatrist. He had been schooled in a deterministic, Freudian way of seeing people. In that view, whatever happens to you early in life shapes you, and you spend the rest of your life living out that conditioning. On paper, you are the product of what's been done to you.

Then a great deal was done to him. Frankl was imprisoned in the Nazi death camps. His parents died there. So did his brother, and so did his wife. Of his whole family, only his sister survived. He was starved, deprived and humiliated, and he had no say over any of it. His captors decided what he ate, where he stood, what happened to his body and whether he lived through the day.

By his own deterministic logic, that should have been the end of the story. A man stripped that bare should be nothing but his circumstances.

In that "naked" condition, as Covey puts it, Frankl became aware of something. He later called it "the last of the human freedoms." Paraphrased, it runs something like this:

> They can control my environment. They can do what they want to my body.
> But how I respond to all of it, inside, is still mine to decide.

##Everything around him belonged to his captors. The space inside him, where he chose how to respond, did not.##

Covey describes that space as a gap between stimulus and response. Something happens to you, and then you respond. Between the two there is a gap, and the freedom to choose your response lives there.

Frankl didn't just notice that freedom. He used it. Covey describes him picturing himself after the camps, standing in front of students and lecturing about what he had learned in that place. In his mind he was already a teacher, not only a prisoner. Using his inner freedom this way, he also helped the people around him find meaning in their own suffering.

Covey builds Habit 1 on this story. Proactivity, he says, starts with responsibility, which he splits into "response-ability": the ability to choose your response. It means you stop blaming circumstances, conditions or your own conditioning for how you act.

He then draws a line between two kinds of people. Reactive people are driven by their feelings, by circumstances and by how others treat them. Proactive people are driven by values they have chosen. He sums it up with an image about weather. Reactive people feel good when the sun is out and bad when it rains. Proactive people, he says, "carry their own weather with them."

[[lessons]]
$$**Your freedom lives in the gap.** The camps controlled nearly everything about Frankl: his food, his movements, his body and who in his family lived or died. What he found was narrow but real, the moment between what was done to him and how he answered it inside. That gap doesn't make hard things less hard. It means they don't get the final word on who you are.$$

$$**Responsibility means response-ability.** Frankl had every reason to say his conditions made him what he was, and his Freudian training would have backed him up. Covey turns "responsibility" into the ability to choose your response and makes it the foundation of Habit 1. Owning your response isn't the same as blaming yourself for what happened to you. It means refusing to let conditions or conditioning do your choosing for you.$$

$$**Carry your own weather.** A reactive person's mood follows the sky, the traffic and whoever spoke to them last. Frankl did more than hold steady. He pictured himself teaching students about what he'd learned, and that gave his suffering a purpose that reached other people. Values you've chosen can set your inner climate even when the outer one is brutal.$$

[[reflect]]
- Think of a situation that sets off the same automatic reaction in you every time. What's the stimulus, and what's the usual response?
- Where are you currently telling yourself that someone else, or your circumstances, "made" you feel a certain way?
- Frankl pictured himself teaching students. What value would you want steering your response in place of the trigger?
- Whose weather are you carrying around this week: yours, or someone else's?

&&Next time that trigger hits, if you took one breath in the gap first, what response would you choose on purpose?&&

[[story]]
Bill Bryson's *The Body: A Guide for Occupants* tells the story of Werner Forssmann, a young German surgeon who became convinced in 1929 that a thin tube could be threaded through a vein all the way into the human heart. Nobody would let him try it on a patient. So he tried it on himself.

He worked at a hospital in Eberswalde, near Berlin. He cut into the crease of his left elbow, found the vein he wanted and fed in a ureteral catheter 65 centimetres long, the only tube he judged thin and long enough for the job. Then he walked. Gerda Ditzen, a nurse at the hospital, helped him hide the tube dangling from his arm, and the two of them went down to the fluoroscopy room in the basement.

The first image was a disappointment: the tube had not yet reached the heart. Forssmann pushed it further in. A technician took a second picture, and this one settled it. The tip of the catheter sat in his right atrium, one of the heart's chambers, and the man it belonged to was standing there to see it.

That photograph was the whole point. For the first time anyone could see, in black and white, that a tube could be placed in a living human heart. He had not argued the case. He had produced an image that anyone could hold up to the light.

The reception was cold. His mentor had opposed the idea, and much of the medical world judged the method too dangerous. He published his report in *Klinische Wochenschrift* in November 1929, and according to one account the formal reason for his dismissal without notice was the publication itself. Forssmann stopped his self-experiments and ended up working as a urologist in a small community, far from the research that his idea would soon feed.

Others picked the method up. In 1956 the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine went to Forssmann, André Cournand and Dickinson Richards, the last two of Columbia University in New York. Twenty-seven years had passed since the afternoon in the basement.

Look at what the image did and did not do. It ended the question of whether a catheter could reach a living heart, and it did so using the one body whose risk Forssmann alone was entitled to take. It did not end the question of whether the method was safe for others, and that was the question his colleagues kept asking. Evidence can win one argument quickly and the next one only slowly.

[[lessons]]
$$**Turn your idea into something people can look at, then wait for the verdict.** A baker who thinks a new recipe will sell can argue about it for weeks, or put a tray of it by the till and watch what disappears. A visible result moves a discussion from opinion to observation, and it gives you something to point at when the doubters speak up. It also teaches you patience, because a clear picture can still take years to be accepted, and the people who decide may care about different risks than you do. Plan for that gap before you start, so that a slow answer does not read as a wrong one.$$

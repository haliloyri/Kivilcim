Cocktails are poured, a bowl of cashews sits on the table, and dinner is still a while away. The nuts disappear at a rapid rate. The host picks up the bowl and carries it off, and his guests thank him for it. Hardly anyone in the room can say why that makes sense.

[[story]]
In *Misbehaving*, Richard Thaler goes back to a dinner party he gave in the 1970s, when he was a graduate student in economics. His guests were fellow students. He had set out cocktails and a bowl of cashews while the meal was still being prepared, and the nuts went fast.

People devoured the cashews at a rapid rate, and Thaler worried that his friends would fill up before dinner. So he took the bowl away and put it in the pantry. His guests thanked him, glad to have room left for a proper meal.

For a roomful of economists in training, that thank-you was a problem. Standard theory holds that more choices can never leave you worse off, because you can always decline the extra option. Here were people made happier by losing one. They started analysing the episode over the empty table, and nobody could make the first principle they had all been taught, that more choices always make you better off, fit what had just happened.

Years later, in his Nobel lecture, Thaler gave the answer plainly. The guests had been worried that with the cashews within reach they would eat too many. It was a question of willpower. He compared the move to Odysseus, who had his crew tie him to the mast so he could not steer toward the Sirens. Cashews were a smaller danger than rocks, so a pantry shelf did the work of the rope. Had the nuts been more tempting, he added, flushing them down the toilet might have been needed. A more farsighted host, he admits, would have served a smaller portion at the start and let everyone eat it to the last nut.

Thaler has joked that where Newton had an apple, he had cashews. The scene fed the model he built with Hersh Shefrin, published in 1981, which pictures a person as a farsighted Planner and a shortsighted Doer. The Planner has two tools. One is commitment, putting the bowl out of reach before the first handful. The other is guilt, which works like a tax on every bite and drains some of the pleasure from it. Guilt costs more, and it is what you are left with when no pantry is available.

[[lessons]]
$$**Move the temptation instead of arguing with yourself.** When you know you will overdo something, changing what sits within reach works better than promising yourself to stop. A freelancer who keeps losing evenings to her phone can charge it in the hallway before dinner, and from then on there is nothing to negotiate, just as someone who snacks all afternoon can leave the packet in the car. Each thing you rearrange in advance is one less fight for your willpower to win, so pick the item you reliably overdo and make the second helping take effort.$$

[[story]]
In 1991, Tony Buzan co-founded the World Memory Championships together with the chess grandmaster Raymond Keene. It was an unusual idea: a competition in which people would memorize things as fast and accurately as possible. Shuffled decks of cards. Long strings of random numbers. Names and faces. Poems. Historical dates.

The results can be astonishing. Top competitors memorize the order of a full deck of cards in very short times, and recall long strings of numbers after only brief study. Records are broken regularly.

It's tempting to assume these people were simply born with extraordinary brains. But most competitors say the opposite. Their skill comes largely from training, and from techniques that are thousands of years old.

The most famous is the **method of loci**, often called the memory palace. You picture a place you know well, perhaps your home or your route to work. Then you place vivid, often absurd images representing the things you want to remember at specific spots along the way. To recall them, you simply walk through the place in your mind and "see" what you left there.

The technique goes back to ancient Greece. According to legend, the poet Simonides was at a banquet when he stepped outside, moments before the hall collapsed. The victims were unrecognizable. But Simonides found he could identify them by remembering where each guest had been sitting. Place, he realized, is a powerful anchor for memory. Roman orators later used similar methods to remember long speeches, walking through imagined buildings as they spoke.

##Brain-imaging research on memory champions has suggested that they generally don't have unusual brains or unusually high intelligence. What they have is training.##

When scanned while memorizing, champions showed more activity in regions linked to spatial memory and navigation, consistent with using memory palaces.

For Buzan, this was the whole point. He believed that most people underestimate their own memory, often because they were never taught how to use it. Images, color, exaggeration, emotion and location make things memorable. Plain lists do not. His Mind Maps use some of the same principles on paper.

The message of the championships is encouraging: memory is not only a gift you have or don't have. It's a skill, and it can be trained.

[[lessons]]
$$**Memory is a skill, not just a gift.** The research suggests memory champions are mostly ordinary people with extraordinary training. That reframes the belief "I have a bad memory." Often it simply means "I was never taught memory techniques," which is something anyone can change with practice.$$

$$**Images and places are memorable.** The memory palace works because our brains are good at remembering locations and vivid pictures. Turning abstract information into strange images placed along a familiar route makes it far easier to recall than repeating a list.$$

$$**Old techniques still work.** A method from ancient Greece still wins modern championships. Simonides and the Roman orators discovered something real about how memory works. Sometimes the best learning tools aren't new apps but very old ideas. They are simple enough to try tonight, with nothing more than a shopping list and a familiar room.$$

[[reflect]]
- Where do you tell yourself that you have a bad memory, and is that really true?
- What familiar place, like your home or your commute, could become your memory palace?
- What would you most like to remember better: names, facts, speeches or something else?

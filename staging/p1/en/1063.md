Think of the habit you've meant to start for months. Here's a guess. It didn't die in week three, when things got hard. It died in the first minute, before any real effort began. It died somewhere between *I should really do this* and the first thing your hands would actually do. James Clear has a rule aimed right at that minute, and it is almost insultingly small.

[[story]]
In *Atomic Habits*, published in 2018, James Clear has a chapter on stopping procrastination. It sits under the law he calls "Make It Easy," and in it he lays out something he calls the Two-Minute Rule. The whole thing fits in one line:

> When you start a new habit, it should take less than two minutes to do.

That's it. At first it sounds like a trick for people who aren't serious. But look at the examples he gives, because that's where it clicks.

"Read before bed each night" becomes "Read one page."
"Do thirty minutes of yoga" becomes "Take out my yoga mat."
"Study for class" becomes "Open my notes."
"Fold the laundry" becomes "Fold one pair of socks."
"Run three miles" becomes "Tie my running shoes."

Notice what's happening. He isn't shrinking the goal. He's pointing at the first physical action, the moment where you either begin or don't. Clear calls these small starting actions **gateway habits**. They're the doorway into the real behavior. Once you've opened the notes or tied the shoes, keeping going is much easier than it was a minute ago. Starting is the hard part.

Then comes the part people find strange. Clear describes a man who went to the gym regularly, with one odd condition. He wasn't allowed to stay longer than five minutes. He'd show up, do a little, and leave. On the surface that looks pointless. What kind of workout is five minutes?

But the workout wasn't the point. The point was to become someone who never misses a workout, and to build that identity before the workouts themselves were long. He was practicing showing up, not practicing lifting.

##A habit must be established before it can be improved.##

That's Clear's argument in a sentence. You can't optimize a routine that doesn't exist yet. Before you worry about the perfect running plan or the ideal reading list, you have to master the habit of showing up. The two-minute version isn't a lesser habit. It's the foundation the bigger one gets built on.

And there's a second layer, which is about who you're becoming. Clear links the rule to identity. Every small action casts a vote for the kind of person you want to be. One page read is a vote for "I'm a reader." Tied shoes on a Tuesday is a vote for "I'm someone who runs." None of those votes is impressive alone. Stacked up, they change how you see yourself, and that shift makes the full habit feel natural instead of forced.

He doesn't suggest staying at two minutes forever, either. The idea is to move step by step. You start with a very easy version, get it firmly in place, and then build toward the full habit over time. The easy version comes first, and the rest follows from it.

So here's the tension the rule resolves. Ambitious habits usually fail at the start, not in the middle. Yet most of us keep defining our habits by the full, effortful goal, the three miles or the thirty minutes. Then we're surprised when we can't get through the door.

[[lessons]]
$$**Name the first two minutes, not the finish line.** "Read before bed each night" is a goal. "Read one page" is something you can actually do tonight, even exhausted. Clear's list works because every item is a single physical action: take out the mat, open the notes, fold one pair of socks. When a habit won't stick, rewrite it until the first step fits inside two minutes.$$

$$**The gateway does the heavy lifting.** Tying your running shoes isn't exercise, but it's the moment the run becomes likely. That's why Clear calls these gateway habits. The resistance lives at the threshold. Once you're past it, continuing takes far less than starting did. So aim your effort at the door, not at the whole room.$$

$$**Master showing up, then build.** The gym-goer who couldn't stay past five minutes wasn't wasting his time. He was becoming the kind of person who doesn't miss workouts, and each visit was a vote for that identity. Clear's point is that a habit has to be established before it can be improved. If you're fine-tuning a routine you still skip half the time, you're working on the wrong problem.$$

[[reflect]]
- Which habit have you kept putting off because the full version feels like too much to face?
- Picture its gateway. What exactly would your hands be doing in the first two minutes?
- Are you tweaking the plan for something you haven't yet made automatic, like the perfect program for workouts you rarely start?
- If you did only the tiny version every day for a while, what would those small votes start saying about you?

Late at night, hundreds of Paris taxis stand in rows on a wide esplanade beside the Invalides. The passengers they are waiting for are soldiers, not fares. Each driver knows only that the city's military governor has asked for his cab, and that the Germans are close enough for the request to matter.

[[story]]
In the first days of September 1914 the German armies were pressing toward Paris, and General Joseph Gallieni, the city's military governor, needed men at the front fast. Barbara Tuchman's *The Guns of August* carries its story up to the Battle of the Marne, where General Joseph Joffre launched a French counter-attack on 6 September. Gallieni's answer to the shortage of transport was to requisition the taxis of Paris.

According to the Army Museum in Paris, 630 taxis gathered on the Esplanade des Invalides at ten o'clock at night. They picked up men of the 103rd and 104th infantry regiments, four or five soldiers to a cab, and drove them toward Nanteuil-le-Haudouin and Silly-le-Long, to reinforce the French Sixth Army on the left of the line. Published accounts say the drivers were paid by the meter, at a cost to the treasury of about 70,000 francs.

The exact numbers differ from source to source. Some accounts speak of 600 taxis and 6,000 soldiers carried in two trips, others of about 3,000 men, others of more than 650 cars. The core of the story is the same in all of them. The cars came from the city's own streets, and they were put to work the same night.

The museum keeps one of these Renault cabs, built in 1905: it is 1.60 metres wide and weighs 1,100 kilograms, a small vehicle to carry four or five soldiers. It also adds a sober point. Compared with the size of the battle, the number of men the taxis moved was small, and most troops reached the front by rail. The cabs did not win the Marne.

What they did was something else. They became a symbol of France's determination to resist,, and they are still called "les taxis de la Marne". The image carried further than the cargo did: a capital that put its own taxis at the army's disposal and would not wait for the perfect arrangement. The Schlieffen Plan and the German hope of a quick victory were foiled at the Marne, and the taxis stand in the memory of the battle for what ordinary resources could do when someone dared to ask for them.

[[lessons]]
$$**When time is short, use what the city already has, and be honest with yourself about whether the gesture moves things or moves people.** A shop owner whose delivery van breaks down on the busiest day borrows a neighbour's car and brings the first orders round by hand. The extra orders reach only a fraction of the customers, but the regulars tell one another the shop will not let them down. A small, visible act is worth making when the situation needs both: say plainly what it carries in material terms, and use the effect on morale for what it is.$$

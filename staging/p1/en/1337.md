You're halfway through your pitch when a colleague folds their arms. Around the table, the verdict forms in a second: they're not buying it. Joe Navarro, who spent his career reading people for the FBI, would slow that verdict down with a single question. What do this person's arms usually do?

[[story]]
Popular body-language advice tends to work like a phrasebook. Crossed arms mean defensive. A hand on the face means something is being hidden. Learn the list, the promise goes, and you can read anyone in the room.

Joe Navarro worked as an FBI counterintelligence agent, where reading people was part of the job, and in *What Every BODY Is Saying* he warns against exactly that kind of list. A single cue pulled out of its context can mislead you. Folded arms are his kind of cautionary case. The person across the table may be defensive. They may also be cold. Or they may simply find that position comfortable and sit that way in every meeting they attend.

So where does he start instead? With the person, not the gesture. Before interpreting anything, you learn their baseline: how they normally sit, where they tend to look, how they move when they're at ease. Some people fidget constantly. Some hardly move at all. Some cross their arms the moment they settle into a chair. None of that tells you much by itself. It's the backdrop.

##What carries meaning is a departure from that backdrop.## Someone who has been leaning back and chatting suddenly goes still. Their posture shifts right after a particular question. That change is worth noticing, in a way the folded arms on their own never were.

Navarro ties many of these reactions to what he calls the limbic brain and its freeze, flight and fight responses. He is just as clear that those responses don't look the same in everyone. One person's stress shows up as stillness, another's as restlessness, and the same person can react differently in a different situation.

That's why he looks for clusters rather than single gestures. One shift could mean almost anything. Several changes arriving together, right after a specific topic comes up, tell you something more reliable: this subject landed differently from the others.

None of this is a trick to memorize on the way to work. Navarro presents it as a skill built by watching, patiently and in context. The phrasebook promises speed. His method asks for attention first and conclusions later.

Back at the meeting table, the question changes. Not "What do crossed arms mean?" but "Is this how they always sit, and did anything change when a particular point came up?"

[[lessons]]
$$**Normal comes first.** A gesture only means something against what is ordinary for that particular person. Navarro's first step is the baseline: how someone sits, looks and moves when they're at ease. Without it, you're comparing a real person to a generic chart that was never about them.$$

$$**Watch for the shift.** The informative moment is a change, especially one that follows a specific question or topic. A single fidget tells you little. Several changes arriving together, right after a subject comes up, tell you that subject matters in some way, even if they don't yet tell you why.$$

$$**Quick labels breed confident mistakes.** Crossed arms can mean defensiveness, a chilly room or plain comfort. Once you've settled on a label, every other cue tends to get bent to fit it. Holding the label back keeps you open to the ordinary explanation, which is often the right one.$$

[[reflect]]
- Think of someone you see often. How do they sit, speak or move when they're relaxed?
- Recall a time you read someone's expression one way and later learned the reason was ordinary.
- Whose moods would you misjudge most easily if all you had was a chart of gestures?

&&In your next longer conversation, could you notice how the other person looks when relaxed, and then whether anything changes when the topic shifts?&&

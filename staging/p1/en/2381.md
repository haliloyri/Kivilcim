A telephone rings in a rented circus tent. The tent stands beside a factory that sends a thick smell over the canvas, and the engineer who picks up the receiver says something that has no business in an aircraft company. Within weeks the whole secret team is using his joke as its name.

[[story]]
Ben Rich and Leo Janos's *Skunk Works* tells how Lockheed's most secret engineering group began, and this story is about its first job. In 1943 Lockheed had to build a jet fighter for the U.S. Army Air Forces, the XP-80, and the company had no free floor space for a project that was meant to stay hidden. Kelly Johnson, the chief engineer, took a small team and set up in a rented circus tent.

The required schedule was 150 days. According to Lockheed's own history, Johnson's team designed and built the XP-80 in 143, seven days ahead of the requirement.

Next to the tent stood another manufacturing plant, and its smell filled the canvas. One engineer, Irv Culver, was a fan of Al Capp's comic strip *Li'l Abner*, which had a running joke about a foul-smelling place in the woods where a strong drink was brewed from skunks and old shoes. When the phone rang, Culver answered it with "Skonk Works, inside man Culver speaking." Colleagues liked it, and the name spread until it became "Skunk Works."

So the group that later built some of the most famous aircraft of the century took its name from a bad smell in a tent. The origin was as plain as the workplace.

The usual way to hit a hard deadline is to add people, meetings and approvals. Johnson took the project out of the main factory instead and gave it to a small team, and he ran it from a place where the normal routines of a large aircraft company did not reach. A question about a drawing could be answered by the people who owned the drawing.

The seven days of slack matter as much as the speed. A deadline met with a week to spare suggests a team that was working from a plan, not sprinting to cover a late start. The tent was an odd and uncomfortable workshop, and it still delivered an airplane that Lockheed had been asked to produce in five months.

[[lessons]]
$$**Give an urgent project its own small room, away from the normal routines of the organisation.** A shop with a rush order, or a department that must ship a report in a week, usually loses days to its usual habits: the long approval chain, the weekly status meeting, the shared inbox. Moving the few people who do the work into one corner, with the authority to decide on the spot, removes most of that waiting. You end up with a team that spends its hours on the task, and a deadline with some slack left.$$

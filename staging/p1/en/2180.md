A stainless-steel prototype climbs to 12.5 kilometres, then starts down toward its landing pad. On the way down the engines cannot deliver the thrust they need. The impact is hard and an explosion follows. What remains is a pile of flight data and one question that has to be answered: why did the engines fall short?

[[story]]
Walter Isaacson's *Elon Musk* is a biography of the founder of Tesla and SpaceX. This story belongs to SpaceX's flight tests of its Starship prototypes. On 9 December 2020 a prototype called SN8 rose to 12.5 kilometres. During its landing attempt the pressure in the methane header tank was low and the engines could not deliver enough thrust. The vehicle hit the landing pad hard and exploded.

Other prototypes flew after SN8, and the programme kept testing in public. On 5 May 2021 SN15 completed the same manoeuvres and landed safely. A fire that broke out around its engines after landing was put out. In five months the programme had gone from a vehicle that exploded on landing to one that stayed in one piece. After SN15 came a new kind of test: not a single prototype but a full stack, two vehicles on top of each other.

On 20 April 2023 the stack, Starship on top of its Super Heavy booster, lifted off together for the first time. It climbed to about 39 kilometres, more than three times the height SN8 had reached. About three minutes after liftoff the flight termination system was triggered, and the vehicle tumbled for roughly forty seconds before it broke apart.

That test had a cost that was not confined to the rocket. According to published accounts, the launch blasted sand and soil into the air, reaching communities within a radius of 10.7 kilometres. A brushfire in state parkland burned about 3.5 acres, roughly 1.4 hectares.

The three outcomes could hardly differ more: a landing explosion, a landing success, and a first joint flight cut short. What stands out is that the failure of SN8 can be told in one concrete sentence: the methane tank pressure was low. A sentence like that shows what has to change before the next test, while "the landing failed" only announces a loss. Fast testing turns into fast learning only when the cause of each result is written down this precisely, and otherwise it is just fast explosions. There is also the bill: the soil and the fire after the 2023 launch are a reminder that learning does not always stay on one side of the ledger.

[[lessons]]
$$**After every failed test, write the cause as one concrete sentence and change only that before the next attempt.** When a pilot or a launch goes wrong it is easy to say "it didn't go well," but that sentence shows no next step. For SN8's landing, "the methane tank pressure was low" leaves exactly one thing to fix. A concrete cause prevents the same mistake from repeating and tells the team which part to focus on. The same sentence also makes the meeting simpler, because you can answer "what did we learn, what will we change" in two short lines.$$

Two soldiers ride toward an emperor carrying a trophy, expecting thanks. The emperor will not look at it. He orders it buried before they arrive. What he had wanted from the war was something the trophy had just made impossible.

[[story]]
Avidius Cassius governed Syria and had won famous victories against Parthia. In 175, according to the Roman historian Cassius Dio, a rumour reached him that Marcus Aurelius had died. Without waiting to confirm it, Dio writes, Cassius claimed the throne. He kept going even after he learned the emperor was alive, and he won over the region south of the Taurus mountains.

Marcus heard the news from the governor of Cappadocia and kept it quiet for a while. When the troops grew restless over the rumours, he called them together. Dio gives a version of the speech, and Dio composed it, so treat the wording as his. In it Marcus says he has come to grieve rather than to rage. He says he would have been glad to invite Cassius to argue the matter before the army or the senate, and would have handed over power without a fight if that were good for the state.

He also tells the soldiers that Cassius's fame counts for little beside their own record, since an eagle is not formidable at the head of an army of jackdaws. Then he names the thing he fears. Cassius might kill himself in shame, or someone else might kill him on hearing that Marcus was on the way. That would rob him of a great prize, he says, which is to forgive a man who has wronged you, to remain a friend to one who betrayed friendship. Dio adds that Marcus never attacked Cassius beyond calling him ungrateful again and again, and that he refused offers of help from barbarian tribes because they should not learn of a quarrel between Romans.

It went the other way. A centurion wounded Cassius in the neck, and a decurion finished the work. They cut off the head and set out to take it to the emperor. Dio says Marcus was so grieved that he could not bring himself even to look at it, and ordered it buried before the men arrived.

The leniency went on. According to Dio, Marcus put no one to death in the provinces that had joined the revolt. He sent the senators tied to Cassius before the senate on other charges and exiled one governor to an island. In a letter he asked the senators that none of them be killed in his reign. In Dio's version he adds that he would hurry to his own death if that request were refused.

[[lessons]]
$$**Name the outcome you want to be proud of before you answer a betrayal.** A shop owner whose assistant has quietly started a rival business can first decide whether she wants a repaired friendship, a fair settlement or just to win, and then speak accordingly. Marcus, in Dio's account, said in advance that the prize he wanted was forgiveness, and his later choices followed it, down to refusing to look at the head. With the goal stated early and in plain words, the first angry draft of your reply becomes easier to hold back and compare against it.$$

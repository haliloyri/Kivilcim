Imagine a customer landing on a company's website. In the first few seconds, they need to understand three things: what's offered, how it will make their life better, and what to do next. Donald Miller argues that most businesses fail this test, and that it costs them far more than they realize.

[[story]]
In *Building a StoryBrand*, Donald Miller makes a claim that many business owners resist at first. Their problem, he says, often isn't their product. It's their message.

Companies tend to say too much. They list every feature, use insider jargon and try to sound impressive. They assume customers will take the time to figure it out. Miller insists they won't. The human brain is always trying to conserve energy. When a message is confusing, people don't work harder to understand it. They move on.

He sums it up in a line he repeats throughout the book: **if you confuse, you'll lose.**

His solution is to organize a message the way stories have always been organized. Stories are how human beings make sense of the world; a good story holds our attention and makes the path forward clear. So he created a framework with seven parts, based on the structure found in countless films and novels. He calls it **SB7**.

##A character who wants something encounters a problem. They meet a guide, who gives them a plan and calls them to action. That action helps them avoid failure and ends in success.##

In business terms, the **character** is the customer, with a clear desire. The **problem** is what stands in their way. Miller stresses that problems have layers: an external problem (the leaking roof), an internal problem (the stress and embarrassment it causes) and sometimes a philosophical one (families shouldn't have to live in fear of the next storm). Companies usually talk only about the external problem, but customers often buy to solve the internal one.

The **guide** is the business, showing empathy and authority. The **plan** is a few simple steps that make taking action feel safe. The **call to action** is direct: buy now, schedule a call, get started. And the story ends by showing the stakes: the **failure** the customer will avoid and the **success** they'll reach.

When a company fills in these seven parts, Miller calls the result a **BrandScript**. It then becomes the basis for everything: the website, emails, pitches and ads. Instead of a collection of claims, the company tells one clear story, with the customer at the center.

[[lessons]]
$$**Clarity beats cleverness.** "If you confuse, you'll lose" is Miller's warning. Customers don't reward effort spent decoding your message; they simply leave. A clear, simple message about what you offer and why it matters will usually outperform a clever, complicated one, even for an excellent product.$$

$$**Tell one simple story.** The SB7 framework, a character, a problem, a guide, a plan, a call to action, failure avoided and success reached, turns a jumble of claims into a story people can follow. Stories hold attention because the brain is built to follow them.$$

$$**Speak to the internal problem.** Customers may describe an external problem, but they often buy to relieve an internal one: stress, embarrassment, fear. Naming that inner frustration shows real understanding, and it's often the part of the message that makes someone finally decide to act. Name it clearly.$$

[[reflect]]
- Could a stranger explain what you offer after five seconds on your website or profile?
- What internal problem, not just external, does your work actually solve for people?
- Is your plan simple enough that taking the first step feels safe?

&&How would your message read if you wrote it as a simple seven-part story?&&

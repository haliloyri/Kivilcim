In a nursing home in Massachusetts, a bed stands on an industrial scale adjusted to register a fifth of an ounce. A physician waits beside it with one question: does a person weigh less in the moment they die? He has six chances to find out, and he will quote only one of them.

[[story]]
Mary Roach's chapter "How to Know If You're Dead" in *Stiff* is about beating-heart cadavers and the old question of where life ends, and with it the question of the soul. The best-known attempt to measure the soul was made by Duncan MacDougall, a physician from Haverhill, Massachusetts, and he published it in 1907 under the title "Hypothesis Concerning Soul Substance Together with Experimental Evidence of the Existence of Such Substance". The title already states the conclusion the weighing was meant to reach.

MacDougall chose six patients near death in nursing homes: four with tuberculosis, one with diabetes, one with an unspecified illness. He preferred people exhausted by their illness, because they would lie still when the end came. The whole bed sat on an industrial scale sensitive to about two tenths of an ounce, about 5.6 grams, and he watched the balance for the moment of death.

One patient lost three quarters of an ounce, or 21.3 grams, at that moment. That is the number everyone remembers. The other five cases behaved differently. In one the scale was, by his own account, not finely adjusted. In another the patient died while he was still calibrating. In some the weight dropped at death and then kept dropping a few minutes later, which is not what a departing soul would do.

He also weighed fifteen dogs after death and reported that none lost weight. The New York Times had reported on the work in March 1907, a month before the full paper appeared in the journal *American Medicine*.

The answer came quickly. Augustus P. Clarke argued in the same journal that the body temperature rises after death, which makes the skin sweat and evaporate water, and that dogs, having no sweat glands, could not be compared at all. MacDougall himself wrote that the experiment would have to be repeated many times before any conclusion was drawn. It never was. He died in 1920, and in 1911 the Times had reported that he hoped to photograph souls.

The physicist Robert L. Park wrote that the experiments are not regarded today as having any scientific merit. What stays in the memory is the number from one patient out of six. The other five did not go away, they were simply not repeated, and 21 grams survived because a single clean result is easier to carry than a pile of unclear ones.

[[lessons]]
$$**Count every case before you quote the best one.** A salesperson who remembers the one huge deal from last spring will describe her product as a sure thing, even though the notebook shows most of her pitches went nowhere. Writing every result down in the same list, the poor ones as well as the good ones, shows you what usually happens and how large the spread is between the best case and the typical one. Then you can say "one in six went like this" about a story, and you will be believed more than when you say "it works", because anyone can ask to see the rest of the list.$$

[[open]]
Two groups of viewers choose between the same two ads. On one screen a frightening film scene has just played, on the other a scene from a love story. One ad calls to the crowd, the other to a select few.

[[story]]
Robert Cialdini describes this experiment in a 2016 PBS interview with Paul Solman, and he uses it to illustrate pre-suasion. He says his own research team ran it. He gives no figures, no year and no number of participants, so the story is known only as far as he tells it.

The participants first watched a film clip. One group saw a scene from *The Shining*, the horror film. The other group saw a scene from *Before Sunset*, a romantic film. The two films have nothing to do with museums: one is set in a remote hotel, the other follows two people walking around Paris. Then both groups were shown ads for an art museum in San Francisco.

The two ads used different lines. One read: "Be one of the many who has visited." The other read: "Be one of the few who have experienced the wonders of this museum." One invited you to join the crowd, the other to join a privileged few.

According to Cialdini, the viewers of the scary film felt threatened and preferred the "many" ad. Viewers of the romantic film chose the "few" ad. He adds that in each group, the people who preferred their ad were also significantly more willing to visit the museum in the future.

One plausible reading is that someone who feels under threat wants the safety of a crowd, so "be one of the many" sounds warm to them. A romantic scene brings closeness and a special bond to the front of the mind, and in that mood being one of the few looks attractive.

The design of the experiment is instructive. The museum was the same museum, and the only difference between the ads was whether they called to a majority or a minority. A film scene that had nothing to do with the museum decided which call looked brighter. The film was a short moment right before the ad, and that moment was enough to turn attention and mood in one direction.

That is why the experiment suggests looking at where people stand just before your message, before you change the message itself. The same sentence can sound warm in one mood and foreign in another.

[[lessons]]
$$**Fit your call to the mood the people in front of you are in right now.** The head of a neighborhood association writing a call for new members after an anxious week might choose "we are all in this together", and after a cheerful week might choose "be among the first". The association and the invitation are the same, yet the feeling of the moment decides which sentence is received warmly. This does not require misleading anyone, only noticing what they are drawn toward. Thinking about how the people on the other side felt that day before you write the message is often a large enough first step.$$

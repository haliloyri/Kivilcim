A fifteen-year-old named John Goddard sat down and wrote out what he wanted to do with his life. He didn't stop at five things, or twenty. He kept going until the list held 127 goals, and one of them was exploring the Nile. What does writing a list that long do to the person holding the pen?

[[story]]
Jack Canfield uses Goddard as his example in *The Success Principles*, and the list is the whole point. Most of us carry our goals around as vague wishes. They live in our heads, next to everything else we mean to do someday, and they rarely get looked at closely.

Canfield's instruction is blunt: get them out of your head and onto paper. Not a handful, either. He suggests a list of 101 goals, and he wants each one specific and measurable, something you could later check off rather than a mood you hope to feel.

A hundred and one sounds absurd. That's partly the design. The first few items come easily, because they are the ones almost anyone would write. Somewhere past them, the obvious answers run out, and you have to ask yourself what you actually want. A long list forces that question in a way a short one never does.

Goddard's list shows how far that can reach. At fifteen he wasn't writing "be successful." He was writing concrete ambitions, big enough to include exploring one of the world's great rivers. According to Canfield, Goddard went on to accomplish a large share of those 127 goals over his lifetime.

Canfield gives two reasons why the writing matters. The first is commitment. A wish in your head costs nothing and can quietly change shape. A goal on paper is something you have declared you want, and it stays in front of you where you can see it.

The second reason is attention. Canfield points to the brain's reticular activating system, which filters the flood of information around you and lets through what seems relevant. ##Once a goal is clearly defined, that filter starts letting through the information and opportunities connected to it, the ones you would otherwise have walked straight past.##

So the list is more than a record of wishes. It tells your attention what to look for. A teenager's 127 lines became, in Canfield's telling, a set of instructions for noticing.

[[lessons]]
$$**Paper makes it a promise.** Canfield asks for goals written as specific, measurable items. That turns "I'd like to travel more" into a line you can check against later. A wish can drift without anyone noticing; a written goal gives you something concrete to hold yourself to.$$

$$**Length pushes past the easy answers.** A list of 101, or Goddard's 127, is long on purpose. The first handful are the goals anyone would write. The ones that surface later, once you've run out of obvious answers, are more likely to say something true about you.$$

$$**A named goal changes what you notice.** Canfield's point about the reticular activating system is that your brain filters out most of what it sees. A clearly defined goal gives that filter a new target, so relevant information and chances stop slipping past. The goal doesn't create the opportunities; it helps you see them.$$

[[reflect]]
- Which of your goals exists only as a thought you've never written down?
- If you wrote your list today, how far would you get before the obvious answers ran out?
- Have you ever started noticing something everywhere right after deciding you wanted it?

&&Next time you have ten quiet minutes, how many specific goals could you write before stopping, and which one would surprise you by being there?&&

On a flat rock by the shore, a half-starved man presses his lips to the stone. Every five minutes a single drop of water seeps out, and he collects a few of them. They do nothing for his thirst. Behind him lies an island nobody in his party has ever seen, and above him the tide is coming in.

[[story]]
When Owen Chase wrote his account of the Essex, this island was the first land his boats had touched in a month. It was 20 December 1820. He guessed it to be about six miles long and three wide, rugged and bare on the slopes and green at the top. Nathaniel Philbrick's *In the Heart of the Sea* follows the crew onto this shore. Chase waded in with a musket, caught a small fish and ate it on the spot, and climbed a crag about twenty feet looking for water. He found none, and the rising tide made him hurry back to the boats.

Two days later he was picking at the damp rock with a hatchet and an old chisel, and the hole filled with nothing. He sat down in despair. Then he saw men carrying a keg along the beach. They had found a spring in a flat rock at the water's edge, so low that the sea covered it by nearly six feet at high tide. They could drink only when the tide was out.

For a few days the island kept them alive. Tropic birds sat in holes in the mountainside and did not fly when the men reached for them, and a plant like peppergrass grew in the cracks. By the 24th, Chase writes, they had picked over everything within reach. Some men came in at night saying they had not found enough to fill their stomachs.

Chase concluded that staying would now be worse than sailing, because in a boat they at least moved toward land. They would head for Easter Island, which their books put eight hundred and fifty miles nearer the coast.

##Three men decided to stay.##

William Wright, Thomas Chapple and Seth Weeks had been talking it over. Chase says the others could make no objection. With three fewer aboard, the boats rode lighter and the provisions went further, and the three had a better chance of surviving on the island than the rest had of reaching the mainland. The others assured them that if they got through, they would report their position and do everything to have them taken off. Captain Pollard wrote letters, put them in a tin case inside a wooden box, and nailed it to a tree.

That promise was kept. After the survivors reached Chile, the United States consul at Valparaiso hired a ship called the Surrey, bound for Sydney, to look for the three. Her captain tried one island, found nothing, then looked at his chart and suspected the men had taken one island for the other. At the second, heavy surf kept the boats from landing, and he called to the men to swim. Chapple finally dashed into the sea, reached the boat and took a line ashore, and his companions were hauled out. All three were alive.

[[lessons]]
$$**Whoever you leave behind deserves a promise with a name, a place and a person to carry it out.** Imagine a team lead who moves to a new project and tells the colleague taking over a difficult account that she will check in. Weeks later nothing has happened, because nobody wrote down who would call, when, and about what. A promise holds only when you fix one concrete step, like the letter in the tin case, and give it an owner, so that it no longer depends on your memory once you are somewhere safer, and the person waiting knows exactly what to expect and when.$$

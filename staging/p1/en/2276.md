A young curate knocks on a door in Soho for the fourth time. He has been here before, and so has his notebook: a name, an age, which pump the family used, the hour the illness began. He is hoping the answers will count against a doctor's theory.

[[story]]
The curate is Henry Whitehead, aged twenty-nine, who served at St Luke's in Berwick Street, in the same neighbourhood as the Broad Street pump. Steven Johnson's *The Ghost Map* tells the story of the 1854 cholera outbreak in Soho, and Whitehead is one of its people. According to published accounts of his inquiry, he expected a careful look to refute John Snow's idea that the pump water was spreading the disease.

So he did what a parish priest could do and a visiting physician could not. He knew these streets and their families, and he went from door to door. Accounts say he sometimes returned four or five times to confirm what he needed, noting for each death the name, the age, where the sick person lived, what water they used and when the fatal illness began. His method was patient and unglamorous.

One household kept appearing in the story. At 40 Broad Street, opposite the pump, a baby girl named Frances Lewis, the five-month-old daughter of a police constable, Thomas Lewis, and of Sarah Lewis, died on 2 September. The doctor who signed the certificate, Dr Rogers, gave exhaustion after an attack of diarrhoea four days before death as the cause, and he doubted that it was cholera at all.

Sarah Lewis told the investigators something small and decisive. During her daughter's illness she had steeped the soiled napkins in pails of water and emptied that water into the cesspool at the front of the house. A surveyor's report of 1 May 1855 put the pump's well about two feet eight inches from that cesspool. The published accounts present this as the link: waste from a sick household, a few feet of soil, and a well that half the neighbourhood drank from.

Whitehead did not rush to this view. By his own later account he reached the conclusion slowly and, as he put it, reluctantly: the use of the Broad Street water was connected with the continuation of the outbreak.

It is worth noticing why that sentence carried weight. Snow, an outsider, could be dismissed as a man with a theory. Whitehead was the local curate who had set out to disprove it, who had stood in the very rooms where people had died, and who changed his own mind in the open. A conclusion reached against one's own expectation is the hardest kind to wave away.

[[lessons]]
$$**Investigate the idea you expect to reject as thoroughly as the one you hope is true.** Whitehead gathered names, ages, water sources and times of onset, and the records kept disagreeing with him until he said so. Try it when a colleague proposes something you doubt: spend an honest week collecting the evidence that would settle it, and write down in advance what would change your mind. If the facts do move you, you become the most credible witness the idea could have, because nobody can say you were looking for it. That credibility is worth more in the next meeting than any argument you could have prepared.$$

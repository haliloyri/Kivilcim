[[story]]
*Difficult Conversations*, by Douglas Stone, Bruce Patton and Sheila Heen, builds on a plain observation: before two people can talk about a problem, they have to talk about what happened, and each arrives sure that their own account is the true one. A college football game from 1951 tested that observation better than any experiment could have been designed to.

On 23 November 1951, Princeton beat Dartmouth 13 to 0. The game was rough. Dick Kazmaier, Princeton's Heisman Trophy winner, left in the second quarter with a broken nose and a mild concussion, and later a Dartmouth player broke a leg. The Daily Princetonian accused Dartmouth of going after Kazmaier on purpose. Dartmouth's coach called the charge outrageous, and the game films, according to the Dartmouth Alumni Magazine, showed no deliberate attempt to injure anyone.

Two psychologists, Hadley Cantril and Albert Hastorf, saw their chance. They gave psychology students at both schools a questionnaire about the game, and they showed students at both schools a film of it, asking them to note every rule infraction they saw.

The questionnaires split the campuses. In the published figures, 86 percent of the Princeton students said Dartmouth had started the rough play. Only 36 percent of the Dartmouth students blamed their own side, and most of the rest said both teams had started it.

The film split them more sharply, because it was one film. Princeton students counted an average of 9.8 infractions by the Dartmouth team. Dartmouth students, watching the same footage, counted an average of 4.3 by their own team. Same reel, same plays, and more than twice the fouls on one side of the room.

Hastorf and Cantril concluded that there had not been one game that people then argued about. There had been many, and each was as real to the person who saw it as the others were to theirs. That is worse than lying, since a liar at least knows what he left out.

This is the ground the authors call the "What Happened" conversation. When a colleague says your meeting was a mess and you remember a good one, you have both watched the same film. Each of you has simply counted different fouls.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask what the other person noticed before you argue about what happened.** When two people disagree about a meeting, a chore split or an old promise, each usually carries a complete, confident account and treats the other's as a mistake. A colleague who remembers the last review as harsh and you, who remember it as fair, may both be describing real moments, because your attention landed on different ones. Begin with "What stood out to you?" and keep listening until you can name two details you had not counted. Only then is it useful to compare conclusions, and by then you may be arguing about less.$$

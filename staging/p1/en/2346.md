[[open]]
Two days, one dense textbook, and a quiz at the next class. A first-year student, schooled mostly at home, sits down to prove she belongs in the room, and she misses every question. What she does with that result tells you more than the result itself.

[[story]]
In *Educated*, Tara Westover describes her first semester at Brigham Young University after a childhood without regular school. Her American history class met in an auditorium named for the prophet Joseph Smith. She expected it to be easy, because her father had taught her children about the Founding Fathers, and she knew the names Washington, Jefferson and Madison.

The professor barely mentioned them. He talked about philosophical underpinnings and about writers she had never heard of, Cicero and Hume among them. At the end of the first lecture he announced a quiz on the readings for the next class.

She spent two days on the dense textbook. Terms like "civic humanism" and the Scottish Enlightenment were, in her own image, black holes that sucked the words around them into nothing. Then came the quiz, and she missed every question.

A reader might expect doubt to follow. It did not. Her loyalty to her father, she says, grew in proportion to the miles between them. Away from home, she clung to every doctrine he had given her, including the teaching that doctors were Sons of Perdition and that home schooling was a commandment from the Lord. The failed quiz left that devotion untouched. What shakes it comes later, in an art lecture, and it takes a single word to do it.

Consider the logic. A student who fails a first test has two obvious readings. One is that the test measured something real and her preparation fell short. The other is that nothing needs to change. Westover, newly alone, took the second path, and that is a very ordinary human move. When new evidence arrives and the person is already far from home, the familiar framework feels like the only solid ground left.

The quiz gave her information, and the information sat unused for a while. Nothing in her account suggests she was foolish, and many people would do the same in a new place with no one to ask. It says that a surprising result does not change a view automatically. Someone has to decide to let it, and in that first autumn she was not yet ready to do so.

[[lessons]]
$$**A result that contradicts your plan will not change it until you decide to treat it as evidence.** Westover's first quiz was a clear signal, and she answered it by holding tighter to everything she already believed. The same reflex shows up when a launch misses its target or a review comes back poor, and the first response is to defend the method. Write down what the result would mean if it were true, then name one specific thing you would do differently. If the honest answer is nothing, check whether you are protecting the plan or reading the data.$$

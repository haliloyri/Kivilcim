On a Sunday morning, a young apprentice has the printing house to himself. On the desk lie a few scribbled words for each sentence of an essay, and a book he has put out of reach. He has to rebuild the whole piece from the hints, then lay it beside the original and see how far off he is.

[[story]]
Benjamin Franklin tells this in his autobiography, written long after the events. As a young apprentice in Boston he loved an argument, and he and a friend, John Collins, once fell out over whether women should be educated. They were not to see each other for a while, so Franklin wrote his arguments down and sent them. Collins answered, Franklin replied, and after three or four letters on each side his father happened to find the papers.

His father did not discuss the question. He talked about the writing. Franklin had the advantage of Collins in spelling and punctuation, he said, which Franklin owed to the printing house, but he fell far short in elegance of expression, in method and in clarity, and the father showed him so with several examples. Franklin saw that he was right and decided to improve.

Soon afterwards he came across an odd volume of the *Spectator*, the third, bought it and read it over and over. He thought the writing excellent and wished to imitate it. He took some of the papers and made short hints of the sentiment in each sentence. He laid them aside for a few days. Then, without looking at the book, he tried to write the papers out again, expressing each hinted sentiment at length, as fully as it had been expressed before, in any suitable words that came to hand. Afterwards he compared his version with the original, found some of his faults and corrected them.

The comparison showed him something else: he lacked a stock of words, or the readiness to find them. Verse, he reasoned, would have forced him to search for variety, since a poem needs words of the same meaning but different length or sound. So he turned some of the tales into verse, and when he had pretty well forgotten the prose, he turned them back again. He also jumbled his hints into confusion and, some weeks later, tried to put them in the best order before writing the full sentences. That exercise was meant to teach him how to arrange his thoughts.

Each time he compared his work with the original and found many faults to amend. Sometimes, he writes, he had the pleasure of fancying that in small particulars he had improved on the method or the language, and that encouraged him to think he might become a tolerable English writer, which he was extremely ambitious to be. He did this at night after work, in the morning before it began, or on Sundays, when he arranged to be alone in the printing house.

Every one of these exercises shared one feature. He did the task from his hints, with the model out of sight, and the model appeared only afterwards, as the answer key. The pause between writing the hints and rewriting from them is what made each attempt a real effort of recall and composition, not copying.

[[lessons]]
$$**Try the task from your notes first, and only then lay your attempt beside a good model.** Comparing straight away lets the model do your thinking for you, while comparing after an honest attempt shows exactly where you were short and where you were already close. A new support agent learning to answer tickets can read an excellent reply, jot the three main points, and the next day write the reply from those points before checking it against the original. What the comparison gives is a short list of specific gaps, which a general wish to write better never delivers.$$

A rook slides across the board and the world chess champion stops. He can find no reason for it. The longer he studies the position, the less sense it makes, and the more he suspects that the machine across from him sees something he cannot. There is nothing there to see.

[[story]]
Garry Kasparov had just done what few expected. In the first game of his May 1997 rematch with IBM's Deep Blue in New York, he had driven the computer into a position it could not rescue, after 43 moves. Then Deep Blue replied with a rook move that, to Kasparov, made no sense at all.

Nate Silver tells the rest in *The Signal and the Noise*. According to his account, the move was not a deep idea. Deep Blue's engineers believed they had fixed a bug in the program, but it was still there. When the machine was cornered and could find no good move, the bug made it pick one completely at random. Murray Campbell, who worked on Deep Blue, told Silver that the bug may have made Kasparov misunderstand what the machine could do.

It was only the first game of a six-game match, and each side was still taking the other's measure. A strange move early in a match is exactly the kind of thing a player replays in his head for days.

The details of the mechanism differ from one retelling to the next. Some describe a truly random pick, others a fail-safe that fired when no good move was found. What they share is the point that matters here: nothing clever stood behind that move.

What Kasparov made of it is a matter of inference, not record. Published accounts suggest it may have unsettled him, and that he may have read the move as a sign the computer was playing above his level. Nobody can measure that. We do know how the match ended: Deep Blue won it, 3.5 to 2.5.

Consider how reasonable his reaction was. A grandmaster spends a lifetime reading the intentions behind moves, and against strong opponents that habit pays, because strong players rarely move at random. Kasparov applied his best tool to a situation it was not built for. He searched for a signal and found noise.

That is the trap Silver keeps returning to in his book about prediction. When something looks inexplicable, we assume the explanation is hidden depth in the other side. Sometimes the simpler answer is that the other side has made a mistake, and our own model of them was fine all along.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you credit a puzzling move to a hidden plan, put a plain mistake on the list of explanations.** When a competitor cuts prices overnight or a client asks for something odd, the reflex is to assume they know something you don't. But the cause may be a typo, a miscommunication or a coin-flip decision nobody planned, which gets expensive in a meeting, because a whole team can spend a week reverse-engineering a decision that nobody actually made. Test the cheap explanation first, then add the sophisticated one. That way you do not rebuild your own plan around a strategy that exists only in your head.$$

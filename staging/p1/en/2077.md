[[open]]
On his last day at a Wall Street bank, a programmer encrypts a large body of code and sends it to a server in Germany. Then he deletes the encryption program and the record of the commands he typed. A few weeks later, at an airport in Newark, agents are waiting for him.

[[story]]
The programmer was Sergey Aleynikov, who had worked at Goldman Sachs from 2007 to 2009 on its high-frequency trading systems, and his case runs through Michael Lewis's *Flash Boys* (2014). According to the Second Circuit, the code he uploaded in June 2009 ran to more than 500,000 lines. He was about to join a competitor, the high-frequency trading firm Teza Technologies. FBI agents arrested him at Newark Liberty airport on July 3, 2009.

In December 2010 a federal jury convicted him under two laws, the National Stolen Property Act and the Economic Espionage Act. The jury had rejected his argument that he copied only open-source material. He was sentenced to 97 months. He then spent more than a year in custody while his appeal went forward.

On February 16, 2012, the Second Circuit ordered the convictions reversed, and he was released the next morning. The unanimous written opinion, by Chief Judge Dennis Jacobs, followed on April 11. It did not turn on whether the code had been copied. It turned on the wording of the two laws. Source code, the court held, is not "goods" that were "stolen" under the first, and Goldman's trading system was not "produced for or placed in interstate or foreign commerce" under the second, because Goldman never intended to sell or license it.

Congress responded in December 2012 with the Theft of Trade Secrets Clarification Act, which changed the second statute.

The matter did not end there. Manhattan prosecutors charged Aleynikov under New York law in August 2012. In 2015 a jury convicted him on one count of unlawful use of secret scientific material, acquitted him of duplicating computer-related material and deadlocked on a third count. On July 6, 2015, Justice Daniel Conviser set the verdict aside, finding that the copy was not a tangible reproduction and that Aleynikov lacked the intent to appropriate. The Appellate Division reinstated the conviction in January 2017. On May 3, 2018, the New York Court of Appeals unanimously upheld it, writing: "A copy of source code may be tangible even if the source code itself is not."

Several courts, two legal systems and one set of uploads produced opposite results. What changed from court to court was which word in which statute reached the act.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you write or enforce a rule, check that its words cover the thing you actually mean.** A club that bans "removing" equipment from its storeroom may find that a member who uses a machine for months without ever carrying it out has broken nothing the rule can name. Whatever you think of the member, the rule described something narrower than the harm it was written for. Reading your own rule the way a stubborn stranger would, one word at a time, shows you where it leaks while it is still cheap to repair. Do it before the dispute, because afterwards every word is under pressure.$$

You are inside a wooden fort beside a Virginia river, sent across the ocean by a company that wants gold. The rules are simple: work in common, eat from the common store, obey orders or be punished. Ask yourself honestly how hard you would dig when the reward by nightfall is the same either way.

[[story]]
In 1607 the Virginia Company of London founded Jamestown, the first lasting English settlement in North America. The investors hoped for what Spain had found in the Americas: gold and a native population to put to work. Neither turned up. Acemoglu and Robinson, who tell this story in *Why Nations Fail*, describe a colony built on the expectation of easy extraction that met a reality in which there was nothing to extract.

The company's first answer was discipline. According to their account, settlers worked together under a harsh regime, with punishments that could run as far as execution, and they depended on a common store instead of what each one produced. John Smith, who became president of the colony's council, pressed the company to send people who could actually build and farm, rather than more gentlemen with no useful skill. The trouble was deeper than the labour mix. In published histories of the colony, the winter of 1609 to 1610 is remembered as the Starving Time, when only about 60 of roughly 214 settlers are said to have survived.

The authors argue that coercion kept failing for a simple reason. Settlers could not be forced to work as hard as a company wanted, because the land was wide, the locals could not be commanded either, and a man who disliked his orders could try his luck beyond the fort. A system that punishes laziness but never rewards effort invites people to do as little as they can get away with.

So the company changed course. Around 1618 it began granting settlers land of their own, a headright of fifty acres, and in 1619 the first representative assembly in English America met in the Jamestown church. Only men of English origin could vote at first, so this was a narrow opening, not democracy. But it marked a turn from ordering people about to giving them a stake in what they built. The company's charter was revoked by King James I in 1624, and Virginia became a royal colony.

What stands out is the order of events. The company did not discover a nicer philosophy. It ran out of other options. When forcing people proved too costly and too ineffective, the only remaining path was to let them keep what they earned and have some say in the rules.

[[lessons]]
$$**People work hardest for what they can keep, so give them a stake in the result before you reach for orders and punishments.** A manager who controls every hour of a team's day but lets the credit and the rewards flow elsewhere will find that people do exactly what is required and no more. A person who owns a piece of the outcome, whether it is a project, a budget or a say in the plan, tends to bring effort that no supervisor could have demanded. Ask what each person on your team gains when the work goes well, and if the honest answer is nothing, expect that much effort.$$

A conqueror who commands armies asks an old monk the one question no army can answer for him. How does a man avoid dying? The monk has crossed deserts and mountains to be there, and when his turn comes he has only one honest thing to say.

[[story]]
In 1219 Chinggis Khan sent two envoys to the Taoist master Qiu Chuji, known as Changchun, with an imperial edict and a golden tablet carved with a tiger. Jack Weatherford's *Genghis Khan and the Making of the Modern World* treats the khan's attitude to religion as part of how he governed. Published accounts of Qiu's life, including the travel record written by his disciple Li Zhichang, tell this episode in detail.

Qiu accepted, reportedly in the hope of restraining the violence of the Mongol army. He left Shandong in February 1220 and wintered in Yanjing, today's Beijing. In February 1221 he set out again with a small band of disciples, travelling through eastern Mongolia, the Altai mountains and the Ili valley to Samarkand, where he stayed for several months. He crossed the Amu Darya by way of Balkh and reached the khan's camp near the Hindu Kush in 1222.

When Qiu arrived, Chinggis rose to greet him. The visitor returned a bow with his hands folded, rather than prostrating himself as court custom would have required. The khan called him an "Immortal" and asked whether there was a way never to die.

The monk's reply, as the accounts give it, was that there are only ways of nourishing life, and no elixir that brings immortality. The accounts add that the khan appreciated the frankness. Over less than a year they met three times, and Qiu spoke about governing and caring for one's health. He told the khan that Heaven loves life and dislikes killing, and that respecting Heaven and loving people are what good government rests on.

At the end of the visit, in spring 1223, Chinggis issued an edict exempting the Quanzhen school of Taoism from all taxes and forced labour. Qiu was back in Yanjing by late January 1224. The khan also ordered his minister Yelü Chucai to compile their conversations into a record.

Think about the position Qiu was in. The most powerful man in Asia had asked for something he plainly wanted, and a flattering answer would have cost the monk nothing in the moment. Instead he said that no such elixir existed. That answer asked nothing from the khan, which may be why it counted for so much.

It is also worth noting what the exchange produced. The bow with folded hands and the plain answer did not cost Qiu the khan's favour. They seem to have established that he could be believed, and a ruler who is surrounded by people who say what he wants to hear has very few such people.

[[lessons]]
$$**When someone with power asks for what you cannot give, a plain answer builds more trust than a pleasing one.** Suppose a client asks a contractor whether the work will be finished by Friday and the honest answer is that it won't be. A hopeful yes keeps the meeting pleasant and leaves a problem for next week, while an honest no with a real date shifts the conversation to what can be done. You are not trying to disappoint anyone. You are showing that your yes will mean something later, because they have seen you give a no, and that trust helps with every question that comes after it.$$

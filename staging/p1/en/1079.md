You've probably pocketed the mint that comes with a restaurant bill without a second thought. It's a throwaway. Yet researchers found that the same cheap candy, handed over in one particular way, moved tips far more than you'd guess. Nothing about the mint changed. What changed was a few seconds of the server's behavior, and those seconds turned out to be worth real money.

[[story]]
Robert Cialdini built *Influence* around a handful of forces that steer human behavior, and one of the oldest is reciprocity. When someone gives us something, we feel we owe them. In his telling, this is no local custom. It's a norm found across human societies, and it runs largely on automatic pilot. We rarely stop to weigh it. We simply feel the pull to repay.

In later work, Cialdini pointed to a restaurant study that shows the principle with unusual precision. The setup was simple. When servers brought the bill, they varied what they did with it, and the researchers tracked what happened to tips.

In the first condition, the bill arrived with no mint at all. That was the baseline.

In the second, the server left one mint per diner. Tips went up, but only slightly, by roughly 3%. A small gift produced a small nudge. Reciprocity was working, just faintly.

In the third, the server left two mints per diner. Now tips rose by roughly 14%. Twice the candy, a noticeably bigger response. So far it looks like a plain equation: give more, get more back.

The fourth condition breaks that equation. The server set down the bill with one mint per diner, the same as the second condition, and began to walk away. Then the server turned back, returned to the table, and offered a second mint, as if this table in particular deserved something extra. In the commonly cited version, it came with a line like:

> "For you nice people, here's an extra mint."

##The diners ended up with the same two mints as the third group, and tips rose by roughly 23%.##

Look at what that means. The candy was identical to the two-mint condition. The cost was identical. The only difference was the delivery. Two mints handed over at once felt like how the restaurant worked. One mint followed by a deliberate turn-back felt like a choice, made on the spot, for these particular people.

The mints cost almost nothing in any of the conditions. What rose from one version to the next wasn't the value of the gift. It was how significant, how unexpected, and how personal the gesture felt. The diners weren't sitting there calculating the market price of peppermint. They were responding to a feeling: *that server singled us out.* And that feeling, running on the automatic machinery Cialdini describes, reached straight into their wallets.

[[lessons]]
$$**Even a tiny gift creates a debt.** A single mint with the bill nudged tips up by roughly 3%. That's small, but it isn't zero. The diners owed the server nothing for a piece of candy, and still some of them felt the pull to give a little more. This is reciprocity at its faintest, and it shows how low the threshold is. You don't need a grand gesture to create a sense of obligation. You only need to give something first.$$

$$**Feeling chosen beats getting more.** The walk-away-and-return second mint beat simply handing over two, roughly 23% against roughly 14%, with the exact same candy on the table. The extra power came from the gift feeling unexpected and aimed at these people rather than at every customer. If you want your generosity to register, make it specific and make it feel chosen. Doubling the size of something routine won't do the same work.$$

$$**It runs on autopilot, which cuts both ways.** Nobody at those tables reasoned their way to a bigger tip. They reacted to the sense of being singled out. That automatic quality is why reciprocity is so strong and so reliable. It's also why others can exploit it. A "free" sample or a surprise extra can be sincere, or it can be a lever pulled on purpose.$$

[[reflect]]
- Think of a time a small, unexpected gesture made you want to do something for the person in return. Was it the thing itself, or the sense that they'd thought of you?
- Look at the favors you usually do. Would the other person see them as routine, like two mints handed over with the bill, or as chosen for them?
- Recall a "free gift" that came right before someone asked you for something. Did it shape your answer more than you'd like to admit?
- Where could you shift from giving more to giving more personally?

&&What small, unexpected, personal gesture could you offer someone this week, with no strings attached?&&

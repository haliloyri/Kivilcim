[[open]]
A survey page asks you to rate, from minus five to plus five, how satisfied a student "with your values and interests" at a California university would be with life overall. You have never met this student. You still have to circle a number, and whichever one you pick is a guess.

[[story]]
There is a stereotype that people are happier in California than in the Midwest. David Schkade and Daniel Kahneman put it to a test in 1998 with 1,993 undergraduates: 492 at the University of Michigan, 499 at Ohio State, 503 at UCLA and 499 at UC Irvine. Each spent about an hour on a questionnaire and was paid between 7 and 10 dollars.

Some students rated their own lives. Others rated a student like themselves at one of the other universities. Everyone gave an overall satisfaction score, then rated eleven areas of life, from job prospects and personal safety to climate and cultural opportunities. Half had been told the survey was about the effect of climate on well-being, which was meant to push climate to the front of their minds.

Students rating their own lives showed no difference in overall satisfaction between the regions. Among white students, the raw averages were 2.95 in the Midwest and 2.98 in California, on a scale where plus five is extremely satisfied. The climate gap was real: Midwesterners were less satisfied with every aspect of their weather. Still, it did not show up in how they rated their lives as a whole.

Students rating an imagined peer told a different story. Midwestern raters thought the California student would be 0.57 points more satisfied than the Midwestern one, and Californian raters put the gap at 0.64. According to the authors' analysis, satisfaction with climate and with cultural opportunities explained the predicted advantage.

Schkade and Kahneman call this a focusing illusion. When you picture life somewhere else, your attention goes to the most visible differences, and you give them more weight than they will ever have in an actual day. The importance ratings show it. The five climate items averaged 3.28 out of 5 for people rating a different region and 3.06 for people rating their own. In everyone's general ranking, job prospects, academic opportunities, money, safety and social life mattered most.

The authors add a detail about culture. Los Angeles has more cultural attractions than Ann Arbor or Columbus, they write, but local residents often visit them only when a friend or relative comes to town. Seen from far away, those attractions look like a feature of daily life. From inside, residents tend to notice them when guests arrive.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you weigh the brightest difference between two options, ask how much of your week it will actually fill.** When you consider moving to a sunnier city or renting a flat with a view, the thing that catches your eye is a small slice of your real days. Write down an ordinary Tuesday from morning to night, then mark which hours the new option would change. That shows you a vivid difference at its true weight. Sometimes the gap is still large enough to justify the move, and sometimes it is little more than a postcard, but either way you decide using your week instead of your imagination.$$

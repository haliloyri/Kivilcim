[[open]]
Two researchers at a computer lab north of New York each open an envelope from a small investment firm. It is a recruitment letter, and neither man wants to leave the lab. They are happy where they are. Each of them drops the letter in the bin.

[[story]]
Robert Mercer had joined IBM in 1972, fresh from a computer science doctorate at the University of Illinois. He spent about twenty years there, working on speech recognition at the Thomas J. Watson Research Center in Yorktown Heights, New York. With colleagues, including Peter Brown, he helped pioneer statistical approaches to language: turning speech into text, and translating one language into another. This episode comes from the life of Jim Simons, the subject of Gregory Zuckerman's *The Man Who Solved the Market*, and from the people he hired.

In 1993 Renaissance Technologies, Simons's firm, sent recruitment letters to Mercer and Brown. According to CNBC's account, they threw the letters out at first. They had been very happy at IBM. Later they changed their minds, and both left.

Brown has given a practical reason. Mercer, he said, was struggling to pay college tuition for his three daughters, and Renaissance was offering about 50 percent more pay. Simons needed help with computer code, and these two men were skilled at it. Renaissance looks for unusual patterns in market prices and writes software to trade on them, so the code is the product, and a coder who could squeeze meaning out of noisy speech was a natural fit.

The choice looks odd on paper. Simons was a mathematician, and a published profile calls his early hire James Ax the first of many hires from outside traditional finance. Asked for advice in a 2019 interview, he said to hire the smartest people you possibly can, and to let everyone know what everyone else is researching. Speech recognition is a puzzle of its own, but it has a similar shape to trading: in both, a program has to guess what comes next from noisy data.

The firm's code was only part of what Mercer and Brown found there. Medallion became a vehicle for employees and their families in 1993, the same year the two men arrived. Later, when Simons stepped down as chief executive in 2010, Mercer and Brown took over the leadership of the firm.

By 2014 Brown could joke about how the job had changed. He said they ended up writing more legal documents than computer programs. Looking back, he also described the early years as hush-hush, so much so that he and his colleagues lost all contact with the outside world.

[[lessons]]
$$**Look at how a candidate has solved problems before you look at the industry on the résumé.** Simons found his coders among people who had spent twenty years teaching computers to hear speech, and a bakery that needs a better ordering system might do better with someone from logistics than with another baker. A person who has fought a similar puzzle in a different field arrives without the habits of the new one. When you next recruit for any role, write down the kind of problem the job really is, and look for people who have solved that kind elsewhere.$$

Draw the same person twice. In the first sketch, their energy leaves them as a spray of small arrows, each one heading somewhere different. In the second, every arrow points the same way. The amount of effort in both drawings is identical. Only one of them gets anywhere.

[[story]]
Those two sketches come from Greg McKeown's *Essentialism: The Disciplined Pursuit of Less*, and they carry most of his argument before he writes a paragraph of explanation.

In the first drawing, the energy is split across many activities. Each arrow is short. The person is busy, trying hard, and moving in all directions at once. McKeown has a phrase for the result: a millimeter of progress in a million directions.

In the second drawing, nothing has been added. There is no extra talent, no extra hours, no secret technique. The same energy has simply been aimed at what is essential. Because it is not divided, it travels much further in one direction.

So why do capable people end up in the first drawing? McKeown's answer is uncomfortable, because it starts with doing well. He calls it the clarity paradox, and it runs in four steps.

First, a person or team has real clarity of purpose. That clarity leads to success. Second, success brings attention, and attention brings more options and opportunities. Third, those options get pursued, and effort starts to spread across them. Fourth, the spreading undermines the very clarity that produced the success in the first place.

##The reward for focus is a pile of new options, and the options are what break the focus.##

McKeown connects this pattern to an observation by Jim Collins: successful companies can decline through what Collins called an "undisciplined pursuit of more." In that picture, the decline does not begin with doing too little. It begins with reaching for more.

Seen this way, the scattered arrows are not a sign of laziness or poor character. They are often a sign of recent success that nobody has managed. Each new arrow looked reasonable when it was added. The problem only shows up in the whole drawing.

McKeown's response is the idea that gives the book its name: essentialism, the disciplined pursuit of less. It means deliberately choosing fewer things, so that the vital few receive full effort instead of a thin share. The word that matters is *disciplined*. More arrives on its own, carried by every success. Less has to be chosen, again and again, against that steady pressure.

[[lessons]]
$$**Even effort means thin progress.** The two drawings hold the same energy. The difference is how many directions it is split into. When effort goes to everything equally, you get McKeown's millimeter of progress in a million directions: plenty of motion, very little distance. Busyness can look exactly like the second drawing from the inside.$$

$$**Success sets its own trap.** The clarity paradox is dangerous because every step looks like good news. Clarity brings success, success brings opportunities, and nobody complains about opportunities. Yet pursuing them is what quietly scatters the focus that earned them. The threat arrives dressed as a reward.$$

$$**Less has to be chosen on purpose.** Collins's phrase, "undisciplined pursuit of more," explains why focus erodes without anyone deciding to lose it. More needs no decision; it simply arrives. Choosing fewer things is the only step here that requires a deliberate act, which is why McKeown puts the word discipline at the center.$$

[[reflect]]
- Think of a recent win. What requests or options has it brought, and are they now competing with the work that made the win possible?
- If your current commitments were drawn as arrows, how many directions would they point?
- Which of those arrows did you add because it looked reasonable on its own, without looking at the whole drawing?

&&Before the next new request lands, can you name the one direction your effort should point this month, and notice which commitment no longer fits it?&&

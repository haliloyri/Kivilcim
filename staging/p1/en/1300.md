Hans Rosling puts a simple question to a well-educated audience: what share of the world's one-year-olds have been vaccinated? There are three answers to choose from. Then he brings in an unusual rival for comparison: a chimpanzee, picking at random.

[[story]]
The chimpanzee is at the heart of *Factfulness*, which Rosling wrote with Ola Rosling and Anna Rosling Rönnlund. It is not a real animal in a real test. It is a thought experiment about pure guessing, and it turns out to be a humbling benchmark.

Rosling and his team asked thousands of people in many countries a set of multiple-choice questions about basic global trends. The audiences included highly educated people, experts, and leaders. Each question had three options.

The questions were not trick questions. What share of the world's one-year-olds are vaccinated? Over the last 20 years, has the share of people in extreme poverty roughly halved, doubled, or stayed about the same? What is global life expectancy today?

Here is where the chimpanzee comes in. With three options per question, anyone picking at random would average about a third correct. A chimpanzee pointing at answers would get roughly that score.

Most of the human groups did worse.

That result alone would be embarrassing. But the more interesting finding was in the pattern of the mistakes. If people were simply ignorant, their wrong answers would be scattered, landing on each wrong option about equally. That is not what happened.

##The wrong answers weren't random: people kept choosing the most negative option.##

Question after question, people believed the world was poorer, sicker, and more dangerous than it actually is. The errors all leaned the same way.

And education did not protect them. Being better informed, being an expert, holding a position of influence: none of it pulled people reliably above the chimpanzee's score.

This is what led Rosling to his diagnosis. The problem, he argues, is not a lack of knowledge that more facts could simply fill in. It is an "overdramatic worldview." It is shaped by a set of instincts in how we think, which he breaks down into ten, and by news that favours the dramatic over the slow and steady.

That explains the chimpanzee's strange advantage. The chimpanzee has no worldview at all. It has not read the headlines, so its guesses carry no tilt. The humans, with all their knowledge, carried a tilt into every question. A random guesser missed in every direction. The people in Rosling's audiences kept missing in one.

[[lessons]]
$$**One-way errors signal a bias.** If mistakes came from not knowing, they would spread across all the wrong answers. Rosling's audiences kept choosing the most negative option instead. When your errors all lean the same direction, the problem is less what you don't know and more the lens you're looking through. More facts alone won't straighten a tilted lens.$$

$$**Education isn't a shield.** Highly educated audiences, experts, and leaders also scored below what random guessing would give. Knowing a lot doesn't automatically correct a distorted picture of the wider world. Being informed and seeing the world accurately turned out to be different things, even for people whose job is to know.$$

$$**Drama crowds out slow progress.** Rosling traces the pattern to an overdramatic worldview, fed by instincts and by news that favours the dramatic. Slow, gradual change in things like vaccination or life expectancy rarely makes headlines. So the picture in our heads can lag far behind the numbers.$$

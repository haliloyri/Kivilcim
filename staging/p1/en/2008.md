[[open]]
A man who once studied at Harvard writes down how far he walks, how many flights of stairs he climbs and which sports he plays. Researchers turn those answers into a weekly number of calories burned, file it, and wait. They are not waiting for a test result. They are waiting to see how long he lives.

[[story]]
The wait lasted years. Ralph Paffenbarger and his colleagues followed 16,936 Harvard alumni, men aged 35 to 74, from 1962 to 1978, which gave them 12 to 16 years of follow-up per man. When they published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 1986, 1,413 of those men had died.

The researchers had asked about walking, stair climbing and sports play, and they converted the answers into kilocalories per week. Then they lined up the death rates against that number. Total mortality fell as activity rose, mostly through fewer deaths from cardiovascular and respiratory causes. The decline was steady from under 500 kilocalories a week up to 3,500. Past 3,500 the rates crept up again slightly.

Alumni who spent 2,000 kilocalories or more a week on exercise had death rates one quarter to one third lower than less active men. The obvious objection is that active men were simply the ones who did not smoke or who had healthy blood pressure. The authors checked. With or without allowance for hypertension, cigarette smoking, extremes or gains in body weight, or an early parental death, the physically active alumni still had significantly lower rates.

The paper also set the habits side by side. For an individual, the highest relative risks of death belonged to smokers and to men with high blood pressure. For the community as a whole, the largest share of attributable deaths fell on smokers and on sedentary men. By the authors' estimate, a man who got adequate exercise could expect, by the age of 80, one to somewhat more than two extra years of life compared with a sedentary one.

It helps to know what this study is. Nobody was assigned to exercise. The alumni reported their own activity, they were all men, and they all had a Harvard connection, so the numbers describe a pattern in one group and not a promise for anyone else.

Even so, the shape of the curve is worth a second look. Daniel Lieberman's *Exercised* is about the puzzle of why a species that evolved without gyms should need deliberate exercise, and a result like this shows why a simple slogan fails. Death rates fell, then flattened, then edged up. A line like that does not say that more is always better. It says there is a stretch where each added effort buys a lot, and a stretch where it buys much less.

[[lessons]]
$$**When effort pays you back, look for the point where the return flattens before you set a target.** In the alumni data, death rates dropped steadily as weekly activity climbed and then crept up at the highest levels. Many investments at work or in learning have the same shape: the first hours transform what you can do, and later ones add less and less. Instead of asking whether more is better, ask where the curve bends, because the answer is often hidden there rather than in any single number, and you can then spend your time where the line is still steep.$$

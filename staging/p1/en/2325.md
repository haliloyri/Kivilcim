[[open]]
A drug needed to cure a deadly infection has exactly one supplier, and each buyer orders a small amount on its own. Treating one patient with the full set of drugs costs about $13,000. To the people who set budgets, that number looks like a law of nature.

[[story]]
In Tracy Kidder's *Mountains Beyond Mountains*, Paul Farmer and his colleagues fight the claim that multidrug-resistant tuberculosis cannot be treated in poor countries. In 2000 the Bill and Melinda Gates Foundation gave Partners In Health and other groups a grant of about $45 million to fight MDR-TB in Lima. This story is about the other front, the price of the drugs themselves.

The second-line drugs used against MDR-TB were costly, and treating one patient came to around $13,000. On 19 July 2001, a paper appeared in the journal *Science* titled "Responding to Market Failures in Tuberculosis Control." Its authors came from the World Health Organization, Harvard Medical School and Médecins Sans Frontières.

The paper did not ask drug makers for charity. It described a system. Médecins Sans Frontières acted as negotiator for all the parties: it pooled the demand of many buyers, negotiated prices, advanced the funds for bulk purchases and gave technical support. The World Health Organization created a regulatory body, the Green Light Committee, to keep access to these drugs safe.

The results were specific. Cycloserine, which at the time came from only one supplier, fell by 96 percent, from $3.38 to $0.14. Ofloxacin, where competition was introduced through tendering, fell by 87.25 percent, from $2.60 to $0.33. The release announcing the paper spoke of cuts of up to 96 percent and projected that countries could save as much as 93.6 percent if they kept their 1998 to 2000 spending trend. For Nicaragua it estimated that second-line drugs, 14.9 percent of the TB control budget, would drop to 2.7 percent.

Notice what changed and what did not. The molecules were identical, the patients were identical, and the manufacturers were still companies that wanted to sell. What changed was the buyer: instead of many small orders from poor health ministries, there was one large, organized order backed by money paid up front. As Bernard Pécoul of MSF put it at the time, with an organized system of procurement, prices can be reduced dramatically.

A later analysis found that prices for some of these drugs rose again after 2001, so the gain needed maintenance. Still, the 2001 numbers showed that the "too expensive" in "too expensive to treat" was partly a description of the market.

[[lessons]]
$$**When a price or a cost looks fixed, ask who is buying and whether they could buy together.** A cost that blocks a good decision is often the product of fragmented demand, with each small buyer paying the retail rate. Pooling orders, advancing funds and tendering competition changed what the same drug cost without changing the drug. You can try the same move with a software license, a supplier contract or a shared service. Call the other people who face the same bill, and compare what each of you pays before you accept the quote.$$

[[open]]
On an April Fools' Day, a company unveils an email service, and a good share of the public takes it for a joke. The inbox it promises holds thousands of messages, while the competition's holds a few dozen. By evening, readers are phoning a news agency to say it has been fooled.

[[story]]
Google unveiled Gmail on 1 April 2004, April Fools' Day. Every account came with 1 GB of storage, enough for about 13,500 emails. Yahoo and Microsoft's webmail held roughly 30 to 60. That is 250 to 500 times more, and plenty of people read the announcement as a prank.

The project's code name was Caribou, after a running gag in the comic strip *Dilbert*. It took three years, and it was led by Paul Buchheit, the 23rd person Google had hired. Gmail used Google's search technology to find old messages and grouped related ones into conversations. One early design that Larry Page showed had no delete button at all, since this much storage plus search made deleting unnecessary.

On launch day the Associated Press sent a reporter to the headquarters in Mountain View, where Page demonstrated the product. The agency ran its story late that afternoon. Then readers called and emailed to say the AP had been duped. The product was real.

Behind the big promise sat a very modest setup. Buchheit later put it this way: "When we launched, we only had 300 machines and they were really old machines that no one else wanted." The capacity covered only 10,000 users, which he called "a little absurd." A huge promise was running on tired hardware that nobody else was using.

That limit shaped the first months. Invitations were being sold on eBay for $250 each, and Buchheit described them as a kind of "social currency." Gmail opened to everyone only in 2007. He also said part of the charm was making a product that people would not believe is real.

The team did not try to be extravagant everywhere. It chose one dimension, storage, and pushed it far past anything the competition offered, then left everything else plain: old machines, a small user count, entry by invitation only. The narrow capacity even worked in the product's favour, because scarcity made people want in. A single bold promise, carried by a modest setup, did the work that a long list of features might not have done.

[[lessons]]
$$**Make one promise truly large and keep everything around it modest.** A baker with a small shop could stop growing the menu and instead bake one loaf that the whole neighbourhood talks about, leaving the display plain. That choice removes the pressure to shine everywhere, and your time, money and attention can flow into a single point. The remaining parts stop competing for the same hours, and it becomes easier to say no to extras without guilt. When you start something, ask which one thing should astonish people and which parts can run perfectly well on old machines.$$

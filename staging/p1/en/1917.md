At supper in a North African town, with the war already lost, a man argues with unusual force that only the good are free and the bad are all slaves. The listeners around the table gradually go quiet, because they hear something under the words.

[[story]]
Ryan Holiday and Stephen Hanselman's *The Daily Stoic* gives a good deal of space to Cato the Younger, the Roman senator who became a model of Stoic firmness. This story is about the last evening of his life, in the city of Utica in 46 BC, as the Greek writer Plutarch tells it.

Julius Caesar had won the civil war. Cato, who had opposed him to the end, was in Utica with his companions and the town's magistrates. A kinsman of Caesar, Lucius, was about to go to Caesar on behalf of the town's three hundred, and he asked Cato to fall at Caesar's knees and beg for himself too. Cato refused, saying he would not be under an obligation to a tyrant for acts the tyrant had no right to commit. He offered instead to work out with Lucius how to win mercy for the three hundred.

Towards evening he went to the bath and, while there, asked whether a friend, Statyllius, had been sent away to safety. Told that Statyllius refused to go and would do whatever Cato did, Cato smiled and said they would see about that presently.

At supper, with all his companions and the magistrates of Utica around him, the talk turned to the Stoic paradoxes. Cato argued with vehemence that the good man alone is free and that the bad are all slaves, even when one of the philosophers present objected. It was a doctrine he had lived by for years. At that table, with Caesar's victory behind them, it carried a different weight, and Plutarch says his intensity made everyone understand that he had decided on his own end.

What followed is told with care. Afterwards he tried to calm the company by voicing concern for those who would travel by sea and across the desert. He embraced his son and his friends with unusual warmth. He went to his room and read Plato. When Cato sent his son and friends away, he kept only two philosophers, Demetrius and Apollonides, and argued with them that the decision was his to make before sending them off as well.

Around midnight he did something that is easy to miss. He sent his freedmen to the harbour to find out whether his friends' ships had sailed. They came back with news that most had gone, though a storm was blowing, and that was the last practical matter he settled. Cato died that night, by his own hand, and the story of those last hours is a grim one.

Plutarch does not hide how heavy the evening was. Still, look at the order of it. Before he acted on what he believed, he spent the day and then the night making sure that other people had a way out. His principle was his own to pay for, and he did not leave the bill with them.

[[lessons]]
$$**Count the cost of your principles, and see that the people near you are safe before you pay it.** A manager who decides to resign over a matter of conscience can first prepare a clean handover, so the team is not left exposed to the consequences. A friend who plans to speak up against something wrong in a club or a family can check, ahead of time, that others will not suffer for it. Your convictions are yours, but the damage they cause can land on someone else, and a few minutes of planning can spare them that, which is a small price for a conviction you mean to keep.$$

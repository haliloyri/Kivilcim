A troop train stops at a station lunchroom. At the tables in the dining room sit about two dozen prisoners, men in captured enemy uniforms, being served. Nine soldiers in American uniforms are shown to the kitchen instead, where they eat out of sight.

[[story]]
The soldiers were Black, and the prisoners were German. TIME magazine's Matthew Taub has retold the episode. In 1944 Corporal Rupert Trimmingham and eight other Black soldiers were travelling from Camp Claiborne in Louisiana to Fort Huachuca in Arizona. They stopped to eat, and they ate in the lunchroom's kitchen. About two dozen German prisoners of war, in the same building, were served at tables in the dining room.

Trimmingham wrote to the soldiers' magazine *Yank* in April 1944. One of his questions was "Then why are they treated better than we are?" The New Yorker published a fictionalised short story based on the incident on 17 June 1944, so the letter did not stay in the soldiers' mailbag.

It was not a single bad afternoon, and it was not one unusual lunchroom manager. Other reports from the period describe the same pattern at bases and on trains. Segregation was official policy in the US armed forces until President Truman's executive order in 1948. The Tuskegee Airman Charles Dryden recalled in his 1997 memoir *A-Train* a base in South Carolina where prisoners of war could walk into the "White" side of the post exchange cafeteria while Black soldiers could not. In 2004 the historian Matthias Reiss published a study of letters from Black soldiers that documents this kind of treatment.

Think about the logic the contrast exposes, because it is the whole point of the story. A rule that keeps Black soldiers out of a dining room might claim all sorts of reasons: custom, order, comfort. None of those reasons explains why men in the enemy's uniform could sit down. Only one rule fits both facts, and it is an uncomfortable one: the line was drawn around who counted as white, not around who was a friend, or a soldier, or a guest.

In the vocabulary of caste, which Wilkerson uses for American life, this is how a hierarchy behaves. Its ranking sits above the loyalties and rules the society says it believes in, and it overrides them without having to announce itself. The prisoner, whose country was at war with America, still stood above the American in uniform, and nobody had to say why. The ranking did the explaining by itself.

[[lessons]]
$$**An exception shows what a rule was really protecting.** When the same rule bends for one group and not for another, the reason given for the rule stops explaining anything, and the real purpose becomes visible. At work it shows when a deadline policy is waived for a favourite and enforced for everyone else, and at home when a house rule applies to one sibling and quietly not to another. Look at who the rule lets through, and ask what they have in common that the stated reason does not explain. You can ask it without accusing anyone, and it usually leads to the real purpose.$$

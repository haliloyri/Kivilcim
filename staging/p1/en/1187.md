Many people are quietly waiting for a particular day: the day they finally stop feeling afraid. Then, they tell themselves, they'll change jobs, speak up, start the project, have the conversation. Susan Jeffers built a whole book on some bad news about that day. It isn't coming.

[[story]]
Susan Jeffers was a psychologist who taught a course on fear. Her students came from every kind of life, and they were afraid of every kind of thing: public speaking, ending relationships, changing careers, being alone, asking for what they wanted. Out of that work came her book, *Feel the Fear and Do It Anyway*.

Jeffers noticed that behind all these very different fears sat the same basic belief. At the bottom of every fear, she wrote, is the simple thought: *I can't handle it.* I can't handle rejection. I can't handle failing. I can't handle being alone. The specific situation changes. The core fear stays the same.

That led her to a conclusion. If the real problem is the belief that you can't handle what happens, then the solution isn't to make fear disappear. It's to build trust that you can handle whatever comes.

She set out what she called **five truths about fear**.

##The first is the one that surprises people most: the fear will never go away as long as you continue to grow.##

Every time you step into something new, fear comes with you. That isn't a sign that something is wrong. It's a sign that you're stretching.

The second: the only way to get rid of the fear of doing something is to go out and do it. Fear shrinks after action, not before.

The third: the only way to feel better about yourself is to go out and do it. Confidence doesn't arrive first and then allow you to act. It's built from having acted.

The fourth: you're not the only one. Whenever you're on unfamiliar ground, you'll feel fear, and so does everyone else. The people who seem fearless are usually just people who act while afraid.

And the fifth: pushing through fear is less frightening than living with the underlying fear that comes from a feeling of helplessness. Staying stuck feels safe, but it carries its own constant, low-level dread: the sense that life is happening to you and you can't do anything about it.

Jeffers's conclusion gave the book its title. Don't wait for the fear to go. Feel it, and do it anyway.

[[lessons]]
$$**Fear is a sign of growth, not a stop sign.** Jeffers's first truth, that fear will never go away as long as you keep growing, removes the illusion that one day you'll feel ready. If you only act when you're unafraid, you'll wait forever. Treating fear as the normal companion of anything new makes it far less paralyzing.$$

$$**Action shrinks fear; waiting feeds it.** The only way to get rid of the fear of doing something, Jeffers says, is to do it. Confidence follows action rather than preceding it. Each time you act while afraid, you collect evidence that you can handle it, and the next step gets a little easier.$$

$$**Helplessness is the bigger fear.** Her fifth truth reframes the choice. Staying stuck isn't actually safe. It brings a constant background fear of being powerless. Pushing through a specific, temporary fear is usually less frightening than living with that helplessness every day.$$

[[open]]
Picture two groups of kids who have just done equally well on the same set of puzzles. An adult leans in and says one warm, encouraging sentence to each group. The two sentences are almost the same length, and both sound kind. By the end of the session, one group is reaching for harder problems. The other is backing away from them, and some of those kids are quietly lying about their scores.

[[story]]
This comes from Carol Dweck's book *Mindset: The New Psychology of Success*, and it's one of the central studies she describes.

Dweck's team worked with hundreds of students, mostly early adolescents. Each student got a set of fairly challenging problems taken from a nonverbal IQ test. These weren't trivial, but most of the kids did quite well on them. At this point the groups were equal. Same kind of kids, same kind of performance.

Then came the one thing the researchers changed. After the first set, one group heard praise for their ability:

> "You must be smart at this."

The other group heard praise for their effort:

> "You must have worked really hard."

That was it. One line.

Right after the praise, every student was offered a choice about what to do next. They could take an easier task they would surely do well on, or a challenging task they could learn from. Most of the kids praised for being smart picked the safe, easy one. A large majority of the kids praised for effort picked the challenging one. The effect showed up before anyone had struggled at all. The praise alone had already changed what the kids were willing to risk.

Next, all the students got a set of much harder problems. Nobody did well on them, in either group. What differed was how the kids made sense of that.

The ability-praised students decided they weren't smart after all. If being good at the first set meant they were smart, then struggling on this set had to mean the opposite. They also stopped enjoying the problems. The effort-praised students read the same struggle differently. To them it meant they needed to try harder. Many of them said the hard problems were the ones they liked best.

Then the researchers gave everyone a final set of problems, just as easy as the very first set. This is where it stopped being only about feelings. The ability-praised students now did worse than they had at the start. The effort-praised students did better. Two groups that began at the same level had moved in opposite directions.

There was one more finding. The students were asked to report their scores to peers, and many of the ability-praised kids inflated their numbers. Dweck points out what that means: ##a single line of praise led ordinary children to lie.## Once being smart had become who they were, a low score was something to hide.

Her conclusion is that praising intelligence pushes children toward a fixed mindset, the belief that ability is a set amount you either have or don't. Praising effort and process supports a growth mindset, where difficulty is part of learning.

[[lessons]]
$$**"Smart" makes people guard an image.** The ability-praised kids hadn't failed at anything yet when they picked the easy task. They had just been told they were smart, and suddenly there was something to lose. A challenging task might expose them, so they avoided it. Praise for ability ties a person's worth to looking capable, and the safest way to keep looking capable is to never attempt much.$$

$$**Effort praise turns difficulty into information.** Both groups hit the same wall on the much harder problems, and nobody did well. The effort-praised kids took the struggle to mean *try harder*, and many said those were their favorite problems. They had been praised for something they controlled, so a hard problem said nothing bad about who they were. It just told them what to do next.$$

$$**The words show up in the results.** It would be easy to treat this as a matter of mood. But on the final set, which was as easy as the first, the effort group improved and the ability group got worse. The same children, with the same starting scores, ended up performing differently because of one sentence. Add the kids who inflated their scores, and you can see the praise shaping behavior, not only feelings.$$

[[reflect]]
- Think back to growing up. Were you mostly called talented, or were you noticed for trying hard? How does that show up when you face something difficult now?
- Recall the last time you picked the easier option mainly so you wouldn't look incapable. What would the harder option have taught you?
- Have you ever shaded the truth about how something went because admitting the struggle felt like admitting you weren't good enough?

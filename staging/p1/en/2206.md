[[open]]
In a London laboratory, a visitor is shown an X-ray photograph of a DNA fibre. He will later write that his mouth fell open and his pulse began to race. The woman whose group produced the picture does not know it is being shown, and the photograph is about to reach Cambridge by two separate routes.

[[story]]
James Watson recalled the moment in *The Double Helix*, his 1968 memoir: "The instant I saw the picture my mouth fell open and my pulse began to race." The picture was Photo 51, an X-ray diffraction image of the B form of DNA, taken at King's College London on 2 May 1952 by Raymond Gosling, the PhD student of Rosalind Franklin.

How it reached Watson is where accounts diverge. By Gosling's later telling, Franklin had set the photograph aside because she was concentrating on the A form of DNA. When she was preparing to leave King's, Gosling, who was now reporting to Maurice Wilkins, handed the picture to Wilkins. John Randall, who ran the laboratory, had asked Gosling to share his data with Wilkins. Other writers describe the same event differently: Wilkins, they say, showed Watson her photograph without her permission. Both versions agree that she was not aware of it at the time.

The visit took place on 30 January 1953, when Watson came to King's and Wilkins showed him the print. A second route to Cambridge opened soon after, and it was a different kind of material: numbers instead of an image. In mid-February Max Perutz gave Francis Crick a copy of a report from a Medical Research Council committee that had visited King's in December 1952. It contained Franklin's calculations and confirmed the 34 Ångström repeat and the two-fold symmetry of the structure. One account says Perutz did not consider it confidential.

Everything above is documented. What those items were worth is not settled. A 2023 commentary in *Nature* argues that, when The Double Helix is read carefully, Photo 51 played little or no part in the way Watson and Crick moved toward the correct structure between January and March 1953. Others treat the picture as decisive.

The useful detail is how ordinary the transfer was. Nobody in this chain stole a notebook. A student passed a print to his supervisor, a visitor was shown it, and a committee report went from one colleague to another. Each step, taken alone, was small, and that is how the owner of the work ended up as the last to know about it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask the owner before you pass on someone else's unfinished work.** Routine handoffs inside a group can still carry a person's results to a place they never agreed to send them. A designer who forwards a colleague's early mock-up to a client, with good intentions and in a hurry, may leave that colleague finding out from the client. A short message such as "may I show this?" takes a minute, and nobody is surprised later by where their work ended up. It also gives the owner a chance to say what is still unfinished, which helps whoever receives it read the material correctly.$$

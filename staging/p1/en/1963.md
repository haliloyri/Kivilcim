[[open]]
Four numbers, each an average, each a percentage of how narrow a heart artery had become. Two come from one group of patients and two from another, measured a year apart on angiograms. In one group the figure drifted down, in the other it crept up. The gaps look tiny on the page, and the whole argument sits inside them.

[[story]]
Dean Ornish's Lifestyle Heart Trial is one of the best-known tests behind the lifestyle approach to heart disease that Michael Greger's *How Not to Die* argues for. Its question was blunt: could the narrowed arteries of people with coronary disease get wider without cholesterol-lowering drugs? Forty-eight patients with moderate to severe disease took part, at two university medical centres, in a trial that ran from 1986 to 1992.

Twenty-eight of them were assigned to the full programme: a low-fat vegetarian diet, no smoking, stress management training and moderate exercise. The other twenty were a usual-care control group. Nobody in the programme was given a lipid-lowering drug.

After one year the researchers went back to the angiograms. They analysed 195 lesions and measured how much of the artery's diameter each one blocked. In the programme group the average narrowing fell from 40.0 to 37.8 percent. In the control group it rose from 42.7 to 46.1 percent, and 82 percent of the programme patients showed an average change toward regression.

The worst lesions, those blocking more than half the artery, followed the same pattern. They went from 61.1 to 55.8 percent in the programme group and from 61.7 to 64.4 percent among the controls.

A follow-up in 1998 carried the trial to five years. By then 35 of the 48 had completed the final angiography, 20 of the 28 in the programme group and 15 of the 20 controls. The programme group had kept its changes up, while the controls had made only more moderate ones. Their average narrowing had improved by 7.9 percent in relative terms, and the controls' had worsened by 27.7 percent. Cardiac events also split: 25 among the 28 programme patients, 45 among the 20 controls.

Read those numbers with their limits in view. Two percentage points is a small movement, and the trial was small, with a bundle of changes that cannot be pulled apart to say whether the diet, the exercise or the stress training did the work. What gives the result its weight is the comparison. Without the control group, a drop of two points would be a curiosity, but next to a group whose arteries narrowed over the same year it becomes a finding.

[[lessons]]
$$**A number only means something next to the group that did not get the treatment.** The programme group's improvement is small enough to dismiss on its own, and the same figure looks different once you see that the controls moved the other way. The same logic applies to a new routine at work, a new diet in the family or a change in how you study: ask what would have happened over the same stretch without it. And ask the second question as well, which part of a bundle actually did the work, because a package that works can still hide the one piece that matters.$$

A retired German central banker sits in an office in Kinshasa with a pen and a list of names. Banks in the country are to stop dealing with about fifty companies and political figures, and some of those companies belong to relatives of the president. The list is his to sign, or so the paperwork says.

[[story]]
Bruce Bueno de Mesquita and Alastair Smith, in *The Dictator's Handbook*, argue that a ruler survives by keeping a small circle of backers paid. A real episode from Zaire shows what happens when an outsider tries to cut off that payment. It begins on 14 August 1978, when Erwin Blumenthal arrived in Kinshasa.

Blumenthal was a German who had headed the foreign department of the Bundesbank, and he came as an IMF-sponsored adviser to the Banque du Zaire. His title was principal director, and Euromoney reported that he effectively ran the central bank. On 1 September 1978 he issued a circular meant to route the country's foreign exchange to the bank itself: importers could no longer open letters of credit without holding the currency, and 30 per cent of export earnings had to be handed over.

Then he went after the list. On 29 November 1978 he barred commercial banks from doing business with about 50 companies and political figures, among them firms linked to Citoyen Lito, an uncle-in-law of President Mobutu Sese Seko. Euromoney also reported that Zaire's foreign exchange had paid for a $13 million Hercules transport plane for Lito that September.

The test came over food. According to Euromoney, Blumenthal refused Lito foreign currency for food imports. Mobutu reportedly sided with him at first, then backed down and ordered the currency released. Blumenthal declined to comment on that account.

On 15 April 1979, according to published accounts, he wrote a confidential letter to Jacques de Larosière, the managing director of the IMF, about his eight months in the central bank. He left Zaire in June 1979, having first arrived in August 1978. By then the country had had no workable agreement with the IMF for over a year.

The adviser had a title, a team and the Fund's name behind him. What he did not have was any hold over the people the president depended on. A man who decides who may spend the country's foreign currency is deciding who gets paid, and Mobutu was not willing to let a foreigner decide that for his relatives. The president did not need to dismiss the reform, only to overrule it once, on something as small as a food order.

[[lessons]]
$$**Find out who controls the money before you accept a mandate to change it.** A new head of a family firm may be handed a title and a signing pen, yet the old payroll of cousins and favoured suppliers can still sit with the owner. Whoever holds that payroll can overrule you in one phone call, and then everyone else in the building watches who won, and adjusts. Before you take a reform job, ask who benefits from the current arrangement and who can reverse your first decision, and get the answer settled in advance, in writing if you can.$$

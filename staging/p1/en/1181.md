Two novelists meet at a public event and greet each other with a kiss on the cheek. Years later, one of them publishes a novel that the other had started, abandoned, and never told anyone about in detail. Elizabeth Gilbert tells this story with delight, because she thinks she knows what happened.

[[story]]
In *Big Magic*, Elizabeth Gilbert offers a playful, slightly mystical theory of ideas. She suggests we imagine ideas as a kind of disembodied life form. They have no body, but they have a will. They want to be made real. And to do that, they need a human collaborator.

So an idea comes to you, and tries to get your attention. If you say yes and start working, you enter a partnership. But if you ignore it for too long, because you're busy or afraid or distracted, the idea may eventually give up on you and go looking for someone else.

Gilbert tells a story from her own life to illustrate this.

Some years earlier, she had begun a novel. It was about a middle-aged woman from Minnesota who is in love with her married boss, and who gets drawn into a doomed highway construction project in the Amazon jungle in the 1960s. Gilbert was excited about it. She did research, made plans and started writing.

Then life intervened. Personal circumstances pulled her away from the book, and she set it aside for a long time. When she finally came back to it, something had changed. The idea had gone cold. She couldn't find the life in it anymore. It had, she felt, left.

Around that time, she had become friends with the novelist Ann Patchett. They had first met at a public event, where they'd shared a kiss on the cheek.

##Some time later, Gilbert learned what Patchett was working on: a novel set in the Amazon jungle, about a woman from Minnesota in love with her married boss.##

The book became Patchett's novel *State of Wonder*. It wasn't the same story Gilbert had planned, but the core was startlingly similar.

Gilbert doesn't accuse anyone of anything. Patchett had never heard about her idea. Instead, Gilbert suggests, with a smile, that the idea simply moved. Tired of waiting, it passed from her to Patchett, perhaps in that very kiss, and found someone ready to give it full attention.

Whether you believe that or not, Gilbert finds the lesson useful. When an idea comes to you and you love it, don't assume it will wait forever. Give it your time. Show up for it. And if it does leave, let it go generously. It went to someone who was ready.

[[lessons]]
$$**An idea's invitation can expire.** Gilbert's Amazon novel went cold while she was away from it. Whether or not ideas really wander, the experience is familiar: the excitement fades if you don't act. Treating a good idea as something that won't wait forever is a strong reason to start now rather than someday.$$

$$**Ideas are honored by work, not by thinking about them.** Patchett didn't have a better idea than Gilbert. She gave it steady attention and turned it into a finished book. What makes an idea real isn't how much you love it, but how consistently you work on it.$$

$$**Hold ideas lightly and generously.** Gilbert could have been bitter. Instead, she was delighted that the idea found a home. Seeing ideas as shared rather than owned makes it easier to celebrate others and to keep creating, instead of guarding one idea jealously.$$

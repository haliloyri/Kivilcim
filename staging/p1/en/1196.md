Top athletes track everything: every meal, every heartbeat, every training minute. Matthew Walker argues that one of the most powerful things they can do for their performance happens after the training is over, the lights are off and they're lying perfectly still.

[[story]]
In *Why We Sleep*, Matthew Walker devotes a chapter to what sleep does for the body, and he makes a bold claim. Sleep, he argues, is one of the most effective performance enhancers there is, and it's completely legal.

You may have read about star athletes who say they sleep ten hours or more. Whatever their exact habits, Walker's point doesn't depend on any one celebrity. It rests on research.

First, endurance. Walker describes studies showing that when people get less sleep than they need, their time to physical exhaustion drops. They tire sooner. Their bodies are less able to sustain effort, and their performance declines. Their lungs, muscles and ability to cool themselves all work less well after a short night.

Second, skill. When you practice a movement, like a tennis serve, a golf swing or a piano passage, your brain doesn't finish learning it during practice. Walker explains that during sleep, especially certain stages of non-REM sleep, the brain replays and strengthens those movements. People often perform a newly learned skill better after a night of sleep than they did at the end of practice itself, without any extra training.

##Practice gives the brain the raw material. Sleep turns it into skill.##

Third, injury. Walker cites research on young athletes that found a striking link between sleep and injuries. Those who regularly slept less had a much higher chance of getting hurt over the course of a season than those who slept more. Tired bodies react more slowly, coordinate less well and recover less fully.

And fourth, recovery. Sleep supports muscle repair and the body's hormonal and inflammatory balance. Training creates small amounts of damage; sleep is a major part of how the body repairs it and comes back stronger.

Walker notes that many professional sports teams have started to take this seriously, bringing in sleep specialists, adjusting travel schedules and treating sleep as part of training rather than as the time left over after it.

The lesson isn't only for athletes. Anyone learning a skill, from a musician to a surgeon to a student, is relying on sleep to finish the work their practice started. Cutting sleep to fit in more practice can mean learning less, not more.

[[lessons]]
$$**Sleep is a performance enhancer.** Walker's research shows that short sleep reduces endurance and makes people tire faster. Athletes and anyone doing demanding physical work lose performance they can't make up with extra effort. More sleep is one of the few improvements that costs nothing and has no side effects.$$

$$**Sleep finishes what practice starts.** The brain replays and strengthens new movements during sleep, which is why a skill can improve overnight. Trading sleep for more practice can backfire: you give your brain more raw material, then take away the time it needs to turn that material into real skill.$$

$$**Tired bodies get hurt.** The study of young athletes found a much higher injury risk among those who slept less. Fatigue slows reactions and weakens coordination. For anyone training hard, protecting sleep isn't a luxury. It's part of staying healthy enough to keep training at all.$$

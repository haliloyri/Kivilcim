Relatives and friends have seen the fund manager on television, and they all have the same question for him. It is about the car company he has just recommended, and it comes out as a warning: do you not know that it is going bankrupt?

[[story]]
Peter Lynch, whose *One Up on Wall Street* sorts stocks into six kinds, counts the turnaround among them: a company that is recovering from serious trouble. He described his own Chrysler position in an interview with PBS's *Frontline*. In October 1982, he said, Chrysler was his biggest holding, and he recommended it on television. As he remembered it, the stock was around $10.

The reaction at home was blunt. Relatives and friends asked him, in his words, "But how could you ever recommend Chrysler? Don't you know they're going bankrupt?" The worry was not silly. Three years earlier Congress had approved a federal loan guarantee of $1.5 billion to keep the company alive, and a company that needs the government to vouch for its debts looks to most people like a company on its way out.

Yet the guarantee had also bought time, which is what a turnaround needs most. Chrysler repaid the federally guaranteed loans in 1983, about seven years ahead of schedule, according to several accounts of the rescue. The government came out ahead as well: a 1985 article in Reason magazine counts $311 million from the sale of stock warrants plus $25 million in guarantee fees. Lynch's own verdict on the position was short: "it worked out fine."

He came back to it. In October 1990 he recommended Chrysler again, after a stock split, and the price was again around $10. That makes two public calls on the same company, eight years apart, each made while a lot of people still pictured the old headline.

Look at what the worry was about. It concerned one question, whether Chrysler would survive, and for a company in the turnaround category that is the question that decides the stock. A loan guarantee and a repayment schedule are facts about survival. They could be looked up, and they said something different from the headline the relatives remembered.

There was a cost to being early and loud as well. A manager who tells a television audience to buy a company the public expects to fail has made his opinion easy to check. Anyone who disagreed could simply wait and see, and relatives and friends were free to raise it with him, as they did.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask what has changed since the headline was written.** A tenant who once heard that a corner shop was in trouble may keep avoiding it for years, although the owner has long since paid off the debt and refilled the shelves. Old news stays in people's heads as if it were current, and the person who checks the present facts sees something the crowd does not. Before you accept or repeat a verdict about a company, a colleague or a project, look for one fact from this year that the verdict ignores. If you find none, the verdict has earned its place; if you find one, you have a better question to ask.$$

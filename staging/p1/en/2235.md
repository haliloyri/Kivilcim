[[story]]
In 1975 a Kodak engineer named Steven Sasson built a battery-operated, hand-held digital camera. It weighed about eight pounds and recorded black-and-white pictures of 100 by 100 pixels, ten thousand in all, using a sensor from Fairchild. Each image went onto a cassette tape and took about twenty-three seconds to record. Kodak filed a patent for an electronic still camera in 1977, naming Sasson and a colleague, Gareth Lloyd, as inventors.

So the company that dominated film photography had, very early, a working prototype of the technology that would later replace film. According to published accounts, executives were hesitant to commit. A full move to digital would have meant heavy spending for a small market, and it would have set Kodak against established computer hardware companies. Film, by contrast, was a business Kodak knew and ran well, with customers, factories and habits built around it.

The record also shows the company did see the future. In 1979 another employee, Larry Matteson, wrote a report predicting a complete shift to digital photography by 2010. And the common claim that Kodak refused to touch digital is a misconception: it was actively involved in developing and producing digital cameras. What it did not manage was to turn early knowledge into a full change of what it was.

The numbers tell the later part. Kodak's cash reserves fell from $1.6 billion in January 2001 to $957 million by June 2011. On 19 January 2012 it filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection in New York, and during the proceedings it sold many of its patents for about $525 million. Sasson himself retired from the company in 2009.

The interesting question is not who was right in 1975. The interesting question is what separates having an invention from acting on it. An invention is a fact in a laboratory, but a strategy is a list of things you agree to stop doing. Each year of delay felt rational, because the film business was real and paid the bills, while the digital market was small. Delay of that kind rarely looks like a decision, and nobody ever signs a memo that says we have chosen to lose the lead. It looks like waiting for the numbers to improve, and the numbers improve only after others have taken the market.

[[lessons]]
$$**Put a date on the day your forecast turns into a decision, and name what you will give up on that day.** A company, a team or a person can see a change coming for years and still do nothing, because acting always costs something that is real today while the benefit is a prediction. If you notice an early signal in your own field, say a tool your team distrusts or a skill your role will need, write down the point at which you will start shifting time and budget. Without that date and that sacrifice, foresight becomes a document no one acts on.$$

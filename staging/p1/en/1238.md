Take thirty ordinary steps and you've walked about thirty meters. Now take thirty steps where each one is double the length of the one before. You won't end up across the room. You'll end up, roughly, a billion units away. Ray Kurzweil believes that gap explains why the future keeps surprising us.

[[story]]
In *The Singularity Is Near*, Ray Kurzweil, an inventor and futurist, argues that much of technology doesn't improve at a steady pace. It improves exponentially: doubling, again and again, at regular intervals. He calls this pattern the **law of accelerating returns**.

The problem, he says, is that human intuition is linear. We expect the future to change at about the same speed as the recent past. If something improved a little last year, we assume it will improve a little next year. That works well for many things in daily life. But it badly misjudges anything that grows exponentially.

To show the difference, he uses the steps. Thirty linear steps take you to thirty. Thirty doublings take you to about a billion. The two paths start out looking almost identical. One, two, three... versus one, two, four, eight. For a while, the difference hardly seems worth noticing. Then it becomes enormous.

Kurzweil also tells a parable. A man owns a lake and wants to stay home to protect its fish. The trouble is lily pads, which double in number every day. At first, there are only a few, covering a tiny corner of the lake. Days pass. The lake still looks mostly clear. Friends tell him not to worry, and he feels safe leaving for a while.

##But a lake that's half covered one day is completely covered the next.##

With doubling growth, the lake looks nearly clear for most of the time and then fills up in the final few days. By the time the change is obvious, it's almost over. That's what makes exponential growth so easy to underestimate: in its early stages, it looks small, slow, even trivial. People dismiss it. Then, suddenly, it's everywhere.

Kurzweil argues that computing power, and many technologies built on it, have followed this kind of curve for decades, and he uses this to make bold predictions about the coming decades. Those forecasts are hotly debated, and some critics argue that not every trend can keep doubling forever.

But even for skeptics, the core lesson is useful. When something is improving exponentially, judging it by how good it is today is a mistake. The better question is how fast it's improving, and what happens if that rate continues.

[[lessons]]
$$**Our instincts are linear.** We naturally expect tomorrow to change about as much as yesterday did. Kurzweil shows how badly that misjudges exponential trends: thirty doublings reach about a billion, while thirty steps reach thirty. Knowing your intuition has this blind spot is the first step toward correcting it.$$

$$**Exponential change looks slow, then sudden.** The lily pads cover the lake in the last few days after looking harmless for weeks. Early exponential growth seems too small to matter, which is exactly why people dismiss it. By the time it's obvious, it's often too late to prepare.$$

$$**Plan for acceleration, stay humble about forecasts.** Kurzweil's specific predictions are debated, and not every trend doubles forever. Still, asking "how fast is this improving?" rather than "how good is it now?" is a useful habit, whether you're looking at technology, skills or savings.$$

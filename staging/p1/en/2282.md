A boat with a swimming pool, a cinema and a helicopter pad is hard to hide, even in an ocean. On a Wednesday morning, local television showed police officers walking its decks off the Indonesian island of Bali. Thirty-four crew members were waiting to be questioned.

[[story]]
Among the things Tom Wright and Bradley Hope's *Billion Dollar Whale* follows from the 1MDB affair is the lavish spending of Jho Low, and this story is about his yacht, the *Equanimity*. On 28 February 2018, Indonesian police seized the 300-foot vessel off Bali at the request of the United States. Metro TV showed local police and what it identified as FBI officers on board.

The chase had been short. According to the national police's director of economic crimes, Agung Setya, Indonesia began working with US authorities a week earlier. The yacht was located on the Tuesday in waters off neighbouring Lombok and then moved to Tanjung Benoa on Bali, where police, holding a court order, boarded it. "We have legal confirmation that this asset is the result of a crime," Setya told the station. He put the yacht's worth at about $250 million.

The US Justice Department had named the vessel the year before in a civil case seeking to recover $540 million in assets. The DOJ alleged that Low, who had no formal role at 1MDB but considerable influence over it, bought the yacht for his personal use, and that it was part of more than $400 million he took for himself. Overall, the department says more than $4.5 billion was stolen from the fund. Low has denied wrongdoing. Charter websites described an Asian-themed interior, a swimming pool, a movie theater and a helicopter landing pad.

What happened next shows how quickly a symbol of wealth turns into a line in an inventory. A seizure is only the start, because someone still has to sell the thing. Malaysia's attorney general later said that the admiralty court in Kuala Lumpur approved an offer from the casino group Genting to buy the *Equanimity* for $126 million, roughly half the value the Indonesian police had given it.

There is something instructive in the two numbers. Police named $250 million as what the boat was worth on a ship's papers. The market, once the ship had to be sold through a court, paid $126 million. A thing that looks priceless in a photograph is worth what a buyer will pay on the day you are forced to sell.

[[lessons]]
$$**Value what you own at the price you would get if you had to sell it this week.** A carpenter might tally up the machines in his workshop at what he paid for them, and feel solid. The number that matters in a bad month is the one a dealer would offer today, and it is usually much lower. Doing that sum once a year, for equipment, a car or a stake in a friend's venture, shows how much of your sense of security rests on a price that only exists on paper. It also makes the next big purchase easier to judge, because you start asking what you could get back.$$

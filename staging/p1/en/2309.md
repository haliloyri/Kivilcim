A company sends its grand new project to another state. Back at headquarters, one engineer walks into the founder's office and asks for permission to build something smaller. He does not call it a rival to the big project. He calls it insurance, and the founder has to decide whether to believe him.

[[story]]
Tom West made a promise to Edson de Castro, the founder of Data General: his group could turn the company's 16-bit Eclipse minicomputer into a 32-bit machine within a year. Tracy Kidder's *The Soul of a New Machine* is the story of that machine, called Eagle. According to Wired's later retrospective, West described it to him as insurance.

The bet it insured was a much bigger one. Rival DEC had released its 32-bit VAX, and in 1978 Data General started its own answer, the Fountainhead Project, named after the Westborough, Massachusetts, apartment complex where it first lived. The company moved its lead development team to Research Triangle Park, North Carolina, and gave it the job of building an advanced 32-bit design to compete with the VAX.

Many of the top engineers, West among them, refused to uproot their families and move. That left a second group at home in Massachusetts. West's plan for it was modest on paper: take the 16-bit Eclipse up to 32 bits and keep it compatible with the machines customers already owned.

The year turned out to be a hopeful guess. The work took longer than he had promised. The machine that emerged would be sold as the Eclipse MV/8000.

Look at what the word "insurance" did. To de Castro, backing West did not mean doubting Fountainhead or giving up on North Carolina. A backup costs little and asks nobody to admit anything, which is also why it could be approved while the main bet was still running. For West, the same framing set a narrow target, a compatible 32-bit Eclipse, instead of a rival grand design, and a narrow target is one a small team can plausibly hit.

The price of the framing is visible too. A promise of one year made to win approval became a deadline the team had to live with, and the deadline slipped. A pitch that makes a project easy to say yes to can also make its schedule look easier than it is.

The numbers behind the choice are simple. One group was building the advanced design in North Carolina, and one was building the compatible upgrade in Massachusetts. If the first worked, the second would have cost a few people some months. If it did not, the second was the only 32-bit Eclipse the company would have. Seen that way, the insurance was cheap for the company and valuable for West, and that asymmetry is the whole logic of the pitch.

[[lessons]]
$$**Pitch a risky project as a backup for something already approved, and give it a narrow, checkable target.** Imagine a bakery owner who wants to try online orders while the shop's counter business is doing fine: asking for a small trial next to the counter gets a quicker yes than asking to rebuild the business. The smaller frame lowers the other person's risk, and it lets you start before anyone has settled the argument over the big plan. Only promise a date you could defend if the work turned out harder than it looked, because the date you give to win the yes is the one you will be measured by.$$

A bricklayer bends down for every single brick, and for every trowel of mortar too. His body weighs about 150 pounds, the brick about five. By Taylor's own arithmetic he lowers that weight two feet and raises it again roughly a thousand times a day.

[[story]]
Frank Gilbreth was a building contractor who had laid bricks himself in his youth, and Frederick Taylor gives him a long passage in his 1911 *Principles of Scientific Management*. Stanley McChrystal and his co-authors in *Team of Teams* tie the efficiency mindset of big institutions back to Taylor. Gilbreth was an engineer who backed Taylor's methods, and he took them to a trade that had barely changed for generations.

Taylor writes that bricklaying was about the last place anyone would expect to find a gain. Gilbreth went through every movement. He fixed exactly where the bricklayer's feet should stand in relation to the wall, the mortar box and the pile, so the man no longer stepped toward the bricks and back with each one. Then he designed an adjustable scaffold that kept brick, mortar, man and wall at the right height and distance from each other.

A laborer sorted the bricks as they came off the cars and stacked them best edge up on simple wooden frames, so the bricklayer could grab one without turning it over to inspect it. Gilbreth had the mortar tempered so that a brick settled into its bed under the pressure of a hand, with no need to tap it with the trowel handle. He also taught the bricklayer to take a brick in one hand while the other hand picked up the mortar.

According to Taylor, the motions needed per brick fell from eighteen to five, and in one case to two. ASME gives the same improvement as eighteen down to four and a half. The sources differ slightly, but they point the same way.

Gilbreth met Taylor in 1907 and supported his time study. The symbol of Taylor's approach was the stopwatch, and his concern was how long a task took. Gilbreth and his wife Lillian looked instead at how many motions the task needed. In 1914 they broke with Taylor and started their own scientific management company.

Notice where the gain came from. The bricklayer did not work any harder. The scaffold, the frames and the mortar did the work, and Taylor himself sorts the gains into three kinds: useless movements dropped, simple equipment added, and both hands used at once. The man was the same, and he did far less for each brick.

[[lessons]]
$$**When work is slow, change the setup around the person before you push the person.** A pharmacist who walks to the shelves, the till and the printer for every customer will not be helped by being told to walk faster. The same pharmacist, at a counter where everything sits within arm's reach tires less and makes fewer mistakes. Walk the job once yourself, slowly, and count the unnecessary steps in the place where the work happens before you press anyone, because the fix is usually there, and nobody ends up feeling blamed for a problem that belonged to the layout.$$

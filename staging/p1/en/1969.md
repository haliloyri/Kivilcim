In a Detroit laboratory, a professor straps himself into a sled that stops as hard as a car hitting a wall head-on. In the same building, researchers prepare donated bodies for the other half of the experiment, dressed in leotards with their faces covered. Both will end up in the same table of numbers.

[[story]]
Mary Roach's *Stiff* has a chapter called "Dead Man Driving", about the people and bodies that stand in for drivers when a car crashes. Wayne State University's bioengineering center in Detroit has studied how much impact a human body can take since 1939. One of its researchers was Lawrence Patrick, a biomechanics professor there from 1960 to 1975, whom Roach later wrote about as a human crash-test dummy.

The description was close to literal. Patrick took about 400 rides on a rapid-deceleration sled built to mimic a head-on crash into a wall. A 22-pound metal pendulum was swung into his chest, and he hurled a knee against a metal bar fitted with a load cell. Each test put a number on what a body in a car could stand.

Early crash research had relied on animals: chimpanzees, bears, pigs and Belgian hares. A conference in 1956 heard about a pig crashed into a steering wheel at about 10 miles an hour. Patrick's work put the same question to human bodies instead.

The donated bodies did what no volunteer could. In a windshield study from 1964, the researchers loosened the joints of the cadavers to approximate the resistance of a living person, and they replaced the brain with gelatin of similar weight and specific gravity. Among the figures from this work, a human skull could break through a windshield of that period at 12.9 miles an hour, and the rib cage could be compressed about 2.75 inches before injury.

The logic of the pairing is plain. A living volunteer can be taken only up to the edge of harm, and the closer the test gets to that edge, the more the volunteer carries the risk. A donated body can show where the harm actually begins. Put side by side, the two kinds of subject drew a line that neither could draw alone.

That line is now built into the dummies. Modern crash-test dummies are checked with calibration tests, such as a head drop, a thorax impact and a torso flexion test, so that they respond like the body they replace. Patrick's student Harold Mertz developed the Hybrid III, the standard dummy in use today. Patrick himself died in 2006 at the age of 85. The figure in a driver's seat during a test carries numbers that came from a professor who took the blows and from people who gave their bodies.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you ask others to take a risk, take the smallest real version of it yourself.** A restaurant owner who plans to change the closing routine can work one closing shift under the new rules before she announces anything. She will find the key that sticks and the step that takes twice as long as she assumed, and her staff will see that the plan was tried on a person first. Whenever you design something other people must live with, such as a schedule, a form or a handover, your own first run produces details and goodwill that no amount of planning on paper can.$$

A novelist finishes a big book and has no idea what to write next. No burning passion. No grand idea. What she does have is a mild interest in the plants in her garden. It doesn't seem like much. Years later, it has become a five-hundred-page novel.

[[story]]
In *Big Magic*, Elizabeth Gilbert takes on one of the most common pieces of creative advice: *follow your passion.* She thinks it's often well-meant but unhelpful. If you have a clear passion, wonderful. But many people don't. And being told to follow something you can't find can leave you feeling stuck, even defective.

So Gilbert offers an alternative. When passion is missing, she says, **follow your curiosity** instead.

Curiosity is gentler than passion. It doesn't demand that you change your life. It simply asks you to turn your head a little and look closer. What interests you, even slightly? What would you like to know more about? Curiosity, she writes, is like a scavenger hunt: each small clue leads to the next.

She tells how this worked in her own life.

At one point, without a clear creative direction, she found herself becoming curious about gardening. It wasn't a grand ambition. She simply started growing plants and enjoyed it. Then she began to wonder where some of those plants had come from originally. That led her to the history of plants and to the adventurous, sometimes strange world of eighteenth- and nineteenth-century botanical explorers, who traveled the globe collecting specimens.

##She kept following that thread, one small question at a time, for years.##

Eventually, all that curiosity became a novel: *The Signature of All Things*, a sweeping story about a woman botanist in the nineteenth century. It started, Gilbert says, with nothing more than a mild interest in her garden.

She also describes two kinds of creative people. Some are **jackhammers**: they have one consuming passion and pursue it relentlessly. Others are **hummingbirds**: they move from flower to flower, interest to interest, cross-pollinating ideas as they go. Our culture tends to celebrate jackhammers. Gilbert insists hummingbirds are just as valuable, and that many people who feel "scattered" are simply hummingbirds who haven't been told it's okay.

Her advice is simple. Don't wait for a lightning bolt of passion. Notice what you're curious about, and take one small step toward it. Then see where the next clue leads.

[[lessons]]
$$**Curiosity is a gentler guide than passion.** Passion demands certainty and commitment; curiosity only asks for a little attention. For people who can't find a clear passion, Gilbert's alternative removes enormous pressure. You don't have to know where you're going. You just have to follow what interests you now.$$

$$**Small interests can grow into big work.** Gilbert's mild interest in gardening led, clue by clue, to a major novel. Many significant projects start this way, as something small and seemingly unimportant. Taking tiny curiosities seriously enough to explore them is how they get the chance to grow.$$

$$**Hummingbirds are valid too.** Not everyone is a single-minded jackhammer. People who move between interests, cross-pollinating ideas, can lead rich creative lives. Seeing yourself as a hummingbird rather than as scattered can turn a source of guilt into a source of strength.$$

- Are you more of a jackhammer or a hummingbird, and have you been judging yourself for it?
- Which small curiosity from your past might have grown into something if you'd followed it?

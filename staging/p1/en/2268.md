[[open]]
A meteorologist retypes one number, three digits shorter than the original, and walks off to get a coffee. When he comes back the printout is nothing like the one he was trying to repeat. The machine has not broken, and he has not made a mistake that anyone could have caught.

[[story]]
Edward Lorenz was running a weather simulation on a computer in 1961. The model had 12 variables, among them temperature and wind speed. He wanted to rerun a simulation he had already done, and when he entered one of the variables he rounded it from .506127 to .506, and then left to have a coffee.

When he came back, the pattern of two months of simulated weather had changed drastically. The run had drifted away from the first one until it described a quite different sequence of weather. Everything else was identical, and only that one number had been shortened.

Silver's *The Signal and the Noise* treats weather as one of the hardest tests of prediction, and Lorenz's coffee break is the reason it is so hard. Lorenz concluded that small differences can have large consequences, a principle now called sensitive dependence on initial conditions, or the butterfly effect. He also saw a second consequence: because such a system never repeats itself exactly, long-range forecasting becomes very difficult.

In 1963 he published the idea in a paper titled "Deterministic Nonperiodic Flow." In it he wrote that two states differing by imperceptible amounts may eventually evolve into two considerably different states. The butterfly image came later, from a 1972 conference presentation on whether the flap of a butterfly's wings could affect the weather.

The model itself was fully deterministic: given the same numbers, only one thing can happen next. The surprise came from the way tiny differences were amplified, not from any randomness in the rules, and that is why two nearly identical starting points could end up so far apart.

Think about the size of the error. It was less than one part in a thousand, a difference no one measuring the real weather would consider worth worrying about. But in a system where each step takes the previous step's result as its input, a small difference does not fade away. It grows.

That explains why weather forecasting is hard. Tomorrow's forecast depends on today's measurements, and no measurement is perfect. The further ahead a forecast looks, the more that tiny imperfection multiplies, so the obstacle sits less in the quality of the model than in the way the system amplifies small errors.

The practical consequence is an honest one. The forecaster's confidence has to shrink as the horizon grows, because the starting error keeps compounding along the way.

[[lessons]]
$$**In any system that feeds on its own results, a tiny measurement error grows over time, so widen your forecast range as you stretch the horizon.** A sales projection, a project timeline or a fitness plan multiplies the small mistakes in today's assumptions a little more each month. Instead of stating month three as firmly as tomorrow, let the range get wider the further out you look. That lets you hold distant dates more loosely, in all honesty, and still trust yourself on the short term. Accepting up front that a small mistake will grow also reduces the surprise when it does.$$

Seventeen high school seniors are honored in front of their families and every younger student in their school network, all the way down to sixth grade. The occasion is not graduation. It celebrates the colleges that have accepted them, and it was planned deliberately, over a drink in a pub.

[[story]]
At YES Prep, a charter school network in Houston, one rule shaped everything: to graduate, every student had to apply to college and be accepted, even if the student finally chose not to go. Chris Barbic founded the network in 1998, and Donald Kamentz was one of the first people he hired. Later Kamentz became its first Director of College Counseling and Alumni Programs.

Chip and Dan Heath open *The Power of Moments* with a scene from the early days. Barbic and Kamentz sat in a Houston pub, recovering from another 14-hour day of running a start-up school. According to the Heaths, what came out of that evening was not a policy but a ceremony. Senior Signing Day did not just happen; the two men set out to create a defining moment for their students.

The first ceremony, in 2001, honored 17 graduating seniors. Their families came. So did every other student in the YES Prep system, from the juniors down to the sixth graders. The younger children were not there as an audience for a speech. They were there to watch older students from their own school be celebrated for the thing the school cared about most.

Think about what the design does. College acceptance is an abstract goal for a sixth grader, something adults mention. Here it became something a child could see: a room, a crowd, a named person being applauded for a result. Each sixth grader could picture their own name in that room six years later. The rule gave the school a standard, and the ceremony gave the standard a face.

It also marked a date. Without the event, the acceptance letters would have arrived one by one, in kitchens and mailboxes, and the achievement would have passed almost unnoticed. A shared moment collected them into one afternoon and told every student what the school expected of them.

According to YES Prep, the event has since grown into arena-sized gatherings, and Kamentz has noted other districts copying it. A small idea, born after a long day, is now repeated in other places.

[[lessons]]
$$**Turn the result your group cares about most into a ceremony everyone can see and look forward to.** A goal that lives only in a policy or a memo stays abstract, and people do not feel it. Give it a date, an audience and a name read aloud, and younger or newer members can picture themselves in that room one day. In your own team or family, ask which achievement quietly passes without notice today. Then give it one shared hour where it is celebrated in public, so the people who come after you can see what is valued here.$$

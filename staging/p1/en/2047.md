[[open]]
A railroad switch runs along the edge of a field of iron bars. Each bar weighs about ninety-two pounds, and seventy-five men take turns carrying them up a plank and dropping them on the end of a car. Beside the plank stands a man with a watch, and he is about to start telling one of them when to walk and when to sit.

[[story]]
Stanley McChrystal and his co-authors trace the way big institutions think back to Frederick Winslow Taylor, who developed his "Scientific Management" in Philadelphia-area steel mills. This scene comes from Taylor's own 1911 book and is set at Bethlehem Steel in Pennsylvania, around the end of the 1890s.

Taylor wrote that a gang of seventy-five laborers was loading pig iron at about 12.5 long tons per man per day. After studying the work, he decided that a first-class handler should manage between 47 and 48 tons. The yard had 80,000 tons to load, so the gap mattered, and so did the pace of everyone in the yard.

He began with selection. For three or four days he watched all seventy-five men and picked out four who looked physically able to keep that pace. He looked into the history and habits of the four and chose one: a small Pennsylvania Dutchman who, as Taylor tells it, trotted home a mile after work, earned $1.15 a day, and had bought a plot of land. Taylor called him Schmidt. Historians identify the man as Henry Noll.

Then came the offer, a raise to $1.85 a day on one condition. Schmidt had to do exactly what the man beside him said. The next day that man stood over him with a watch and said, "Now pick up a pig and walk. Now sit down and rest." By half past five Schmidt had 47.5 tons on the car, and, according to Taylor, he kept that pace for the three years Taylor stayed at Bethlehem, earning 60 per cent more than the men around him.

Every figure here comes from Taylor himself. Some later historians argue that he drew sweeping conclusions from thin experiments, so the numbers are best read as his own account of his method, not an audited record.

What the authors draw from the scene is how the system divides the labor. The manager studies, calculates and decides. The worker walks when told and rests when told. In stable conditions that division is fast and predictable, and McChrystal's team argues that the military inherited this habit of seeking one efficient, repeatable solution and applying it at scale.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask the person doing the work how they do it before you set the pace.** A shop manager who times every cashier with a stopwatch and imposes one average will never learn why the quickest cashier is quick. When the people who measure and the people who work stay on opposite sides of a stopwatch, what the worker knows stays on the shop floor and never reaches the plan or the people who wrote it. If you listen first, your measurement checks that knowledge instead of replacing it, and the two of you can change the pace whenever the work itself changes.$$

Frank Shamrock, a mixed martial arts champion, had a rule for training fighters. Every one of them needed three people. Someone better. Someone worse. Someone exactly as good. Not one great coach, not a crowd of sparring partners, but those three specific roles. Ryan Holiday thinks the reason behind that rule applies far outside a gym.

[[story]]
In *Ego Is the Enemy*, published in 2016, Ryan Holiday has a warning for anyone who has started to do well. The moment you begin to succeed is the moment you are most tempted to stop learning.

He sets up the problem with a line from Epictetus, the Stoic philosopher. It is impossible, Epictetus said, for a person to learn what they think they already know.

That sentence explains a lot. Learning needs a gap, a sense that there is something you don't have yet. The more you believe you've arrived, the smaller that gap feels, until it seems to disappear. Nothing new can get in, not because you lack ability, but because you've closed the door yourself.

Early success makes this especially tempting. You've proven something. People treat you as the one who knows. It becomes easy to stop being a student, and hard to notice that you have.

To show what the opposite looks like, Holiday turns to Frank Shamrock and the system he used for training fighters. Shamrock called it "plus, minus, equal."

Every fighter needs a plus: someone better to learn from.

Every fighter needs a minus: someone less skilled whom they teach.

And every fighter needs an equal: someone at the same level to challenge them.

On the surface this is a training schedule. Underneath, it is a way of keeping ego in check from three directions at once.

The plus humbles you. As long as someone in the room is clearly better, it is hard to believe you have nothing left to learn.

The minus forces you to understand what you know. Teaching a less experienced fighter means explaining things you usually do without thinking. If you can't explain it, you find out where your understanding is thinner than you assumed.

The equal keeps you honest. Against someone at your level, you can't coast on reputation. Every weakness gets tested.

##Each of the three roles blocks a different way that ego sneaks in.##

This is Holiday's larger argument. The people who keep improving are the ones who treat themselves as learners no matter how far they get. They don't wait until they feel humble to seek out a plus, a minus and an equal. They build those three relationships into how they work, so the student in them never gets the chance to retire.

[[lessons]]
$$**Thinking you know closes the door.** Epictetus put it bluntly: you cannot learn what you think you already know. The risk isn't ignorance. It's certainty. Every time you feel sure you've mastered something, it is worth asking whether that feeling is information or just a sign that you've stopped looking for what you're missing.$$

$$**Someone above, someone beside.** In Shamrock's system, the plus shows you how far there still is to go, and the equal makes sure you can't hide from that. Together they keep you from settling into a comfortable story about your own level. Being the best person in every room you enter is pleasant, and it quietly stops your growth.$$

$$**Teaching reveals the gaps.** The minus isn't just a favor to a beginner. Explaining your skill to someone less experienced forces you to turn habit into words. The spots where you stumble are the spots where your own understanding is thin. The person you teach ends up teaching you where to look next.$$

[[reflect]]
- Who in your life right now is clearly better than you at something you care about?
- When did explaining something to a beginner show you that you understood it less than you thought?
- Is there anyone at your level who regularly challenges your work, or have you drifted away from them?

Each week in London, parish clerks tally the dead on a printed sheet. Older women paid to inspect the bodies report what each person died of. Most readers glance at the numbers and move on. One shopkeeper keeps the sheets and starts counting what nobody else has counted.

[[story]]
John Graunt sold small wares in London, a haberdasher by trade and a tradesman rather than a scholar. Peter Bernstein tells of him in *Against the Gods* as the man who first treated a city's records of death as data. The records were the weekly Bills of Mortality, kept by parish clerks, listing christenings, burials and the causes the paid searchers gave. Most Londoners saw in them only a count of the dead.

Graunt read them as a pattern. His book, *Natural and Political Observations Made upon the Bills of Mortality*, appeared in 1662, and what he drew from the sheets was new. For every fourteen boys christened, he found, there were thirteen girls. More boys were born than girls, and the proportion held across the years he studied. Because more males also died, the population ended up close to evenly split between men and women. His figures covered only London, and only the years the lists covered.

He also summed up the causes of death over a twenty-year stretch in a table of its own, according to published accounts of the book, and then built a table of survival. By his reckoning about 36 of every hundred children born died before the age of six. It is usually described as the first life table, and it rested on nothing but the parish lists and his own judgment of how far to trust them. He did not trust them blindly. He doubted that the bills recorded plague deaths as cleanly as they claimed, and suspected that some were filed under other causes.

That doubt matters. Graunt's conclusions were only as good as the sheets, and he said so. A man who knew exactly how rough his source was could still draw a conclusion from it, and he could say how far the conclusion could be pushed.

The book reached the Royal Society in early February 1662, when copies were distributed at a meeting. Within weeks the haberdasher was proposed as a fellow, and he was admitted on 26 February. According to the account of his life in the national biography, Charles II urged the society to admit more such tradesmen. A shopkeeper's patience with columns of figures had earned him a seat among the learned men. Later editions followed in 1665 and 1676.

[[lessons]]
$$**Count what everyone else only files away.** A small repair shop writes down every complaint on a slip and drops the slips in a drawer. If its owner sorts the slips once a month, the same few faults turn up again and again, and that tells her which one to fix first. You do not need a degree or a large data set to do this, only the habit of keeping the record and the patience to read it as a pattern, while remembering how rough the record is. Doing so lets you say what is likely, and how sure you are, instead of arguing from the one case you happen to remember.$$

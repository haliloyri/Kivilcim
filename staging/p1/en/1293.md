Earn, get taxed, spend. Earn, spend, get taxed. Read those two lines again. The same three steps, the same income, and only one thing has moved: where the tax sits in the line. Robert Kiyosaki thinks that small shift explains a large part of why some people keep more of what they make.

[[story]]
In *Rich Dad Poor Dad* (1997), Robert Kiyosaki gives a whole chapter to taxes and corporations, and at its heart is a comparison of two sequences.

The first belongs to an employee. You earn your pay. Tax is taken. Then you spend whatever is left. Most people with a salary will recognize this order without needing it explained; it is simply how a paycheck works.

The second belongs, as Kiyosaki describes it, to a business owner using a corporation. The business earns. It spends on its expenses. Then it is taxed on what remains. The steps are the same. The order is not.

That is the whole mechanism he wants you to see. ##Two people with the same income can end up with different amounts left, just because of the order in which money flows.## One is taxed on everything before spending a cent. The other spends on legitimate business costs first, and the tax applies to a smaller figure.

Kiyosaki wraps this in a short history. Income tax, he says, was sold to the public as a tax on the rich. In his account, it came to fall most heavily on the middle class and on salaried workers instead. You do not have to accept every step of that history to see the argument he is building.

His recurring claim is that the rich keep more of their money through legal structures and financial knowledge, not only through harder work. He has a name for that knowledge: "financial IQ." And he makes it concrete. It means understanding accounting, investing, markets and the law. Not a mood or a mindset, but four fields you can actually study.

This leads to his complaint about school. Schools, he argues, teach many subjects but not how money works. So people pick it up by accident, late, or not at all. Many first learn that the two sequences exist when a paycheck arrives smaller than they expected.

It matters to be clear about the limits here. This is Kiyosaki's simplified interpretation, written with US rules of the 1990s in mind. Tax professionals have criticized it, and it does not apply the same way in every country or to every expense. Which costs can legitimately be deducted is set by local tax law, not by a book. What the book does offer is the question: do you know which sequence your money follows?

[[lessons]]
$$**The route matters, not just the amount.** Kiyosaki's two sequences put the same money through the same three steps in a different order. His point is that the structure your income passes through can shape what's left as much as the size of the income. Whether any of that applies to you depends on your local rules.$$

$$**Financial knowledge is a list, not a vibe.** "Financial IQ" sounds vague until he spells it out: accounting, investing, markets and law. That turns a fuzzy goal like "getting better with money" into four areas where you can find out, quite specifically, what you don't know.$$

$$**What nobody teaches you, you learn late.** His complaint is that school skips how money works entirely. The practical upshot is that the gap won't close on its own. Someone has to go looking, and that someone is usually you.$$

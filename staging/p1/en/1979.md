A classroom has a coffee mug on every other desk. The students have turned the mugs over in their hands, checked the crest, and been told that any of them can go home with one. A market opens for the mugs, with money on the table, and almost nothing changes hands.

[[story]]
Eleven trades. That was the number economists would predict for the mug market that Richard Thaler, Daniel Kahneman and Jack Knetsch set up at Cornell University, an experiment *Misbehaving* recounts. The reason it matters is how far the real count fell short.

The experiment began with a warm-up using tokens. Forty-four participants took part, and half were handed a token that came with an assigned cash value. Everyone could see how a trade would pay. The market ran three times, and each time price and volume came out exactly as the textbook says. In his Nobel lecture Thaler put it this way: "Economics 101 was true!"

Then real goods replaced tokens. Twenty-two mugs stamped with the Cornell insignia went to half the group by lottery, and everyone, owner or not, was told to inspect a mug and decide whether to take one home. The same market opened four times, with one round picked at random to count. Since the mugs were handed out by chance, roughly half should have landed with people who valued them less than someone empty-handed, and about eleven should have changed hands.

The four rounds produced 4 trades, then 1, then 2, then 2. The median mug owner would not let go of it for less than a price somewhere between $4.25 and $4.75. Across the room, the median person without a mug would pay between $2.25 and $2.75. Two groups sat across a gap and neither would cross it.

The team repeated the exercise with pens. Those who had missed out on a mug got a pen instead, and the market ran as before. The result was similar, with four or five trades instead of eleven and owners asking about twice what buyers would pay. Thaler adds that the experiment also showed neither income effects nor transaction costs were significant in this market, so neither could account for the stalled trades.

Thaler's explanation is loss aversion. Selling the mug counts as giving something up, and giving up hurts more than getting feels good, so owners ask for more than non-owners will offer. He drew two conclusions from the study. Token markets are special, because in real life nobody tells you what a thing is worth to you, which leaves you free to misbehave. And the endowment effect survives markets and a chance to learn.

[[lessons]]
$$**Price what you own as if you had to buy it today.** The moment something is yours, you tend to value it above what you would pay for the identical thing in a shop, even if you never use it. Someone clearing a garage may hold on to a pile of old equipment, then ask what they would pay for it at a market stall and find the answer is nothing. Asking before you sell, keep or cling gives you a second number to set beside the first, and the gap shows how much of your price is plain ownership and how much is real value.$$

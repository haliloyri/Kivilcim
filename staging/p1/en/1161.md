A fire crew is inside a burning house, doing everything by the book. Then their lieutenant, for no reason he can name, shouts at them to get out. They obey. Moments later, the floor they were standing on collapses. Afterwards, he thinks he might have ESP.

[[story]]
Malcolm Gladwell tells this story in *Blink*. It comes from the work of Gary Klein, a psychologist who spent years interviewing firefighters, nurses and soldiers about how they make split-second decisions.

The call seemed routine. A fire in a one-story house in an ordinary neighborhood, apparently in the kitchen at the back. The lieutenant led his crew in through the front, toward the kitchen, and they began spraying water on the flames.

But the fire didn't behave the way it should. It didn't die down. They sprayed again, and still it barely responded.

The lieutenant stood in the living room and felt something change inside him. He didn't have a clear thought. He just knew something was wrong.

> Let's get out, now!

His men left the building. Almost immediately, the floor where they had been standing gave way. The fire hadn't been in the kitchen at all. It had been burning in the basement, directly underneath them.

##If they had stayed a few moments longer, they would have fallen into it.##

When Klein asked him how he knew, the lieutenant couldn't say. He thought perhaps it had been a sixth sense.

Klein kept asking questions, and slowly the real explanation came out. The living room had been much hotter than a small kitchen fire should have made it. And the fire was strangely quiet. Fires are usually loud; this one wasn't, because the floor was muffling it. And of course, it hadn't responded to water. None of these clues had risen to conscious thought. But together, they didn't match anything in his long experience of kitchen fires. His mind registered that mismatch before he could explain it, and turned it into a feeling: *get out.*

Gladwell calls this ability **thin-slicing**: the mind's capacity to find meaningful patterns in very narrow slices of experience. It isn't magic. It's years of experience, compressed so deeply that it works faster than words.

But Gladwell adds an important caution throughout *Blink*. This kind of intuition is powerful only where it has been earned. The lieutenant had fought many fires. His gut was trained. A beginner who "just has a feeling" may simply be guessing, or following a bias.

[[lessons]]
$$**Intuition is experience in fast-forward.** The lieutenant's "sixth sense" turned out to be years of firefighting, working faster than conscious thought. He'd seen enough kitchen fires to know what one should feel and sound like. That's why expert hunches are often worth taking seriously even when the expert can't explain them yet.$$

$$**Pay attention to a mismatch.** Too hot, too quiet, not responding to water: none of those alone meant disaster. Together, they didn't fit the pattern. When something in your own field feels "off," it's often because a detail doesn't match your experience. That feeling is worth pausing on.$$

$$**Only trust the gut you've trained.** Gladwell is clear that thin-slicing works where you have deep experience. Outside that zone, a strong feeling may just be a guess or a bias. Knowing which of your instincts are earned is as important as having them.$$

- In which area of your life do you have enough experience for your gut feeling to be trustworthy?
- Recall a time you felt "something's off" before you could explain it. Was it right?
- Where might you be trusting a feeling that hasn't been earned yet?

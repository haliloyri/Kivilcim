In a Redwood City gym, a team of twelve-year-old girls misses another layup. Their coach watches from the sideline. He is an immigrant software entrepreneur who has never played basketball and has no idea what a good practice should look like. Next to him, the clock runs on.

[[story]]
Vivek Ranadivé was the one who had to coach. His daughter's team in Redwood City, California, needed an adult, and he was the adult who volunteered. He had grown up in India, where basketball was not part of his life, so he had no habits to unlearn and no instinct for how the game is "supposed" to be played. What he could see was that the girls lacked talent. They could not shoot well, and passing the ball down the court was a risk every time.

Gladwell tells what Ranadivé did with that fact in *David and Goliath*, and in his earlier New Yorker article on the same team. Most youth teams, losing or winning, play the usual way: the defence waits near its own basket while the other team walks the ball up the court. Ranadivé decided his players would not wait. They would guard their opponents across the whole court, from the first second to the last, and chase the ball the moment it was thrown in. That tactic is called the full-court press.

The press demands effort in place of skill, and effort is the one thing any twelve-year-old can give. A team that cannot shoot can still run at a girl who is trying to dribble, and the girl dribbling may be no better at it than they are. Opponents, used to a calm walk up the floor, started to lose the ball before they reached half-court.

It was also tiring, and most coaches do not try it for that reason. Ranadivé's team did it in every game, whoever the opponent was. Over the season it moved through its local league and reached the national championship tournament for the age group. It did not win the title, yet a group that began with little basketball talent had made it onto that national stage.

The detail that matters is how the choice was made. The weaker side skipped the copy-the-leader game and looked for the one rule everyone had agreed to follow without ever saying it, and broke it. Doing that takes a coach who does not know how things are normally done, and a team willing to be tired.

[[lessons]]
$$**When you cannot match a stronger rival on skill, find the unwritten rule of the game and refuse to follow it.** Every field has a polite assumption nobody questions, such as how a pitch is made, how a meeting runs or how a negotiation opens. The unconventional move usually costs effort rather than talent, which is why people with less to start with are the ones able to afford it. Look for the move your rival avoids only because it is tiring, and ask whether you could make it your habit. It will be uncomfortable, and that discomfort is simply the price.$$

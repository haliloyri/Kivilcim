[[open]]
A page in a logbook has a dead moth stuck to it with tape. Under it, a line of handwriting dryly records the insect as the first actual case of bug being found. The handwriting is joking, and the joke only works because everyone in the room already knew what a bug was.

[[story]]
The logbook page belongs to the Harvard Mark II, an electromechanical computer built from relays, small switches whose metal contacts open and close with a click, and it dates from September 1947. Operators had been hunting for the reason the machine was misbehaving. In a relay they found a moth, trapped and dead. Someone removed it, taped it into the log and wrote the line that made the page famous: "First actual case of bug being found." The logbook is now held by the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History.

Grace Hopper worked on the Mark II team, and Walter Isaacson's *The Innovators* gives her a place among the pioneers of programming. The story of the moth is bound up with her name, but an earlier Smithsonian record said the logbook was probably not hers. Accounts name the operators who found it, among them William Burke, and say Hopper was not credited with the discovery. What she did was retell the story so often that the whole profession knew it, and she went on to become a rear admiral in the US Navy, which gave her tellings an audience.

The word itself was older than the moth. Thomas Edison had used "bugs" in an 1878 letter for the small faults and difficulties that show up between an idea and a working machine, and engineers had said it for decades. That is why the note says "actual": the team was making a small joke about a figure of speech that had finally come true.

Notice what the page does as a document. It does not blame anyone, and it does not hide the cause. A time, a place in the machine and the cause are written down, and the cause itself is attached to the paper, flat under a piece of tape. Anyone who opened that log months later, with no memory of the afternoon, could see what had gone wrong and what had been done about it.

The moth did more than end one afternoon's debugging. It kept a word in circulation that programmers use daily, and it left a specimen anyone could look at. Few records of failure survive so literally.

[[lessons]]
$$**Keep a plain log of what broke, with the evidence attached.** The Mark II team did not just fix the relay; they wrote the cause into the book and taped the proof beside it, so the problem could be checked later by someone who was not there. A repair shop that writes down each fault with the part that failed can see which supplier keeps sending bad ones. Your own version might be a short note in a shared file with the symptom, the cause and a screenshot, which turns an embarrassing afternoon into something the next person can learn from.$$

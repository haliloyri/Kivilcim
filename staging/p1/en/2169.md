[[story]]
Edward O. Thorp, author of *A Man for All Markets*, also summarized his move from blackjack to Wall Street in a 2003 article, and this account follows it. In 1965 and 1966 he worked with the economist Sheen Kassouf on models for pricing stock purchase warrants. A warrant gives its holder the right to buy a share at a fixed price, and what that right is worth is hard to say.

The two took a further step. They treated a convertible bond, which pays interest like an ordinary bond but can be exchanged for a set number of shares, as two parts: an ordinary bond with all terms identical, plus the conversion privilege. They called the implied market value of the ordinary bond the "investment value of the convertible," so whatever else the buyer paid for was the conversion right. They counted that privilege as "latent" warrants and calculated its theoretical value. To compare different bonds with their stocks, they standardized their diagrams and put them on one chart. Thorp calls this a crude first pass at finding underpriced issues.

Then, in his own words, he guessed the Black–Scholes formula in late 1967 and traded warrants with it from late 1967 on. The formula would be published under Black's and Scholes's names in 1973, years after Thorp had begun using his version. Thorp's version came from a guess, and he started testing it with money.

Once he began valuing the latent warrants with the model, his progress on convertibles quickened. The stock price, volatility and the riskless rate of return now went into the calculation. One problem remained: estimating how volatile the stock would be over the life of the warrant or option. Another was the bond's "investment value," because as the stock fell, the bond's credit rating tended to fall too. They began with ad hoc corrections and later built a more complete analytic model.

In 1969 Thorp and Jay Regan started Princeton Newport Partners, which Thorp calls the first market-neutral derivatives hedge fund. Convertible hedging was a core profit center. By the time the firm closed at the end of 1988, it had made some $250 million for its partners, half or more of it from convertibles.

[[lessons]]
$$**Start using a rough model early and keep sharpening the input you know least.** A shopkeeper who sells seasonal goods can set prices with a simple rule, even a crude one, and watch what sells. As the rule proves useful, she can then gather more data to estimate its most uncertain ingredient, such as how many customers will come next week. Waiting for a flawless formula costs you more than knowing where yours is weak and tightening that spot step by step, because every month of use also shows you where the rough rule fails. That record tells you which number deserves your next hour of effort.$$

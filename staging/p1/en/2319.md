Hundreds of personnel files lie on desks in a government office, each belonging to a military test pilot. All of the men are skilled, and the country has no use for hundreds of astronauts. By the end of the process, the pile must become seven names.

[[story]]
In the weeks after NASA announced Project Mercury, one question mattered more than the hardware: who would sit in the capsule? Tom Wolfe's *The Right Stuff* follows the seven men who were chosen, and their selection is part of its story. Let us look at how the choice was made, because the method is as interesting as the result.

NASA did not advertise for volunteers. It went to the military and looked only at test pilots. In January 1959 a small group at the agency went through the records of 508 of them. From those files they picked 110 candidates. That is the first, cheapest filter: paper only, no man had yet been asked to climb into anything, and nearly four of every five files were put aside.

The next stages used interviews and written tests, and cut the group to a few dozen. According to NASA's own history, the harshest part came at the Wright Aeromedical Laboratory in Dayton, Ohio. There the remaining candidates went through medical and physical examinations designed to find the limit of what a body can stand. The stages grew more expensive as the pool grew smaller, which is exactly how a sensible filter should work.

On 2 April 1959 NASA picked seven men: Scott Carpenter, Gordon Cooper, John Glenn, Gus Grissom, Wally Schirra, Alan Shepard and Deke Slayton. A week later, on 9 April, they were introduced to the press. Seven out of 508 is a little over one percent, and every one of them had already been a selected pilot before the process began.

There is a lesson in the order of the stages. Reading 508 files is cheap, and a laboratory test of a single candidate is costly. If NASA had tested everyone in the laboratory it would have wasted months, and if it had chosen from paper alone it would have trusted the part of a person that paper cannot show. The design put each kind of evidence where it was cheapest and most reliable.

It is also worth seeing what the process did not try to do. It did not look for the perfect man in the abstract. It started from a group already narrowed by the profession, and then asked a single question at every stage: who is still standing?

[[lessons]]
$$**Put the cheap checks first and the costly ones last, so every stage removes the people or ideas that do not fit.** Reading files cost NASA almost nothing, while a medical laboratory cost a great deal, so the files came first. The same order works in hiring, in choosing a supplier, or in picking which idea to build. Start with a quick screen on facts you can read, run an interview for those who pass, and keep the hard trial for the last few. That way the expensive stage is spent only on strong candidates, and no one is judged by a single tool.$$

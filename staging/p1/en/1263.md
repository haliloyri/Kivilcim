John Boyd had a warning for the most promising young officers he worked with. Soon, he told them, you'll come to a fork in the road. You'll have to pick a direction, and you won't get to take both. Boyd knew what each path cost, because he had already chosen one himself.

[[story]]
Ryan Holiday builds a chapter of *Ego Is the Enemy* around John Boyd, and it helps to know who Boyd was.

He was a U.S. Air Force fighter pilot and strategist. His ideas about aerial combat and aircraft design went on to influence a generation of American fighter planes. In other words, his thinking ended up in the machines an air force actually flew.

Boyd also mentored younger officers, and Holiday describes the talk he gave the ones who showed real promise. At some point, Boyd told them, they would reach a fork in the road.

Down one path was the choice *to be somebody*. That meant compromising, going along with the system, and in return collecting the promotions and the good assignments. It was the comfortable road, and plenty of capable people took it.

Down the other path was the choice *to do something*. That meant working for what actually mattered, even when it cost you promotions and even when it made your superiors unhappy.

Boyd was not describing a hypothetical. He had made the choice himself, and he had chosen to do.

##His ideas had a large impact, but he made enemies in the establishment and never rose beyond the rank of colonel.##

That is the part that gives the story its weight. Boyd did not get to have it both ways. The path that let his ideas matter also closed the doors to the higher ranks and recognition that many of his peers went after. His career looks modest on paper and enormous in its effects.

Holiday uses Boyd to draw a line through the middle of ambition. Ego, he argues, pulls us toward recognition and status: the title, the rank, being seen as important. Purpose asks a different question: what do you actually want to accomplish?

Those two pulls often point in different directions. The path to being somebody rewards fitting in. The path to doing something often requires refusing to. It is easy never to make the choice consciously, to drift toward status because that is what the system rewards, and only to notice later that the work they cared about fell away along the way.

Boyd's talk was an attempt to make that choice visible before the fork arrived, so the young officers could pick a direction on purpose and accept the price of it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Status and work pull apart.** Boyd's fork makes a simple point about ambition: aiming at status and aiming at the work are not the same thing, and they often lead different ways. You can compromise your way to a better title, or push for what matters and risk the title. Seeing the two as separate goals is the first step to choosing between them.$$

$$**Doing has a price.** Boyd's influence was real, and so were his costs: enemies in the establishment and a career that stopped at colonel. Choosing the work is not a free upgrade. It helps to name the cost in advance, so it doesn't feel like a betrayal when it arrives, and so you can decide whether you are willing to pay it.$$

$$**Knowing what you want to do sharpens your judgment.** Holiday contrasts recognition with purpose. Once you are clear about what you want to accomplish, the small compromises made for status become easier to spot. Without that clarity, each one seems reasonable on its own, and together they can steer you onto the other road.$$

- Have you ever traded the work you cared about for approval or a title?
- Which of your recent decisions were really about how things would look?

Sarah learned to bake pies as a child, in her aunt's kitchen. She loved it, she was good at it, and one day she did what so many people with a gift like that are told to do: she opened her own pie shop. The shop was supposed to be about pies. It turned out to ask something of her that baking never had.

[[story]]
Michael E. Gerber tells Sarah's story in *The E-Myth Revisited*, the 1995 expanded edition of his book on small business.

Sarah's reason for opening the shop was simple. She loved baking pies, a craft she had carried with her since those early days in her aunt's kitchen. A shop of her own seemed like the natural next step for someone with that skill.

Then she started running it. And she found herself buried in work that was not baking. She was working long hours. Over time, she came to resent the very business she had started in order to do what she loved.

Gerber uses Sarah to name what went wrong. The trigger for starting a business like hers, he says, is often an "Entrepreneurial Seizure." A technician, someone who does the hands-on work, decides to stop working for somebody else and go into business for themselves.

Behind that decision sits what he calls the "Fatal Assumption." It goes like this: if you understand the technical work of a business, you understand a business that does that technical work.

##Knowing how to bake a pie is not the same as knowing how to run a pie shop.##

That assumption is what caught Sarah. Her skill was real. But the shop needed things her skill did not cover, and those things took over her days.

Gerber offers a way to see why. Everyone who goes into business, he says, is really three people in one. There is the Entrepreneur, the visionary who imagines what the business could become. There is the Manager, the organizer who brings order and plans. And there is the Technician, the doer who actually does the work.

Gerber describes the typical small business owner as roughly 10 percent Entrepreneur, 20 percent Manager, and 70 percent Technician. The doer dominates. The owner keeps doing the work, and the parts of the business nobody is designing pile up around them.

Sarah's pies were never the problem. The problem was that the business around them needed an Entrepreneur and a Manager, and she had opened the shop as a Technician.

[[lessons]]
$$**Doing the work is not running the work.** Gerber's Fatal Assumption is that technical skill equals business skill. Sarah's baking was excellent, and it was the reason she opened the shop. But the shop needed a different kind of competence, and her talent at the oven did not come with it.$$

$$**Three roles compete, and the doer usually wins.** Entrepreneur, Manager, Technician: every owner carries all three. With a typical profile of roughly 10, 20, and 70 percent, the hands-on work fills the day by default. Planning and direction do not happen unless someone deliberately makes room for them.$$

$$**A passion can get crowded out by its own business.** Sarah opened the shop to bake pies and ended up resenting it. Turning something you love into a business does not automatically protect the part you love. Unless the business is designed around it, the rest of the work can swallow it.$$

- In your current work, how much of your week goes to doing the work, versus organizing it, versus deciding where it should go?
- Which of Gerber's three roles do you slip into when you are tired or under pressure?

There's a sentence many people have said to themselves on a bad night: *I can't live with myself anymore.* One man said it, and then he heard it. The sentence stopped him cold, because it seemed to be talking about two different people.

[[story]]
Eckhart Tolle opens *The Power of Now* with his own story. Until he was about thirty, he writes, he lived in an almost continuous state of anxiety, broken up by stretches of deep, suicidal depression. The book doesn't dress this up. It reads like a description of someone who had run out of ways to feel better.

One night, not long after his twenty-ninth birthday, he woke in the early hours with a feeling of absolute dread. Everything around him felt alien and hostile. And one thought kept repeating in his mind:

> I cannot live with myself any longer.

Then something odd happened. Instead of sinking further into the thought, he became aware of how strange it was. *If I cannot live with myself,* he asked, *there must be two of me. The "I" and the "self" that "I" cannot live with.* And then a second question followed: *Maybe only one of them is real.*

##He was so taken aback by this realization that his mind simply stopped.##

He describes what came next as being drawn into something like a vortex of energy, and then losing consciousness. When he woke, birds were singing outside the window, and he felt a peace he had never known. The dread was gone. Nothing about his circumstances had changed, but the voice that had been tormenting him no longer had the same grip.

For a while afterwards, Tolle writes, he had no job, no relationship and no fixed plans. He spent a great deal of time sitting on park benches, in a state of intense joy. To anyone walking past, he might have looked like a man with nothing going on. Inside, something enormous had shifted.

The rest of the book grows out of that night. Tolle's main practice starts with the voice in your head. Most of us never notice it, because we assume the voice *is* us. It comments, judges, rehearses, worries and argues. His advice is to start listening to it as often as you can, the way you'd listen to someone talking in the next room. He calls this "watching the thinker."

The moment you do that, you notice something. There is the voice, and there is someone aware of the voice. That second one, the watcher, is the part of you the thoughts can't touch.

[[lessons]]
$$**Hearing a thought gives you room.** Tolle had said "I cannot live with myself" many times in his head. The night he actually *heard* it, and noticed the two people in the sentence, the spiral broke. You don't have to win an argument with a painful thought. Noticing that you're thinking it is often enough to loosen its hold.$$

$$**The voice isn't the whole of you.** If there's a voice in your head and someone listening to it, you can't be only the voice. This is the core of "watching the thinker." The worries, judgments and replays are real, but they happen to you. They aren't the final word on who you are.$$

$$**Peace doesn't always wait for problems to be solved.** After that night, Tolle's life looked emptier than before: no job, no plans, hours on park benches. He felt more at peace than ever. What changed wasn't his situation. It was his relationship to the voice narrating it.$$

[[reflect]]
- Which sentence does the voice in your head repeat most often? Write it down word for word.
- Read it back as if someone else had said it. Does it sound fair?
- Where in your day could you spend one minute simply listening to your thoughts, without doing anything about them?

[[open]]
A customer of an American car insurer opens a form that asks for the odometer reading of each of up to four cars. On the page sits a single sentence: "I promise that the information I am providing is true." Whether that line comes before the numbers or after them has been decided by chance.

[[story]]
Dan Ariely wrote *The Honest Truth About Dishonesty* (2012), a book about why ordinary people cheat a little. A field experiment from the same year, published in a scientific journal with his name on it, became one of the best-known examples in the field. Its subjects were 13,488 customers of an auto insurance company in the southeastern United States, randomly assigned to sign that promise either at the top of the form or at the bottom.

The paper reported that people who signed at the top gave odometer readings 2,400 miles higher, 10.3 percent more, than people who signed at the bottom. Higher readings were taken as more honest ones. The result was quoted in talks, articles and policy discussions for years.

In 2020 the five original authors, together with two other researchers, published six new studies in the same journal. None of them reproduced the original lab results. The field experiment was not rerun, but its data went onto a public site, and there the authors noticed something odd: before the forms were even filled in, the two groups already differed by about 15,000 miles. They guessed that the randomization had failed.

Others read the same file more closely. In August 2021 the blog Data Colada published what anonymous researchers had found. The miles reported for the first car, statistically, looked like numbers drawn evenly between 0 and 50,000. In the 40,000 to 45,000 band there were 1,313 customers, and 1,339 in the next band up. Above 50,000 there was nobody at all, and the highest reading anywhere was 49,997. Real drivers do not stop at a round number.

The font told the rest. Half the rows were in Calibri and half in Cambria, 6,744 of each for the first car. For a group of customers, every Calibri row had a Cambria twin, with mileage higher by less than 1,000 on each car. Data Colada's reading was that someone had copied the real rows and added random noise to them.

The authors wrote that Ariely was the only one of them in contact with the insurer. Data Colada listed three possibilities for who made the data: Ariely, someone in his lab, or someone at the insurer. The journal retracted the paper on 13 September 2021, citing the questions Data Colada had raised about its data. The 2,400 miles no longer stand as a finding.

[[lessons]]
$$**Treat a striking result as a claim until someone else has run it.** A finding that fits the story everyone wants to tell travels fast, and by the time it lands in a slide deck nobody asks where the numbers came from. Say a supplier sends you a neat chart showing that its training course lifted sales: before it shapes your budget, ask for the raw rows and who collected them, or whether another team got the same result. Often that costs one email. If the answer is a shrug, you have learned something about the chart without reading a single number on it.$$

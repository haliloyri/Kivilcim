A Salt Lake City television station gets hold of a leaked letter. It shows that the committee bringing the Winter Olympics to Utah paid more than ten thousand dollars for the daughter of an Olympic committee member. The people who vote on host cities were not supposed to be receiving anything of the kind.

[[story]]
In the Dictator's Handbook, Bruce Bueno de Mesquita and Alastair Smith treat the International Olympic Committee as a small electorate that picks hosts, and so as something that can be courted. The Salt Lake case is a real one, and its details come from the reporting and inquiries that followed.

Salt Lake City had lost its bid for the 1998 Games to Nagano. It wanted the next one badly, and it knew the voters were a short list of named people. Its bid committee, SLOC, paid money that ended up with the families of the people who would vote. A published accounting of the scandal says about $400,000 in scholarships went to 13 people from 1991, and at least six of them were close relatives of IOC members. One student's studies at American University cost $108,350 in all. In one case a member's son had his tuition paid and received $700 a month.

The station's report on 24 November 1998, about a letter dated 1996, was the start. The scholarship in question was a payment of more than $10,000 to a member's daughter. On the same day Marc Hodler, a senior IOC official, used the word bribery for abuses he had seen. In December he said that between 5 and 7 per cent of IOC members had taken or asked for bribes. In January 1999 the committee's chief executive, Frank Joklik, and a senior vice president, Dave Johnson, resigned, and six IOC members were suspended.

By the end, ten IOC members had lost their seats, either expelled or resigning, and another ten were reprimanded. The IOC added an age limit of 70, eight-year terms for most members, and a ban on members visiting bidding cities. It also adopted an ethics code and an ethics commission. The Games went ahead in 2002, held in the same city whose bid had caused the trouble. Juan Antonio Samaranch stayed on as IOC president and testified before the US House in December 1999.

The reforms tell you where the IOC saw the problem. Members had been visiting the cities that wanted their votes, and ending those visits was one of the first fixes. A voter who never meets the bidder has fewer occasions to be offered anything, and the few who still decide are kept a little further from the people who want something from them. The Salt Lake bid had spent years learning exactly who those voters were, and what their families needed.

[[lessons]]
$$**Keep the people who decide away from the people who want something.** When a landlord picks among twenty applicants and one of them sends a gift basket, the landlord is no longer choosing only on the application. The fewer people who decide and the more each applicant needs their yes, the more a small favour is worth. If you are the one deciding, put the criteria in writing before you meet anyone and ask to see all offers at once, so that no single offer can earn a private favour, and so that you can later show others exactly how you chose.$$

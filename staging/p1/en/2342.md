When a university offered Hope Jahren a job, she did not start with her own salary. She asked first whether there was a funded position for the man who ran her lab with her. For her, a job without that line in the budget was no job at all, and she had learned why the hard way.

[[story]]
In an interview with *Time* around the publication of her memoir *Lab Girl*, Jahren called Bill Hagopian her "lab partner and best friend." She had hired him at each of the three labs she built in three states. By her account, his funding was non-negotiable, and it was the first thing she looked at when a move was on the table.

She also told *Time* how she came to hold that rule. At Johns Hopkins, she said, her lab was lost when the money ran out while she was pregnant. A grant-funded lab stands on soft money, and when the grants stop, the people on the payroll go first.

The next offer came from the University of Hawaii at Manoa. It was a stable position, and it came with funding for Bill. She put her condition in one sentence: "If there's not a job for Bill there's no job for me." That sentence is a negotiating position, but it also says how she thought about the work, as something two people did together, a job with more than one name on it.

As she tells it, the pattern repeats across her career: three labs, three states and the same person hired in each. A new lab means starting a research program again in a new place, and Bill was the one constant in that restart. Her condition protected him before anything else was settled. A technician's salary is a separate line in a budget, and making it the first item in the negotiation keeps that line from becoming a detail that gets trimmed later.

Hawaii is where she built the Isotope Geobiology Laboratories, according to the publisher's author biography. By the time of the *Time* interview she was a tenured professor there, with a lab at Manoa. The same interview adds a warning: she said the lab's funding was due to run out in August. Even with tenure and a stable offer, the money for the lab itself stayed a yearly question.

What stands out in her account is the order of her priorities. She did not describe a move as a step up in title or prestige. She described it as a test with a single question, whether the person who made the experiments work would be paid. A lab, in this telling, is a small organism with two or three essential parts, and a scientist who lets one of them starve may keep her own salary but loses the thing that made it worth earning.

[[lessons]]
$$**Name the one person your work cannot do without, and put them in the terms of the deal.** Most of us negotiate for our own title, our own pay or our own schedule, and treat the colleague who makes the results possible as a detail to sort out later. A bakery owner who takes a bigger shop but leaves her best oven operator on a short contract has quietly bet the whole business on that person's patience. When you state that person's place before you sign anything, you find out early whether the offer is for the work you actually do.$$

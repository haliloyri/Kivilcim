A tall stone memorial, a crowd stretching back to a long pool of water, and a man at a lectern reading from prepared pages. Near the end he is reading a conclusion. Then he lifts his eyes from the paper and starts speaking in a different way.

[[story]]
On 28 August 1963 Martin Luther King Jr. stood at the Lincoln Memorial at the March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom. The day's logistics were so demanding that, according to his adviser Clarence Jones, the speech was not a priority, and on the evening before the march King still did not know what he was going to say.

He began by reading prepared remarks. Near the end, something changed. The gospel singer Mahalia Jackson, seated behind him, called out, "Tell 'em about the dream, Martin!" King then stopped reading his text and began to improvise, with the refrain "I have a dream" tying his points together. That is the passage the world remembers.

Historians are careful here. Taylor Branch writes that King later said he simply grasped at the first run of oratory that came to him, and that he did not know whether Jackson's words reached him at all. The speech also exists in several versions written at different times, with no single definitive draft. So the exact moment is partly legend. What is firmly documented is the improvisation and the refrain.

The refrain was not new that day. King had made the dream the centerpiece of several national speeches in 1961. A speech in Rocky Mount, North Carolina on 27 November 1962 contained the "I have a dream" refrain, and in June 1963 he used it again in Detroit, at Cobo Hall, after the Great Walk to Freedom. Some accounts add that in September 1962 he drew on portions of a speech by the SNCC activist Prathia Hall, which repeated the phrase.

This changes how the famous moment looks. The moment did not come out of nowhere: the passage was one he had already tried out in smaller rooms, and he could reach for it when the crowd, the day and the voice behind him asked for it. The prepared text gave him the structure, and the rehearsed passage gave him somewhere to go when the structure was not enough. Improvising well rests on material that has already survived an audience. Seen this way, Jackson's call, whether or not he heard it, matters less than the fact that he had the material ready to answer it. A speaker with nothing prepared beyond the page has no place to go when the page fails.

[[lessons]]
$$**Keep one passage you have already tried in smaller rooms, so you can set the script aside when the moment asks for it.** People imagine that great improvisation is invention on the spot, but most of it is a prepared piece used at the right second. Before a big talk or a hard conversation, find the single idea you have said aloud before, to a colleague or a friend, and notice what reaction it got. That passage becomes your second route if the first one is not working, and it lets you speak to the people in front of you instead of the page.$$

[[open]]
A mathematician convalescing from an illness lays out cards for a game of solitaire. He wonders how often the game comes out, and he starts to work out the odds on paper. The sums take a great deal of time. Then he stops calculating and looks at the cards again.

[[story]]
Stanislaw Ulam was a mathematician at Los Alamos, and he recalled the moment himself. In 1946, as he was convalescing from an illness and playing solitaires, a question occurred to him: what are the chances that a Canfield solitaire laid out with 52 cards will come out successfully? Ideas like this are the kind Brian Christian and Tom Griffiths gather in *Algorithms to Live By*, a book on computer science and human decisions.

He first tried to answer by pure combinatorial calculation and spent a lot of time on it. Then, in his own words, he wondered whether a more practical method than abstract thinking might be "to lay it out say one hundred times and simply observe and count the number of successful plays." The idea was to stop deriving the answer and start sampling it.

Fast computers were beginning to arrive. Ulam immediately thought of problems of neutron diffusion and other questions of mathematical physics, where the same trick might work: a process too tangled to solve on paper could be played out many times with random numbers, and the results counted. He described the idea to John von Neumann, and they began to plan an actual calculation.

In 1947 von Neumann sent a handwritten letter to Robert Richtmyer, who led the theoretical division at Los Alamos. It described how to use statistical sampling to follow neutrons through a fissioning material. He suggested starting 100 neutrons and following each for 100 collisions, and thought that might be enough. He estimated about five hours of computing time on ENIAC. Richtmyer replied that he was very interested and proposed changes.

The coding for ENIAC was finished in December 1947, and the first calculations of this kind ran in April and May of 1948. The name came from Nicholas Metropolis, who suggested it as an obvious label for the method, and he said it was not unrelated to the fact that Ulam had an uncle who borrowed money from relatives because he "just had to go to Monte Carlo." The name first appeared in print in a 1949 paper by Metropolis and Ulam.

What makes the story satisfying is how small the trigger was. A sick man playing a card game, and a thought: counting the successes might beat calculating the odds. The method that came out of that thought was soon pointed at neutron physics.

[[lessons]]
$$**When an exact answer is out of reach, run the process many times and count what happens.** A manager choosing between two ways of covering the front desk can argue about the schedules forever, or try each for a few weeks and tally how often the line gets long. The tally will not be perfect, yet it is often good enough to choose with, and it grows more reliable with every added trial. Next time a question seems too tangled to reason through, ask how you could simply try it and write down the results.$$

Unlock your phone and look at the home screen. The app you most wish you opened less is probably sitting right there, one tap away. Now picture it buried in a folder, notifications off, three swipes deep. Nothing about your willpower has changed. So why might the day play out differently?

[[story]]
There's a familiar story about why we give in to distraction. You meant to focus, the temptation showed up, and your willpower simply ran out. In *Indistractable*, Nir Eyal questions that story.

He pushes back on the popular claim that willpower is a limited resource that gets used up with each effort. His argument is that believing willpower runs out is itself part of the problem. People who think their self-control is draining away tend to give up sooner.

So instead of relying on strength in the moment, Eyal suggests shaping the situation ahead of time. The idea is precommitment: deciding in advance, while you're calm, how you'll act later, so you're guarded against a future moment of weakness.

He describes three kinds of pacts that make this concrete.

The first is an effort pact. It makes the unwanted behavior harder to do. One example is an app or tool that blocks distracting sites during set hours. The site still exists. It just takes more work to reach than it's worth in a weak moment.

The second is a price pact. It attaches a cost to getting distracted, such as money you forfeit if you don't follow through. Now slipping up isn't free.

The third is an identity pact, and it works differently from the other two. It ties behavior to how you see yourself. Eyal's example is people who call themselves vegetarians. They don't agonize over meat every time they see it on a menu. The label settled the question long before the menu arrived.

##The decision was already made, so there was nothing left to fight about in the moment.##

Alongside the pacts, Eyal offers practical ways to "hack back" the external triggers on your phone. Remove the apps you don't need. Move the tempting ones off the home screen. Turn off notifications.

None of these steps asks you to be stronger. Each one simply changes what's easy. The app that took one tap now takes several. The alert that used to call you back no longer arrives. The choice you'd otherwise face dozens of times a day gets made once, in advance, when you're thinking clearly.

That's the heart of his approach. Rather than waiting to win a battle with temptation, you arrange things so there's less of a battle to fight.

[[lessons]]
$$**Decide while you're calm.** Precommitment moves the decision from the tempting moment to an earlier, quieter one. The version of you who sets up a site blocker in the morning is better placed to choose than the version facing a stalled task at four in the afternoon. The pact carries that earlier decision forward.$$

$$**Friction and cost can be built on purpose.** Effort pacts and price pacts both change the math of giving in. One adds steps, the other adds a price. Moving an app off the home screen is the smallest version of this: a few extra taps that can interrupt a reach you didn't even know you were making.$$

$$**A label can end the debate.** The vegetarian doesn't weigh every plate of meat, because "I'm a vegetarian" answers the question in advance. An identity pact does the same for any habit. Describing yourself as someone who does, or doesn't, do something can settle choices before they begin.$$

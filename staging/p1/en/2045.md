[[open]]
A speech written to praise one candidate gets a new name pasted over the old one and is delivered, once, to about two dozen listeners. Afterwards the speaker decides that this small performance won a presidential election. He then starts turning up at the White House to collect what he thinks he is owed.

[[story]]
Candice Millard's *Destiny of the Republic* includes the man who shot President James Garfield, Charles Guiteau, and in an interview she described him as a former lawyer and evangelist who believed Garfield owed him a diplomatic post. His life before that is a record of failed schemes. Born in 1841, he joined the Oneida religious commune, started a newspaper in 1865 that folded after three months, worked mainly as a bill collector, and later toured as a preacher.

In early 1880 he moved to New York and attached himself to the Stalwart faction that backed Ulysses S. Grant. He wrote a speech titled "Grant against Hancock." When Garfield won the Republican nomination in June, Guiteau kept the speech nearly unchanged and put Garfield's name where Grant's had been. He delivered it once, to about two dozen people, and afterwards claimed his ideas had won Garfield the presidency.

After the election he became a regular in the White House reception room and wrote to Garfield many times. He received a brief audience with the president and asked for the consulship in Paris, according to the National Park Service, though he had no relevant training. After months of waiting, he concluded he would not get it.

What he decided next is recorded in the accounts of his trial. He concluded that God wanted the president removed, and he believed the Republican Party needed saving from Garfield's civil service reform, which would end patronage. He borrowed money from an old acquaintance, bought an ivory-handled pistol and followed the president around Washington for weeks.

On 2 July 1881 Garfield was walking through the Ladies Waiting Room of the Baltimore and Potomac Railway Station with Secretary of State James G. Blaine. Guiteau fired twice. The first bullet grazed Garfield's right arm and the second entered his back.

His trial began in November, two months after Garfield's death. His lawyers argued that he was insane, while the prosecution argued that he knew his act was unlawful, and he insisted that the doctors had killed Garfield, saying that he had simply shot at him. In a Christmas greeting to newspapers he wrote that he presumed he would live to be president. The jury returned a guilty verdict after deliberating for just under an hour, and he was hanged on 30 June 1882.

Nothing in this record makes the act understandable, and none of it excuses it. What it does show is a pattern: a claim of credit, a request, a long wait with no resolution, and a private story that filled the silence.

[[lessons]]
$$**When you are waiting for a decision that matters to you, set a date to ask for a clear answer, and decide beforehand what you will do with a no.** An open request keeps feeding whatever story you are telling yourself about it, and the story rarely gets kinder with time. Most of us will never do anything like what happened here, but small versions are common: the promotion you keep expecting, the apology you keep waiting for. Ask once, plainly, and let a clear answer, even a disappointing one, end the waiting. If the answer is no, you can grieve it for a day and move on, which costs far less than months of not knowing.$$

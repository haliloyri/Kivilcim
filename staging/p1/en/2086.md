A Berkeley mathematics professor spends a couple of days a month advising a small firm that trades futures by computer program. After a stretch away, he comes back and looks at the numbers. The results have deteriorated badly. He sees an opening, and he takes it.

[[story]]
Elwyn Berlekamp was a Berkeley professor, and he appears in Gregory Zuckerman's *The Man Who Solved the Market* as one of the mathematicians around Jim Simons. This story follows his own account, which he published on his university web page. The firm was Axcom, and its only business was running systematic, computer-based trading for the Medallion fund, whose pool operator was Simons. In 1986 Berlekamp began consulting there a couple of days a month, mostly to learn about futures and derivatives.

His contact lapsed in the spring of 1989. When he returned that summer, performance had deteriorated badly. Berlekamp saw a turnaround opportunity. He bought the biggest share of Axcom and became its chief executive.

Sandor Straus and other members of the firm moved from Newport Beach to Berkeley, and trading resumed in late 1989. The next calendar year was very good, in Berlekamp's words. After fees of 5 percent of assets and 20 percent of profits, the net return to investors in 1990 was 55 percent. That figure is what the investors kept, once the firm had taken its cut.

That is the sort of result that makes a person stay. Berlekamp sold instead. About sixteen months after buying in, he sold his interest to Simons at six times what he had paid.

His reason was a difference over where to live and what to do. His account describes no quarrel and no falling-out, only two men who wanted different things from the same firm. Simons wanted Axcom to move to New York, closer to the markets. Berlekamp preferred to spend more of his time on academic work. He has added that the interest he sold is now worth over a thousand times more.

Seen from the outside, the decision looks like an error that only hindsight can name. Seen from his desk in Berkeley, it was a trade he could price. He had bought low, he had fixed what was broken, he had made a sixfold return on his purchase price in sixteen months, and what remained on the table was a life in a different city doing different work. He took the money and kept the life.

[[lessons]]
$$**Decide what you want from a venture before its results make the choice for you.** Berlekamp had a clear picture of how he wanted to spend his working days, so even a very good year did not pull him toward a move he did not want. A freelance designer whose business is booming faces the same fork when a bigger client offers a role that would swallow every evening. Writing down the life you are protecting, in a few plain sentences before any offer arrives, gives you a measure to hold the offer against when the numbers get loud.$$

A college football coach once ended up in hospital with exhaustion. In six seasons his teams had won twelve games and lost forty-one. Years later the people who ran some of the most valuable companies on earth would phone that same man before their hardest decisions, and he could still say exactly what he had got wrong on the sideline.

[[story]]
Before Silicon Valley, Bill Campbell, the subject of *Trillion Dollar Coach*, was a small-town kid with a football. He grew up in Homestead, Pennsylvania, where his father worked nights at the mill and days as a teacher and basketball coach. Campbell chose Columbia, where his father knew the football coach, and played guard and linebacker. He stopped growing at five foot ten and a half, and his nickname on the team was "Ballsy."

In 1961 he captained the Columbia team that tied Harvard for the Ivy League title, the only one in the program's history. A teammate, the actor Brian Dennehy, told Fortune: "There was this core, this nuclear reactor, at the center of the team, and that was Billy."

He came back in 1974 as head coach. By then he had finished a master's degree while assisting his old coach, Buff Donelli, and had worked on the staff at Boston College. Columbia had not had a winning season since 1963, according to the university's own football exhibition, and it did not have one under him either. Over six seasons his teams won twelve games, lost forty-one and tied one.

The job cost him physically, and not only in the standings. Fortune reported that he was hospitalized for exhaustion. In the middle of the 1979 season he announced that he would resign, and the university's account lists mounting losses, too little support and the pressures of recruiting among the reasons.

What he said afterwards is the interesting part. He told Fortune that his flaw as a coach was that he had never asked his players to put football above everything else. That is a very specific confession. The schedule, the talent and the luck all stayed out of it. He named a demand he had held back, and it was one he could actually have made, which is what makes the sentence useful.

Columbia kept faith with him anyway. He joined its board of trustees in 2003 and became chairman in 2005. When he died in 2016, flags at Baker Field were lowered and the number 67, his number, was painted on the turf. No Columbia varsity athlete will wear it again.

[[lessons]]
$$**Turn a failure into one precise sentence about what you did or did not ask of people.** "It was a bad stretch" explains nothing, while "I never asked them to put this first" points at a choice you can make differently next time. When a project goes badly, write down the single request you held back, whether that was a deadline, a hard conversation or a level of commitment. A loss described that exactly stops being a verdict on you. It becomes a piece of information you can carry into the next team, the next quarter or the next season.$$

A school principal in southern India stands in front of his students and introduces a famous guest from abroad. The guest is a visiting American, a preacher and a leader. The word the principal chooses for him is one the guest has never used about himself, and it stings.

[[story]]
In 1959 Martin Luther King Jr. spent a month in India, a trip he made with his wife, Coretta, to learn from the country that had produced Gandhi. Isabel Wilkerson's *Caste* tells one afternoon of that trip. In Trivandrum, in the state of Kerala, the Kings visited a school whose students came from families that had once been classed as untouchables, the lowest rung of the Indian caste order.

The principal presented his guest to the students as a fellow untouchable from the United States. King later described his own first reaction in plain terms. In a sermon in July 1965, he repeated the principal's words to his congregation and added that for a moment he was "a bit shocked and peeved" to be referred to as an Untouchable. By that account, the story did not become public until about six years after the visit.

What came next is the part Wilkerson cares about. According to her account, King stayed offended only briefly. He thought it over and came to see that the principal had a point: Black Americans were also a people set apart by birth, assigned a place at the bottom, and kept there by custom and by law. In her telling, he realised that he had lived inside a caste system of his own all his life, one not unlike India's.

The principal's sentence was a comparison, and the strength of a comparison is that it puts two separate worlds under the same light. In India, caste was fixed by birth. In America, people were sorted by what the law and custom said about their skin. In both places the ranking looked at where a person was born, never at what that person did. Wilkerson's book builds on this shared structure, and the principal's sentence showed it to King through his own life.

Notice how little happened on the surface. Nobody argued with the principal, and the school visit went on. A man was handed a label he disliked, and he asked what it was pointing at instead of how to push it away.

His first reaction mattered too, and King did not hide it. He told it against himself, in a church, years later. That candour is part of why the episode is believable: it shows a person feeling the sting and then following it to the question underneath. For Wilkerson, the principal's sentence became a doorway to a larger argument, that what Americans call race is better understood as caste.

[[lessons]]
$$**A label that stings is worth examining before it is rejected.** The first reaction is usually to defend yourself against the word, which means the question underneath it never gets asked. The person who coined the label has often noticed a resemblance you were unable to see from inside your own view. At work this might be a nickname for your team, a fair-minded colleague's blunt description of your habits, or a comparison you find unflattering. You can feel the sting, say so honestly, and still ask what the label is pointing at, because the comparison sometimes sees something that your own vocabulary was built to avoid.$$

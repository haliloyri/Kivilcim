One of the most famous women in the world walks through a crowded New York subway station. She rides the train like everyone else. Nobody looks twice. Then, a few minutes later, on the street above, she makes a small change, and within moments she's surrounded.

[[story]]
Olivia Fox Cabane opens *The Charisma Myth* with this story about Marilyn Monroe.

Monroe wanted to prove something to a magazine editor and a photographer who were with her. She could, she said, turn her famous persona on and off at will. So, dressed in ordinary clothes, she walked into a busy subway station in New York. She stood on the platform. She got on a train. She sat among the other passengers.

No one recognized her. The most photographed woman of her era was, for the moment, just another person on the subway. The photographer took pictures of her sitting there, and not a single commuter reacted.

When they came back up to the street, Monroe turned to her companions and asked whether they wanted to see her become *Marilyn*.

##Then she changed, not her clothes or her face, but her way of being.##

She fluffed up her hair. She struck a pose. Her posture shifted. She began to project her presence outward, instead of holding it in. Within moments, people noticed her. Heads turned. A crowd began to gather, and soon her companions had to help her get away.

Nothing about her appearance had fundamentally changed. She was the same person in the same clothes. What changed was her behavior: how she held herself, how she moved, what she was signaling to the people around her.

For Cabane, this story captures the central idea of her book. Most people think of charisma as something mysterious and innate. Some people simply have it, the belief goes, and everyone else doesn't. Cabane calls that belief the charisma myth.

Her argument is that charisma is the result of specific behaviors. Some are visible, like posture, eye contact and how you use your voice. Others are internal: where your attention is, how you're feeling, and what that inner state leaks out through your body language. If Marilyn Monroe could switch charisma off to become invisible and switch it back on to draw a crowd, then charisma isn't a fixed trait. It's something people do.

And if it's something people do, Cabane argues, it can be learned.

[[lessons]]
$$**Charisma is behavior, not magic.** Monroe didn't change her face or clothes. She changed her posture, energy and presence, and the reaction of everyone around her changed instantly. That suggests charisma is less a gift you're born with and more a set of signals you send, often without realizing it.$$

$$**Your body is always communicating.** On the subway, Monroe's body language said "don't notice me," and nobody did. On the street, it said "here I am," and a crowd formed. Posture, movement and how you hold your attention send powerful messages. Becoming aware of them is the first step toward choosing them.$$

$$**Charisma can be learned.** Cabane's whole book rests on this story's implication. If charisma can be switched on and off, it can be practiced. That's good news for anyone who has assumed they're simply not charismatic. It becomes a skill to develop, not a verdict on who you are.$$

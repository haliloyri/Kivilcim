Picture a car factory where the person tightening bolts is allowed to stop everything. Not just their own station. The whole line, over a single defect. It sounds like a way to miss every deadline you've ever set. At Toyota it was standard practice, and the factory ran faster because of it. Eric Ries thought that one strange rule had a lot to teach anyone building something the world hasn't seen yet.

[[story]]
Start on the factory floor. Under the Toyota Production System, any worker who spotted a defect could pull a cord, the andon cord, and the production line stopped. It didn't slow down or carry on while someone made a note. It stopped until the problem was dealt with.

Think about how that must have felt the first few times. You're on a moving line, there's pressure to hit numbers, and one person can bring the whole thing to a halt because a part is slightly wrong. It looks like wasted speed.

In *The Lean Startup*, Ries argues the opposite. Stopping to fix a problem where it appears feels slower in the moment, but over time it gives you faster and more reliable work. A problem you push past doesn't disappear. It sits in the system, the next one lands on top of it, and eventually you're paying for a pile of problems that have become expensive to untangle. Ries brings the andon idea into software to make this point: when tests fail, you stop deploying and fix the cause before you keep going.

That's one piece of a bigger move. Ries takes ideas from lean manufacturing, the thinking Toyota is known for, and adapts them to startups. The catch is that a startup isn't a factory. A factory knows what it's making. A startup operates under extreme uncertainty. It doesn't yet know whether anyone wants the thing, or how it will ever grow.

So Ries changes what counts as progress. In a factory, progress is output: more cars, fewer defects. For a startup he says the unit of progress is **validated learning**, meaning things you've actually found out about customers, backed by evidence.

He asks you to treat the whole company as an experiment. Your strategy is a set of hypotheses waiting to be tested. Two matter most. The value hypothesis asks whether this delivers real value to the people using it. The growth hypothesis asks how new people will find it.

To test them, you run what he calls the Build-Measure-Learn loop. You build a minimum viable product, the crudest version that can teach you something. You measure how real customers respond. Then you learn whether to pivot, meaning change direction, or persevere. The goal is to go around that loop as fast as you can.

##Ries suggests planning the loop backwards: first decide what you need to learn, then what you'd have to measure to learn it, and only then what you'd have to build.##

That order changes the question you start with. It stops being *what should we make?* and becomes:

> What do we need to find out, and what's the least we can build to find it out?

That still leaves a hard question: how do you know you're getting anywhere when revenue and cost figures tell you almost nothing yet? Ries's answer is **innovation accounting**. You set a baseline with your MVP. You try to improve the engine that drives growth. Then you decide whether to pivot or persevere.

It only works if you measure honestly. Ries separates vanity metrics, numbers that look impressive and make everyone feel good, from actionable metrics, which show cause and effect and actually tell you what to do next.

Back to the tension. Stopping the line for one flaw looks like waste. So does shipping something crude just to learn. Ries's point is that the real waste is moving fast without learning anything.

[[lessons]]
$$**Count what you learned, not what you made.** For Toyota, output made sense as a measure because the product was already known. Ries saw that a startup doesn't have that certainty, so he made validated learning the unit of progress. If you're working on something new, a week of shipping features can leave you exactly as ignorant as you were on Monday. A week that settles one real question about your customers moves you forward, even if it produced much less.$$

$$**Plan the loop from the end.** Build-Measure-Learn is the order things happen in, but Ries suggests planning it in reverse. Decide what you need to learn, then what you'd measure, then what you'd build. That's how you end up with a minimum viable product instead of a maximum one. The build shrinks to whatever the question actually requires, and nothing gets built just because it seemed like the next thing to do.$$

$$**Pull the cord early, and trust honest numbers.** The andon cord shows that stopping to fix a problem at its source is faster over time than pushing through while problems pile up. The same discipline applies to measurement. Vanity metrics let you keep going without looking closely. Actionable metrics show cause and effect, so you can see a problem clearly enough to stop and fix it.$$

[[reflect]]
- Look at what's on your plate this week. Which piece are you building mostly because it feels productive, when you can't say what it will teach you?
- Pick one number you check regularly. When did it last change a decision you made? If it never has, it may be a vanity metric.
- Where in your work are small problems getting parked "for later"? Picture pulling the cord on one of them today.

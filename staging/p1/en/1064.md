[[open]]
Picture the best meeting of your career. You've just walked out of a room where the people at the very top liked what they heard. Then your boss says, "Let's take a walk." She tells you it went well. You can feel a "but" coming, and you've already decided it won't matter much. This is how one of the best-known management books of the last decade begins.

[[story]]
Kim Scott had not been at Google long. She was leading the AdSense team, and she had been asked to present the business's progress to Google's founders and to CEO Eric Schmidt.

It went very well. The numbers were strong, strong enough that Schmidt's reaction wasn't a grilling. He asked what resources the team needed. For someone new to the company, that is about as good as a presentation to the top of the building can go.

Afterward, her boss, Sheryl Sandberg, suggested they walk together. Sandberg started with praise. She talked about what had gone well in the presentation and about the team's results, and she meant it.

Then she raised one more thing. Scott had said "um" a lot. Sandberg offered to set her up with a speech coach.

Scott waved it off. She didn't argue. She made a small dismissive gesture that meant *sure, fine, not important*. She had just impressed the CEO. A few filler words hardly seemed worth a coach.

Sandberg noticed the gesture. In the book Scott recalls her reply, and the gist of it was this:

> I can tell I'm not getting through to you. I'll have to be more direct.

Then came the line Scott never forgot. Paraphrased, it was:

> When you say "um" every third word, it makes you sound stupid.

##The polite version had bounced right off her. The blunt one landed.##

Scott went to the speech coach. Looking back, she didn't see that walk as a moment when her boss was harsh. She saw it as a real kindness. Sandberg had cared enough to make sure the message got through, even after the easy version had been brushed aside.

Years later, Scott asked herself why Sandberg had been such a good boss. She settled on two dimensions. The first was **caring personally**: Sandberg's praise was genuine, and her offer of a coach was an investment in Scott. The second was **challenging directly**: she was willing to say the uncomfortable thing plainly once hinting hadn't worked.

From those two axes Scott built four quadrants. Care plus challenge is *Radical Candor*. Challenge without care is *Obnoxious Aggression*. Care without challenge is *Ruinous Empathy*. Neither is *Manipulative Insincerity*.

Her sharpest claim is about the third quadrant. Most management mistakes, she argues, happen in Ruinous Empathy, where people keep quiet to be nice and the other person never learns what's wrong. Her advice for getting out of it starts with you. Ask for criticism yourself first. Listen without defending, and thank the person, before you start handing criticism to anyone else.

[[lessons]]
$$**Softened criticism is easy to wave away.** Sandberg's first version was polite: a mention of the "ums" and an offer of a coach. Scott dismissed it with a gesture. The problem wasn't Scott's ego. The message was vague enough to be optional. Only when Sandberg said plainly what the "ums" did to how Scott came across did it register. If your feedback can be shrugged off, it probably will be.$$

$$**Directness reads as help when care came first.** The blunt line could have stung like an insult. It didn't, because of what came before it. Sandberg praised the results sincerely, and she offered a concrete resource instead of just a complaint. By the time she got direct, Scott had no reason to doubt her motives. The care has to be real and visible before the challenge arrives.$$

$$**Silence to be nice is the common failure, and it starts with you.** Scott puts most management mistakes in Ruinous Empathy. Picture Sandberg stopping after the praise: Scott keeps saying "um" in front of executives, and nobody ever tells her. Scott's remedy is to earn candor by asking for criticism first, listening without defending, and saying thank you. It's hard to give what you won't accept.$$

[[reflect]]
- Think of a hint you've dropped more than once. Is it possible the other person never actually heard it as a problem?
- Recall the last time someone criticized you. Did you make your own version of Scott's dismissive wave?
- Who in your life has praised you honestly? Would you trust a blunt sentence from them more than from anyone else?
- When did you last ask someone what you're getting wrong, and just say thanks?

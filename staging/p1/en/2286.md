In one college office, administrative assistants sit at neighbouring desks. They answer the same phones, book the same rooms and sort the same mail. Ask them what the work is to them and the answers split three ways. Some say it pays the bills. Some say it is a step upward. Some say it has become part of who they are.

[[story]]
In the mid-1990s the psychologist Amy Wrzesniewski and her colleagues Clark McCauley, Paul Rozin and Barry Schwartz asked a simple question. When people talk about their work, do they mean the same thing? Their answer appeared in 1997 in the *Journal of Research in Personality* under the title "Jobs, Careers, and Callings".

They drew on a framework from earlier sociologists with three ways of relating to work. A job is mainly about material benefits: you work for the money and the rest of life happens elsewhere. A career adds deeper personal investment and a wish to advance. A calling is work that has become inseparable from the rest of your life. The researchers surveyed 196 employees at two work sites, from clerical staff to professionals, and asked which description fitted them best.

The answers were spread out. Roughly even numbers of people saw their work as a job, a career or a calling. That is the first surprise, because many people assume that only doctors, artists or founders could have a calling.

Then came a closer look at one group: 24 administrative assistants at a college. They had nearly the same kind of work, in the same type of workplace, and the paper reports the same spread across job, career and calling among them too. The authors concluded that the differences could not be explained by demographics or by occupation alone.

Newport brings this research into his argument about passion, according to published summaries of the book. The study matters to his case because it cuts against a common story, in which some kinds of work are inherently meaningful and others are not. If two people with nearly identical tasks can describe them so differently, then the feeling cannot be sitting inside the task, waiting to be discovered. It has something to do with the person, the way the work is done and what the person has built around it.

That is a more practical idea than it first sounds. You cannot change your job description by wishing, but you can ask what makes some people in the same role feel that the work is part of their lives. The study does not hand over the answer. What it does is move the question from the question of which job has a hidden calling in it to what the person is doing with the one they have.

[[lessons]]
$$**Check how you relate to the work before you decide the work is wrong.** Two people at neighbouring desks can have a job and a calling, so the meaning of a role is not stored in the task list. Before you quit, change teams or hunt for your passion, ask three questions: what am I getting better at here, what do I control, and what would make this feel more like my own? Write the answers down and try one of them for a month, then judge the role again. You may find the problem was never the work itself.$$

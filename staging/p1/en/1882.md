A man runs up a grassy slope in Montana with a fire behind him that moves faster than he does. He stops, strikes a match and sets the grass in front of him alight. Then he lies down in the ashes. It looks like madness, and it saves his life.

[[story]]
On 5 August 1949, lightning started a fire in the Helena National Forest in Montana, in a ravine called Mann Gulch. A crew of smokejumpers parachuted in to deal with it. Smokejumpers are firefighters trained to reach remote fires from the air, and their foreman that day was Wagner Dodge.

Then the fire changed its nature, and the situation the crew had trained for stopped being the situation they were in. Firefighters call it a blow-up, a sudden surge of flame that races across the ground. In about ten minutes it spread over roughly 3,000 acres. The crew had a plan for a fire that turned on them, and the plan was to run for safety. They ran.

Dodge took a different decision. Running had stopped being a plan and had become a hope, because the fire was closing the distance. So he did something that had no rule behind it. He lit a second fire in the grass ahead of him, let it burn the ground clean, and lay down in the burnt patch while the main fire swept past.

The day ended with 13 dead, twelve smokejumpers and one fire guard. Three men survived: Dodge, Sallee and Rumsey. The Forest Service later cleared Dodge of blame for what happened.

What separates Dodge from the others is not strength or speed. He stopped asking how to run faster and asked what the fire needed to reach him. It needed fuel, and he removed the fuel from the spot where he lay. That is a different question, and it only occurs to you once you let go of the plan that has always worked.

Running had been the right answer for ordinary fires, and that is exactly why it was hard to give up. A plan that has saved you many times feels like safety, even on the one day it has turned into the danger. Dodge held the old plan only as long as it worked, and he dropped it while some room was left to try something new. Few people manage that at the moment when everyone around them is still sprinting, and it is hard to do with the heat at your back.

[[lessons]]
$$**When the plan that always worked starts failing, switch to a new one while there is still room to try it.** A habit that served you for years feels like safety, so you keep running it past the point where it helps. A team that wins every quarter with the same sales script can cling to it long after buyers have changed. Set yourself a trigger in advance: if this approach has not moved things by a given point, you will ask what the situation actually needs. Doing so gives you a few seconds of choice while others are still running.$$

[[open]]
In a lecture hall, a professor holds up a single banknote and offers it for sale to the room. The opening bid is a few cents. Hands go up. Ten minutes later two students are still bidding against each other, and both look slightly sick.

[[story]]
The dollar auction was invented by the economist Martin Shubik and published in 1971 in the *Journal of Conflict Resolution*. Ori and Rom Brafman use a version of it in *Sway* to show how commitment drags people onward. The rules are simple, and one detail turns the game into a trap.

A dollar bill is sold to the highest bidder, who pays the amount of the bid and gets the dollar. The second-highest bidder also pays the amount of their last bid, and gets nothing at all. Bidding starts at a few cents, so at first everyone sees a bargain: a dollar for a nickel.

The trouble begins when the bids approach a dollar. Suppose you have bid 90 cents and I have bid 95 cents. If it stops there, I win and you are out 90 cents for nothing. A bid of one dollar would at least get the dollar back, so you raise it. Now I am the one facing a loss of 95 cents, and my best way out is to bid a dollar and five cents, which loses me only a nickel instead. Each step looks smart by itself. Seen from outside, two people are paying more than a dollar to win a dollar.

The logic does not stop at one dollar, because stopping always means taking the whole loss now. Past that point, the money already spent keeps both bidders in. Only the auctioneer profits, since he collects both bids. According to a published review of *Sway*, in a version with a twenty-dollar bill the bids reached 204 dollars.

What makes this such a clean example is that nobody in the game is stupid. Every bid answers the question "what is my best move from here?", and the answer is always to continue. Nobody asks the other question: "Would I enter this game, if I were starting now, knowing how it ends?" The sunk bids make that question feel disloyal to the person you were a minute ago.

There is one more feature to notice. The players are not fighting over the bill any more. They are fighting to avoid being the one who loses first. The prize has quietly changed from winning a dollar to not admitting a mistake, and that is a prize no one can sell back.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you raise your stake, ask what you would do if you were starting fresh today.** Money, hours and pride already spent pull you towards the next step, even when the next step costs more than it can return. In a project that is behind schedule, a negotiation that has become a contest or a relationship you keep patching, the question changes the picture. Write down the price at which you would walk away before you begin, and let the other people you work with hold you to it. Then ask the question aloud in front of the team, because the answer is often already in the room and simply has not been asked.$$

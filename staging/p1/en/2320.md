A small capsule rides the swell of the Atlantic, and the man inside has just finished a flight that went exactly to plan. He waits for a helicopter to come and lift him out. Then the hatch blows away, and the sea starts to pour in.

[[story]]
Gus Grissom is one of the seven astronauts in Tom Wolfe's *The Right Stuff*, and this is the flight that followed him for years afterwards. On 21 July 1961 he flew Liberty Bell 7, a Mercury capsule launched on a Redstone rocket, in the second American crewed spaceflight. The flight itself went well. The trouble began after the splashdown.

Grissom's capsule had a different hatch from the one on Alan Shepard's Freedom 7. Shepard's opened with a latch, while Liberty Bell 7 had an explosive hatch that could be blown off in an emergency. As Grissom floated in the ocean waiting for a recovery helicopter, the hatch fired. The sea poured in, and Grissom got out and into the water. The capsule filled and sank to the bottom of the Atlantic.

Afterwards the question was why. Grissom said it was a malfunction and denied panicking, and some people at NASA suspected that he had set it off himself. Nobody could settle the argument, because the evidence lay on the ocean floor.

The capsule was found only in 1999. A salvage expedition led by Curt Newport, funded by the Discovery Channel, raised it from about 15,000 feet of water. It came up without its hatch. Years later, in 2021, a new analysis reported by NPR suggested that a technical cause, such as static electricity, could have fired the hatch. That reading would support Grissom's account.

An explosive hatch exists for speed: in an emergency every second counts, and a charge removes the door at once. That same speed leaves little room for a second chance when something goes wrong, and here it left nothing behind to explain what had happened.

The part worth noticing is how long the question stayed open. For decades there was a man's reputation on one side and a missing piece of metal on the other. Anyone who judged him in the first week was guessing, and the guess depended on whom they already trusted.

The episode also shows what a reasonable observer does in such a case. He keeps the possibilities open, writes down what is known, and lets later evidence speak. The wreck was recovered, the analysis was redone, and the story changed. A verdict handed down early would have had to be taken back.

[[lessons]]
$$**While the evidence on a person is ambiguous, hold back the verdict and keep the record, so later evidence can still speak.** Grissom lived for years under a suspicion of panic, and the first guesses leaned on reputation and impression, not on facts. At work the same thing happens when a deadline is missed or a system fails and someone is blamed in the first meeting. Ask what you actually know, what you would need to know, and who can check it. Say plainly that you do not know yet, and write down the facts so that you can correct yourself without embarrassment.$$

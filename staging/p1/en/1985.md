A coin is tossed until it lands heads. Heads on the first toss pays one ducat, on the second two, on the third four, and the prize doubles every time. Someone asks what a ticket to this game is worth. The arithmetic gives an answer that nobody believes.

[[story]]
Peter Bernstein's *Against the Gods* takes up Daniel Bernoulli and the question of what a gamble is really worth. It began with a puzzle his cousin Nicolaus posed in a letter to Pierre Raymond de Montmort, dated 9 September 1713. Peter tosses a coin until it lands heads, and pays Paul a prize that doubles with each toss. Work out the average prize, the way Pascal and Fermat had taught, and each possible ending adds half a ducat to it. There are endless possible endings, so the average is endless too.

Nobody would pay an endless sum for a place in that game. The mathematics said one thing and plain sense said another. The puzzle took its name from the city where Daniel then worked: the St. Petersburg paradox.

Daniel's answer, published in 1738 in the proceedings of the St. Petersburg Academy, was that the average prize was the wrong thing to measure. The value of an item, he wrote, must not be based on the price but on the utility it yields. A ducat means something different to a rich man than to a poor one, and the same gift gives the poor man a great deal more.

He turned that into a rule: the satisfaction from a small gain in wealth shrinks in proportion to how much you already own. Do the sums with that rule and the endless average disappears. Daniel judged that a reasonable person would sell Paul's chance, with great pleasure, for twenty ducats.

The rule also explained insurance. In an example from the paper as later retold, a merchant ships goods worth 10,000 rubles from Amsterdam, with a 5% chance that the ship is lost, and is offered cover for 800 rubles. The average loss is only 500 rubles, so the premium is more than a fair price. Whether he should pay it depends on what else he owns: a man with little besides this cargo has everything to lose, and a man with a large fortune has far less.

That is what changed. Risk was measured against the person who had to bear it, and no longer by the size of the bet alone.

[[lessons]]
$$**Judge a risk against what the loss would do to you, not only against the odds.** A freelance designer is offered a client who pays late but pays well, and she weighs the pay against the chance of a long delay. If she has savings, a delay is an annoyance she can absorb; if rent is due next week, the same delay could sink her. The same risk therefore deserves a different answer from each of them, and neither is being foolish. Before you take or refuse a gamble, ask what the bad outcome would cost you in your own circumstances.$$

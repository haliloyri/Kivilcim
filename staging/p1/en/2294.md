[[open]]
A farm fair on the English coast, a fat ox standing in a pen, and a stack of numbered cards sold for sixpence each. Hundreds of people, butchers and farmers and passers-by, write down how much the animal will weigh once it is slaughtered and dressed. Nobody is allowed to peek at anyone else's card.

[[story]]
James Surowiecki opens *The Wisdom of Crowds* with a story about Francis Galton, the Victorian scientist, and a weight-judging contest at the West of England Fat Stock and Poultry Exhibition in Plymouth. Competitors paid sixpence for a stamped, numbered card, wrote their name, address and estimate on it, and the closest guesses won prizes. The question was how heavy a particular ox would be after it had been slaughtered and dressed.

Galton got hold of the cards afterwards. Thirteen were defective or illegible and he set them aside, which left 787 estimates. He noted that the sixpenny fee probably kept out practical jokers. Then he did what he did with most things, which was to measure. He ranked the guesses from lowest to highest and took the middle one, the value half the crowd had guessed above and half below.

That middle estimate was 1,207 pounds. The butchered ox weighed 1,198 pounds. The crowd had missed by nine pounds, less than one percent. Galton also saw that the guesses piled up around the middle and thinned out on both sides, and that individual guesses were often far off: he put the typical error of a single person at about 37 pounds. The accuracy belonged to the group, not to any one person in it.

Galton published this in *Nature* in 1907 under the title "Vox Populi". A later re-examination of his working papers corrected some of his arithmetic. It put the plain average of the guesses at about 1,196 pounds, even closer to the truth than his median.

Why did it work? Each of those 787 people had a different scrap of knowledge: someone knew cattle, someone had seen a similar animal, someone only had a feeling. Each guess carried some real information and some noise. Because the people wrote their numbers separately, the noise did not line up. Errors too high and errors too low cancelled, and what was left was the shared signal.

That is the condition worth noticing. The crowd was not wise because it was crowded. It was wise because the guesses were independent, collected cheaply and added up by a simple rule. Put the same people in a room to agree on one number, and the loudest voice would pull everyone toward its own error.

[[lessons]]
$$**Collect separate estimates first, and only then let people talk.** When a team has to guess a deadline, a budget or a risk, the first number said aloud tends to drag all the others toward it. If everyone writes a figure down privately and you take the middle value, the high and low errors cancel and you keep what people actually know. Try it in your next planning meeting: write, reveal, take the median, and only then ask the outliers why they differ. You will often learn more from the disagreement than from the average itself at no cost and in a few minutes.$$

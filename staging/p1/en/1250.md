Two friends decide to save. The first puts money away every year from age nineteen to twenty-seven, then stops completely. The second starts at twenty-seven and keeps going every single year until retirement. Same yearly amount. Which one ends up with more? The answer surprises most people.

[[story]]
In *Money: Master the Game*, Tony Robbins returns again and again to one force he believes ordinary people underestimate: **compounding**. When your money earns a return, and then that return starts earning returns of its own, growth begins to build on itself. At first it's barely noticeable. Over long periods, it becomes enormous.

The most important ingredient in compounding, Robbins stresses, isn't a high income or a clever investment. It's time.

A classic illustration makes his point vivid. Imagine the two friends from above. The early saver invests a fixed amount each year for about eight years, starting at nineteen, and then never adds another cent. The late saver invests the same amount each year, starting at twenty-seven, for almost forty years.

The late saver contributes far more money in total. And yet, with a steady high rate of return, the early saver can end up with a similar or even larger sum by retirement.

##The early saver's secret isn't more money. It's more years.##

The money invested at nineteen has decades to compound. Each year's growth adds to the base for the next year's growth. By the time the late saver starts, the early saver's money already has a head start that keeps widening. The exact numbers depend on the return you assume, and real markets don't deliver the same return every year. But across reasonable assumptions, the pattern holds: the early years are extraordinarily valuable.

Robbins draws a practical conclusion. Many people wait to start investing until they earn more, pay off something, or feel financially ready. That waiting feels sensible. But in a world ruled by compounding, it's expensive. Every year you wait is a year your money can never get back.

His message is to start now, with what you have, even if it's small. Automate it, so you don't have to rely on willpower. Increase it as your income grows. And then, crucially, leave it alone long enough for compounding to do its work.

For young people especially, this is one of the most powerful financial truths there is. And for anyone older, it's a reason not to wait even one more year.

[[lessons]]
$$**Time matters more than amount.** In the illustration, the early saver puts in far less money but can end up with as much or more, because that money had decades to grow. Robbins's point is that when you start often matters more than how much you invest.$$

$$**The early years are the most valuable.** Compounding builds slowly and then accelerates. Money invested early sits at the base of that curve and benefits from every later year of growth. That's why a year of delay costs far more than it seems.$$

$$**Start with what you have.** Waiting until you earn more feels responsible, but it wastes the one ingredient you can't get back: time. Robbins encourages starting small, automating it and increasing it later. A modest beginning today often beats a larger one years from now. Consistency over decades matters more than any single brilliant decision.$$

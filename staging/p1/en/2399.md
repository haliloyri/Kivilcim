In a glass case in London lies a page covered in neat brown handwriting that nobody can read at a glance. Hold it up to a mirror and it turns into plain Italian. For centuries people have asked what was being hidden, and the answer from the people who look after the page is a good deal less dramatic.

[[story]]
Walter Isaacson's *Leonardo da Vinci* draws heavily on the notebooks Leonardo filled over his life, and this story is about the way he wrote in them. Many of his pages are in mirror writing: the lines run from right to left, and the letters are reversed, so they read normally only in a mirror.

One surviving collection is the Codex Arundel, now in the British Library as Arundel MS 263. It is a gathering of loose papers in Italian, with diagrams, drawings and short texts, dated from about 1478 to about 1518. In the seventeenth century it was bought by Thomas Howard, Earl of Arundel, and it later reached the British Museum library, the ancestor of today's British Library.

The popular explanation is secrecy. A genius with dangerous ideas, so the legend goes, wrote in a private code. The British Library says this is a myth. Leonardo was left-handed, and for a left-handed writer using ink, moving right to left keeps the hand behind the pen, so it does not drag across what has just been written. In other words, he avoided smudging.

Other accounts are less sure and allow that privacy might have played a part. It is fair to say that nobody can read his mind, but the practical explanation needs no hidden motive. It rests on a plain fact about hands and wet ink.

There is a lesson in how easily the secret-code story won. A mirror script looks like a puzzle, and a puzzle begs for an answer with a mystery in it. The ordinary reason, a left hander and a wet pen, is less exciting and so travels less well.

Compare the two explanations by what each needs. Secrecy needs someone to keep out, a reader worth worrying about, and a code that would actually stop him. Smudging needs only a left hand and wet ink. The second asks for far fewer assumptions. The British Library has also put the Codex Arundel pages online, so anyone can open one and try the mirror.

Meanwhile the notebooks themselves tell a plainer story. They are working pages, full of diagrams and notes made over decades, and the handwriting is that of someone writing for himself, in the way that came easily to his hand. Anyone who has kept a notebook knows the feeling.

[[lessons]]
$$**Look for the practical reason before you reach for the mysterious one.** When a person does something odd, such as an unusual format, a strange filing system or a habit that baffles the team, a simple explanation is often hiding in plain sight. Leonardo's backwards writing looked like a code and, by the Library's account, was mostly a way to keep ink off his hand. Before you judge a colleague's quirk or defend your own, ask what problem it solves for the person doing it. The answer is usually more useful than the theory, and it saves an argument about motives.$$

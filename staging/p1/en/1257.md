"I can't." You probably said it this week. Maybe about a class you never signed up for, a call you keep putting off, a project you turned down. It sounds like a plain statement of fact. Susan Jeffers suggests a small experiment: say "I won't" instead, and listen to what happens to the sentence. Something shifts, and it isn't only the grammar.

[[story]]
In *Feel the Fear and Do It Anyway*, first published in 1987, Susan Jeffers goes after a habit most of us never notice. She argues that a lot of fear is kept alive by what she calls a victim stance. From that position, whatever happens to you is someone else's doing, or the fault of circumstances. The boss, the economy, the family, the timing. You are the one things happen to.

Her answer is responsibility. That word tends to make people brace. It sounds like being handed the bill for everything that went wrong. Jeffers means something different, and her definition has two halves.

Taking responsibility, she writes, means never blaming anyone else for what you are being, doing, having or feeling. That part is familiar. The second half is the surprise: it also means never blaming yourself.

##Blaming yourself, in her view, is not the grown-up version of responsibility. It is simply another way of staying a victim.##

Whether the finger points outward or inward, the effect is the same. The situation becomes a verdict someone has passed on you, and your job is to suffer it. Responsibility, for Jeffers, is about reclaiming power, not accepting guilt.

So how do you actually do that? Her concrete tool is surprisingly small. She calls it the Pain-to-Power Vocabulary, a list of word swaps.

"I can't" becomes "I won't."

"I should" becomes "I could."

"It's a problem" becomes "It's an opportunity."

"It's not my fault" becomes "I'm totally responsible."

"It's terrible" becomes "It's a learning experience."

Look at the first pair. "I can't" describes a wall. "I won't" describes a decision. Sometimes the decision is a perfectly good one, and saying "won't" lets you own it instead of apologizing for it. Sometimes, once you hear yourself say it, the decision sounds less convincing than the wall did.

"I should" works the same way. A "should" hangs over you like a debt. A "could" puts an option on the table, one you are free to take or leave.

The last swap changes where a setback lands. "It's terrible" closes the story. "It's a learning experience" keeps it open, and keeps it from becoming a judgment on who you are.

Jeffers' point is that the words we use shape how powerless or capable we feel, even when the situation itself is exactly the same. Nothing outside you has to change for the sentence to change. The swaps don't fix circumstances. They change who is standing in the middle of them.

[[lessons]]
$$**Responsibility cuts both ways.** Jeffers' definition rules out two moves at once: blaming others and blaming yourself. Most of us stop after the first one and assume that owning a problem means punishing ourselves for it. Her framing takes that option away. If self-blame is just another victim position, then owning a situation means looking at what you can do next, without anyone, including you, being put on trial.$$

$$**One word can hide a choice.** "I can't" and "I won't" often describe the same behavior. The difference is that the first one hides the decision and the second one admits it. The same goes for "should" and "could". Swapping the word does not force you to act differently. It shows you that a choice was sitting there all along, and lets you make it on purpose.$$

$$**A setback is not a verdict.** "It's terrible" treats a bad outcome as the end of the matter, and often as a statement about you. "It's a learning experience" keeps the event in proportion: something happened, and there is something to take from it. The facts stay the same. What changes is whether you walk away smaller or with something useful.$$

[[reflect]]
- Think of the last time you said "I can't" about something. Would "I won't" have been the more honest sentence?
- When a plan goes wrong, where does your mind point first: at circumstances, at other people, or at yourself?
- Which "should" has been hanging over you the longest, and how does it sound as a "could"?

[[open]]
In a big house in Florida, a journalist sits across from a retired chief executive. He has a list of twenty traits and reads them out one at a time. Each time, the man across from him answers in a way that sounds like a reference for his own career.

[[story]]
One of the encounters in Jon Ronson's *The Psychopath Test* was with Al Dunlap, an American executive nicknamed "Chainsaw Al" for his harsh cost cutting. He took over Sunbeam in 1996, and a New York Daily News headline from that period counted 6,000 jobs going.

Ronson tracked him down at his home in Florida and put Robert Hare's psychopathy checklist in front of him. According to published accounts, Dunlap read the traits on it not as flaws but as qualities that running a company demands. The same behavior could carry two names: "ruthless" on one side of the table, "decisive" on the other.

No diagnosis is being made here. What can be said about Dunlap comes from the court and regulatory record.

Sunbeam's board had hired him in July 1996 as a turnaround specialist. In 1997 the company reported record earnings of $189 million.

Sunbeam's board fired him in June 1998 after concluding that he had misled investors. In a complaint filed in May 2001, the US Securities and Exchange Commission alleged that at least $60 million of those 1997 earnings came from accounting fraud, citing methods such as booking goods not yet delivered as sales and pulling sales forward from later periods. Dunlap called the allegations "totally false." In September 2002 he settled with the commission, which had alleged that he used improper accounting to produce "materially false and misleading" results while he ran Sunbeam. He agreed to pay $500,000 and to a permanent ban from serving as an officer or director of any public company. He neither admitted nor denied wrongdoing.

A month earlier, in August 2002, he had agreed to pay $15 million to settle a lawsuit brought by Sunbeam shareholders. Jim Chanos, a fund manager whose funds had bet against Sunbeam's shares, said the settlements showed why investors distrust wrongdoing by executives.

So the same words were being weighed on two different scales. In Dunlap's house, "decisive" described a trait. For the people at the other end of a cost cut, it meant a lost job. Later, the regulator asked about the numbers, not about the nerve it took to produce them. Which room a word is spoken in changes how much weight it is given.

[[lessons]]
$$**When someone names a trait a strength, ask who pays for it.** Suppose a store manager tells the team "I'm just target-driven." Asking who carries the weight of those targets, and what they have given up to meet them, shows you a side of the sentence the manager left out. The same behavior earns praise from one seat and complaints from another, and the difference is usually who gains and who bears the cost. Before you judge a boss, or yourself, put a question next to the word: who lost something because of this? Writing down the answer gives praise and criticism alike something solid to stand on, and it keeps the conversation fairer to both sides.$$

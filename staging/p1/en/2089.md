A widower in Linz sits down with a quill and a list. On it are eleven women, and he is writing to a baron to explain how he weighed each one. The man is the astronomer who worked out how the planets move. He is now stuck on a much smaller orbit: whom to marry.

[[story]]
Johannes Kepler's first wife died on 3 July 1611, after a long illness. He was left with children to raise, and for about two years he went looking for a second wife. Ideas like this are the kind Brian Christian and Tom Griffiths gather in *Algorithms to Live By*, a book on computer science and human decisions.

According to the 1911 Britannica, he later wrote to a Baron Stralendorf, going over the merits of eleven candidates for his hand. The woman he chose was a portionless orphan girl named Susanna Reuttinger. They married on 30 October 1613, and the marriage was described as a happy one.

Published accounts of the letter give the order of the search, and this is where it gets interesting. Number four was a tall, athletic woman in Linz, and Kepler was close to marrying her. Number five was Susanna. He nearly accepted her, was drawn back to number four, and then returned to number five and proposed. In those accounts, one candidate was rejected because he considered her short and fat and himself as thin as a stick. Another turned him down. Another withdrew because she was too young.

Mathematicians later gave this puzzle a name: the secretary problem. The cruel rule is that you see candidates one by one, you cannot go back, and you must say yes or no on the spot. In a 1989 article in *Statistical Science*, Thomas Ferguson traced the history of the problem and touched on Kepler along the way.

The solution is surprisingly clean. With eleven candidates, the best strategy is to let the first four go by without choosing anyone, only to learn what the field looks like. From the fifth on, you take the first candidate who is better than everyone you have seen so far. That gives you about a 40% chance of landing the very best one, which sounds low until you compare it with the one-in-eleven chance of picking blindly.

Count again. The fifth candidate is exactly where that rule would start saying yes. Kepler did not follow a formula, and no source says he did. But his list shows the same shape: a stretch of looking, then a commitment to the first person who beat everything before.

The part the formula cannot tell you is that Kepler also went back to someone he had passed over. The strict version of the problem forbids that, but a real person sometimes has a second chance. In this case it worked out.

[[lessons]]
$$**Spend the first stretch of a search only measuring, then commit to the first option that beats everything you have seen.** When you look for a flat, a supplier or a new hire, you can burn endless weeks comparing, or you can pick the first decent one and never learn what was out there. A fixed look-only period gives you a yardstick, so the later choice rests on evidence instead of mood. Decide how long you will only look before the search begins, and write the number down so the next promising option cannot talk you out of it.$$

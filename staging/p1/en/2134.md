A young man in his early twenties spends almost every day, and late into every night, inside video games. He has left college and lives with his parents. When a psychiatrist suggests he stop for a month, he answers that the games are the only thing that gives him any relief.

[[story]]
Anna Lembke, a psychiatrist at Stanford, tells this story in *Dopamine Nation* about one of her patients. He was bright and thoughtful, in his early twenties, and struggling with severe anxiety and depression. He had dropped out of college and moved back in with his parents. Most of each day, and late into each night, he played video games.

Twenty years earlier, Lembke says, she would have started with an antidepressant. This time she suggested a "dopamine fast" instead: one month with no screens at all, video games included.

He pushed back. Gaming, he said, was the only thing that gave him relief. So she explained the pleasure-pain balance and the neuroscience of addiction, including how repeated hits of reward can reset the brain's baseline so that you need the activity just to feel normal. He agreed to try.

A month later he came back and reported less anxiety and less depression. He said he felt better than he had in years. Lembke's reading is that the month gave his brain time to return to balance. She adds that cause and effect are hard to see while someone is still chasing the thing they use, because from inside the habit the game looks like the cure.

He did not give up gaming for good. He went back to it under rules he set himself. No more than two days a week, and no more than two hours on those days. No highly addictive games, meaning the ones he could not stop playing once he started. One laptop for gaming and a separate one for schoolwork. And only with friends, never with strangers, so that the games tied him to people.

Lembke says she holds herself to similar limits with her own habit, watching YouTube clips: two days a week, two hours a day, ideally with other people, and she avoids one channel about popping pimples altogether.

Look at how specific those rules are. Each one closes a particular door: the day, the length, the kind of game, the device, the company. None of them depends on a mood or a moment of willpower, because all of them were set ahead of any session. This is one patient's account, and no one can promise another person the same month or the same result.

[[lessons]]
$$**Give a habit a clean break first, then bring it back only under rules you wrote in advance.** Suppose someone checks the news all day and feels worse by evening: after a stretch without it, they could decide on one time slot, one device and one place for it, then notice how that feels. Rules fixed ahead of time are easier to keep than a promise to do better when the moment comes, because each of them answers one question before it comes up. You can then see how the habit fits into your life without it quietly setting the terms for the rest of your day.$$

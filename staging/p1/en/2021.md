Off the Italian island of Ischia, a few metres under the surface, strings of bubbles rise from the sea floor. It is not air. Volcanic vents are seeping carbon dioxide into the shallow water, and have been for decades. A biologist who wants to know what the future sea will hold can swim from clean water into the fizzing and watch who stays.

[[story]]
Elizabeth Kolbert went to these vents for *The Sixth Extinction*, off Castello Aragonese, a small island beside Ischia, with the marine biologists Jason Hall-Spencer and Maria Cristina Buia. Hall-Spencer's team had published their results in *Nature* in 2008, and the problem they were attacking was a practical one. Seawater absorbs carbon dioxide from the air, and the more it absorbs, the more acidic it becomes. The paper noted that atmospheric carbon dioxide would almost certainly reach double its pre-industrial level by 2100. By its estimate, the concentration of hydrogen ions in surface water, which is what acidity measures, had already risen by about 30 per cent since the early 1900s, and the pH might fall by as much as half a unit by 2100.

What would that do to living things? Almost all earlier work had been done in laboratory conditions, on isolated parts of the ecosystem, over short periods, with abrupt changes. The vents offered something else: a whole rocky shore, with all its species, exposed for years to gradually rising acidity along a natural slope. Away from the vents the water had a normal pH of 8.1 to 8.2. Closer in, the mean fell to 7.8 or 7.9, and the minimum dipped to 7.4 or 7.5.

Along that gradient the community changed. A typical rocky shore, crowded with animals and algae that build shells and crusts of calcium carbonate, gave way to one with no stony corals and far fewer sea urchins and coralline algae. In the most acidic spots, around a mean pH of 7.6, the shells of snails were dissolving during periods when the water was undersaturated in carbonate. The authors called it the first ecosystem-scale confirmation of what laboratory predictions had said about these groups.

Not every species lost out. Seagrass production was highest at the spot with that mean pH of 7.6. A suite of organisms tolerant of high carbon dioxide populated the vent sites, and the authors warned that acidification may favour highly invasive non-native algae. A separate study of the same vent area, looking at the seagrass blades themselves, found that at a mean pH of 7.7 the coralline algae were gone and the only shell-builders left were bryozoans. The total mass of calcium carbonate on the blades was 90 per cent lower than at pH 8.2.

The authors were careful about what this showed. The vents gave a first look at how shallow communities might change once sensitive species drop out. They did not show how the open ocean will behave, and they did not claim to.

[[lessons]]
$$**Test a change where all the parts that will react are present together.** A tank experiment can say how a single sea urchin copes with acid water, but not who disappears from a rocky shore, who takes the empty space and which of them is a nuisance. A restaurant that tests a cheaper supplier by tasting one sauce in the kitchen will miss what shows up only in the full dining room, such as slower prep, more waste or fewer repeat orders. Before you judge a change, ask which neighbours of the thing you tested will react and set up a trial that lets them.$$

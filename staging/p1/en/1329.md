In the cockpit of an airliner, the instruments keep reporting the same thing: off course. Then off course again, a little the other way. Nobody on board panics. The plane corrects, reads the next signal, corrects again, and lands where it was meant to land.

[[story]]
Jack Canfield uses that image in *The Success Principles* to describe how goals actually get reached. A plane flying to its destination is off course much of the time. It gets there anyway, because it keeps adjusting based on what its instruments tell it.

Canfield's point is that success depends less on getting things right the first time than on how you respond to feedback. That includes the unwelcome kind. The plane doesn't need a perfect heading. It needs readings, and a willingness to act on them.

People, he notes, often handle negative feedback in ways the plane never would. Some quit, as if one wrong heading proved the trip was impossible. Some get angry at the source and treat the messenger as the problem. Others simply ignore the signal and keep flying the same way. All three throw the information away.

The alternative is to go looking for it. Canfield recommends asking directly, and he offers a simple pair of questions. The first: "On a scale of 1 to 10, how would you rate our work together?" Whatever number comes back, follow with the second: "What would it take to make it a 10?"

The pairing is practical. The number gives a quick reading. The follow-up turns that reading into something you can adjust, and it invites the other person to be specific rather than polite. A number on its own is just a score. What it would take to make it a 10 is a heading correction.

Behind all this is a shift in how to see a miss. ##In Canfield's view, an unsuccessful attempt is information about what to adjust, not a verdict on the person who made it.##

A plane that treated every "off course" reading as failure would never land anywhere. The one that arrives isn't the one that was never wrong. It's the one that kept reading the instruments and kept adjusting.

[[lessons]]
$$**Off course is the normal state.** The plane in Canfield's image is off course much of the flight and still lands. Drifting isn't the problem. The time between noticing the drift and correcting it is what decides where you end up. Nobody expects the plane to fly a perfect line, only to keep checking and adjusting.$$

$$**Feedback mostly has to be asked for.** People rarely volunteer honest criticism. Canfield's two questions, how would you rate this from 1 to 10, and what would it take to make it a 10, make it easy for someone to tell you what they'd otherwise keep to themselves.$$

$$**Three ways to waste a signal.** Quitting, blaming the source and ignoring the message all feel like responses, but none of them uses what the feedback says. Each leaves you flying the same heading. The useful response is duller: note the reading and adjust.$$

[[reflect]]
- When a recent attempt fell short, did you treat it as a verdict or as a reading on the instruments?
- Whose honest feedback have you been avoiding asking for?
- Which of the three reactions, quitting, blaming or ignoring, do you reach for first?

[[open]]
Two cousins, both rulers of enormous empires, write to each other in English and sign with their first names. Their telegrams fly back and forth through the night. Twice, a message is already on the wire while the answer to the previous one is still being typed, and neither man has read the other's latest words.

[[story]]
Between 29 July and 1 August 1914, Kaiser Wilhelm II of Germany and Tsar Nicholas II of Russia exchanged ten telegrams in English. Wilhelm signed his "Willy" and Nicholas signed his "Nicky". Germany printed them in a pamphlet, the German White Book, once war had begun, to justify its own position.

The first came at 1 a.m. on 29 July. Nicholas wrote that he expected to be pushed into extreme measures that would lead to war, and he asked Wilhelm to stop his ally Austria-Hungary from going too far. Forty-five minutes later Wilhelm's telegram went out. He had written it the day before, and it offered to press Austria toward a direct understanding with Russia. The two messages crossed.

That evening Wilhelm suggested that Russia stay out of the conflict. At 8:20 p.m. Nicholas thanked him, but pointed out that the official message from the German ambassador had a much harder tone than Wilhelm's own, and he proposed sending the Austro-Serbian quarrel to the Hague conference. Wilhelm never took up the idea.

Early on 30 July Wilhelm warned that Russian mobilisation would endanger his mediation, and he put the choice between peace and war on Nicholas. On 31 July the telegrams crossed again. Wilhelm wrote that his mediation was nearly pointless and asked Russia to halt its military measures. Nicholas wrote that halting them was technically impossible, because Austria was mobilising, and he promised that Russian troops would do nothing provocative while talks went on. At one point Nicholas pulled back from a general mobilisation order, and his advisers persuaded him to keep it.

On 1 August Nicholas asked for a guarantee that Germany's mobilisation did not mean war. Wilhelm replied that no answer had come from Russia, that he had therefore mobilised, and that Nicholas should order his troops not to cross the frontier. Germany declared war on Russia that day.

Read in order, the telegrams are personal, quick and polite, and each man asks the other to stop something. Yet the thing being asked for was not held in either man's hand alone. Nicholas said in writing that his army's preparations could not simply be halted. Wilhelm's own army had its timetable too. The channel between the two was as warm and fast as a channel can be, and the decision it was meant to move sat somewhere else, running on its own clock.

[[lessons]]
$$**Warm words between two leaders cannot stop what runs on its own timetable, so find out early who can still stop it and what stopping would take.** A manager may promise a client a calm talk with the vendor, while the vendor's shipment is already on a truck and cannot be recalled. The friendly message feels like progress because it arrives quickly and sounds sincere, yet it moves nothing that has already been set in motion elsewhere. Before you rely on a conversation to change an outcome, ask where the actual lever is, who holds it, and how many hours it still has.$$

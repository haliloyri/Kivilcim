[[story]]
Among hungry shoppers who had no list, 51 percent of the food they bought was unplanned. Among shoppers who had just eaten a muffin, it was 34 percent. That gap comes from a field experiment by Daniel Gilbert, Michael Gill and Timothy Wilson, published in 2002, which tested what hunger does to our picture of future meals. Gilbert is the author of *Stumbling on Happiness*.

The researchers set up their table at the entrance of Central Market in Austin. A hundred and thirty-five shoppers took part, and 111 were analyzed, aged 12 to 80. Shoppers who had brought a list from home were turned away. The others wrote down what they planned to buy that day, and some, as if it were a minor detail, were handed a copy of their list. Then half were asked to eat a muffin of about 100 grams, and half shopped hungry. When they left, their receipts were used to code each item as planned or unplanned by a judge who did not know the groups.

The effect appeared only among shoppers without lists. Lists changed it. Hungry ones spent 49 percent of their money on unplanned food, against 30 percent for the muffin-eaters. Among those given lists, the difference was small and not statistically significant: 36 percent of items for the hungry and 28 percent for the sated. Only about half of the listful group said they had used the list. The pattern held anyway.

The authors' explanation runs like this. To predict how you will enjoy a meal later, you first picture the food with no date attached. You respond to that picture with your feelings right now, so a hungry person finds it delicious. Only afterwards, and with effort, do you ask when you will eat it, and that correction is easy to skip. It takes effort. A list helps because it holds clues about time, such as which items are for early in the week and which are for a Friday dinner.

A laboratory study showed the same mechanism. Sixty-one female undergraduates predicted how much they would enjoy a taste of spaghetti the next morning or the next evening. Some did this while also pressing a button whenever they heard a particular three-tone sequence. For those busy students, the forecast followed their current hunger and ignored the time of day. For the others it was the reverse: evening spaghetti sounded better than morning spaghetti, and hunger did not matter.

[[lessons]]
$$**Do not let today's appetite make next week's decision; use a written list that ties each choice to a date.** Agreeing in an energetic meeting to take on next month's work, or planning next week's dinners while starving, means deciding for a self who is not the one who will live with the result. Write the list when you are calm and fed, and note beside each line when it will actually be needed. That lets you separate what you feel now from what you will need then, and pick the item that fits that day instead of the one that appeals at this moment.$$

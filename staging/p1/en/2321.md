High above the Earth, a man floats alone in a small capsule after more than a day in orbit. The lights that tell him how the machine is doing have gone wrong, and the automatic controls cannot be trusted. Soon he must bring himself home through the atmosphere using his eyes and one wristwatch.

[[story]]
Gordon Cooper is another of the seven astronauts in Tom Wolfe's *The Right Stuff*, and his flight on 15 May 1963 closed Project Mercury. His capsule, Faith 7, was to circle the Earth 22 times, longer than any earlier American flight. For most of it the mission went to plan.

Late in the flight the capsule's electrical systems began to fail one after another. According to one published account, the trouble set in around the nineteenth orbit. The automatic control system, which was meant to hold the capsule in the right position and fire the retrorockets, could no longer be relied on. The retrorockets are the small engines that slow a spacecraft enough for it to drop out of orbit. Fire them at the wrong angle and the capsule will not come down where it should.

That left Cooper with the manual option. He lined the capsule up himself, using the stars and the horizon as his guide. John Glenn, speaking by radio, counted him down to the moment of firing. Cooper later said he used his wristwatch to time the retrofire burn, while his eyes judged the attitude of the capsule. It was as old-fashioned as navigation gets: a clock, a view and a steady hand.

Faith 7 came down in the ocean on 16 May, close to the aircraft carrier Kearsarge, around four miles away. A flight that had lost its automatic controls ended with a landing close to the ship. What saved it was the oldest part of the system, a trained person who could still do the job by hand.

What stands out is the order of dependence. The designers had added automation to make flights easier and more accurate, and for most of the flight it did. But they had also left a manual route in the capsule, and a pilot who knew how to use it. When the clever layer disappeared, the basic layer was still there to carry the load.

There is a quieter point in the countdown. Cooper did not act alone: Glenn kept time with him. A person flying by hand still needs a second pair of eyes and a plan for who says what. Skill and teamwork together turned a failing system into a safe return.

[[lessons]]
$$**Keep the manual version of every automated task alive, so that you can finish the job when the system fails.** Cooper brought the capsule home because the old, hand-flown skills had never been removed. At work, a team that depends on one tool, one report or one script is exposed in the same way when it breaks. Choose the most important automated task you own and ask who could do it by hand tomorrow, with what, and in how long. Practise that route a few times while nothing is wrong, and write down the steps, so that on the bad day nobody has to invent them.$$

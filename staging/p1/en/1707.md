Before I tell you today's story, I want you to do one thing. Think of someone who got on your nerves this week. The driver who cut you off, the friend who left you on read, the colleague who showed up to the meeting with nothing prepared. Picture their face. Got it? Hold on to it. We'll need them later.

[[story]]
Stephen Covey tells this one about himself, in *The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People*.

It's a Sunday morning in New York, and Covey is on the subway. The car is quiet in that nice, lazy Sunday way. People are reading the paper. A few are staring out the window. Some have their eyes closed.

At one stop, a man gets on with his kids. And just like that, the calm is gone. The children are loud. They're running up and down the car, throwing things, even yanking at people's newspapers. Everyone is bothered.

And the father? He does nothing. He sits down right next to Covey, closes his eyes, and seems completely unaware of what's going on.

Covey admits he could hardly believe it. How can a parent just let his kids run wild like that and not take any responsibility at all? You can feel the whole car thinking the same thing.

Finally Covey turns to him. He uses what he considers a remarkably patient, controlled voice, and says something like: *Sir, your children are really disturbing a lot of people. Could you maybe keep them in check a little more?*

The man opens his eyes. He looks around as if he's only now noticing where he is. Then he answers quietly. Yes, Covey's right, he should probably do something.

> "We just came from the hospital. Their mother died about an hour ago."

He says he doesn't know what to think. And he suspects the kids don't know how to deal with it either.

Covey describes what happened inside him in that moment. Suddenly he saw the whole scene differently. And because he saw it differently, he thought differently, felt differently, acted differently. The irritation was simply gone. He didn't have to work at being calm. He was filled with this man's pain. *Your wife just died? I'm so sorry. Can you tell me about it? Is there anything I can do?*

And here's the part I love.

##The kids were still yelling. Nothing in that subway car had changed except the way Covey was looking at it.##

[[lessons]]
$$**We don't react to what people do. We react to the lens we're wearing.** Covey was looking at the same kids and hearing the same noise. The only thing that changed was the story in his head, from "careless father" to "grieving father." He didn't force himself to calm down. His view shifted, and his feelings and behavior followed on their own. If you want to change how you treat someone, start with how you're seeing them.$$

$$**Everyone in that car had written the same story, and nobody had asked.** We fill in the blanks with guesses, and usually with the worst possible guess. It took one sentence to get the truth. And that sentence wasn't an accusation. It was a question.$$

$$**This isn't a story about keeping your mouth shut.** Covey wasn't wrong. The kids really were disrupting everyone, and if he hadn't spoken up, he'd never have known. The point isn't silence. It's holding your judgment a little more loosely and leaving room for the other person's side. Not every situation hides a tragedy like this one. But remembering that there might be something you don't know will at least change the first thing you say.$$

[[reflect]]
Go back to the person you pictured at the start.

- What story did you write about them? "Rude"? "Lazy"? "Doesn't care"?
- How much of that story do you actually know, and how much is guesswork?
- Have you ever just asked them, "Hey, is everything okay?"

And flip it around for a second.

&&Have you ever been the father on that train, judged on a bad day by people who had no idea what you were going through? What would you have wanted someone to say?&&

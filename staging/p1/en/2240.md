[[open]]
A professor at a business school hears the same sentence from student after student: "I'm an auditory learner, so I prefer podcasts." They say it calmly, as a settled fact about themselves, the way someone might state a blood type. It sounds like self-knowledge, and nobody asks for evidence.

[[story]]
The idea took hold in the 1970s. Many American schools at the time ran like assembly lines, with students treated as interchangeable parts, and a new view promised a fix: children struggled because lessons did not match how they learn best. Some were verbal learners, some visual, some auditory, some kinesthetic. Find the style, teach to it, and the struggle would fade. It was a tidy promise, and it spread fast.

Adam Grant's *Hidden Potential* takes the idea on. He notes that, according to a figure he cites, 89 percent of teachers around the world believe in matching instruction to learning styles. Students have told him they like podcasts because they are auditory learners, as if the label settled the matter.

In 2008, four psychologists, Harold Pashler, Mark McDaniel, Doug Rohrer and Robert Bjork, reviewed the evidence in the journal *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*. The right test is simple to describe. Sort learners by their preferred style, randomly assign each to a lesson that matches it or one that does not, then give everyone the same exam. If matching works, visual learners should do best with visual lessons and verbal learners with verbal ones.

Very few studies had been run that way. According to published summaries of the review, all but one of those found no benefit from matching. The authors concluded there was no adequate evidence base to justify using learning-style assessments in general educational practice. Grant adds that, across controlled experiments and semester-long studies, neither students nor adults scored better when teaching or study habits fit their preferred style.

The reviewers called the gap between the idea's popularity and its evidence "striking and disturbing."

Notice what the research did not say. It did not say students have no preferences, and it did not tell anyone to stop enjoying podcasts. It asked a narrower question: when a person studies the way they prefer, do they actually learn more? The answer from the best-designed tests was no. A preference is a fact about comfort, and comfort and results turned out to be two separate measurements.

Grant's own concern runs further. Treating a preference as a limit can keep people inside what is familiar, and avoiding unfamiliar techniques can quietly stall growth. A student who only ever listens never practises reading with a pen in hand, and the label becomes the reason for it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Check a comfortable habit against a result before you build your routine around it.** Someone who is sure they learn best by watching videos at double speed may find, when a colleague quizzes them a week later, that little stuck. A short self-test after any study session would show which method is working, and it costs only a few minutes of an evening. Over a month, those little scores add up to a record that your feelings about the method cannot argue with. Treat "this is how I learn" as a hypothesis to try, and you stay free to pick up the unfamiliar method when it works better.$$

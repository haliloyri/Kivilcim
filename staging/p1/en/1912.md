Two people walk down a corridor side by side, not speaking much. Behind them, a door has just slammed. Nobody is giving a lecture and nobody is asking for an explanation yet. One of them is an adult, the other a child, and the adult has decided that the explanation can wait.

[[story]]
Most of us argue in the wrong order. When a child melts down or a colleague snaps, we reach for the reasons first: here is why that was not acceptable, here is what you should do instead. Bruce Perry, the psychiatrist who wrote *What Happened to You?* with Oprah Winfrey, teaches a different order. He calls it a sequence of engagement, and it has three steps: regulate, relate, reason.

The first step is regulate. A person who is flooded with stress cannot take in an argument, however sound it is. So before any talking about what went wrong, the body has to settle. In talks Perry has given, he says this starts with the adult. A frustrated, angry adult will never calm a child, so the first thing to regulate is yourself.

The second step is relate. In the same talks he puts it plainly: before you can reason with somebody, you have to be connected to them. Connection is the road that the reasoning will later travel on. Without it, the best explanation in the world arrives at a closed door.

Only the third step is reason. By then the child or adult is calm enough, and close enough to the other person, to hear a reason, think about what happened and consider what to do next. This is where lessons, consequences and problem solving belong. They have not been dropped. They have been moved to the place where they can work.

Perry also describes how the first two steps can look in practice. He prefers to sit or walk parallel to the child instead of facing them, and to do something patterned and repetitive together, such as walking or singing. A face-to-face talk can feel like an interrogation, whereas side by side the pressure drops, and the steady rhythm helps the body settle.

How long each step takes varies. A handout used by practitioners in the UK notes that moving through the sequence can take minutes or weeks, depending on what the child needs. That detail protects against a common mistake: treating the three steps as a script with a timer, when they are a direction of travel, and going back a step is normal.

The model gives adults a plain test for any tense moment: which of the three steps are we on? Most arguments go wrong because someone asks for step three while everyone is still on step one.

[[lessons]]
$$**Calm your own body first, connect second, and offer reasons only after both are done.** When a teenager slams a door or a coworker snaps in a meeting, the logical case you carry is probably right and also probably useless for the next ten minutes. Take a slow breath, lower your voice, offer a walk or a glass of water, and say that you are not going anywhere. Once the other person has settled and feels you are on their side, your reasons, said once and kindly, will land where they would have bounced off a minute before, and you will not need to repeat them.$$

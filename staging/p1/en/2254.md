A catcher cannot throw the way he used to, and a club asks him to play a position he has never stood at, for less than a million dollars. The ball he was trained to receive and fire back is no longer the job. The job is a corner of the infield where throwing matters far less.

[[story]]
Scott Hatteberg's move to Oakland is one of the most unusual moves by the Athletics, the club at the centre of Michael Lewis's *Moneyball*. Hatteberg had caught for the Boston Red Sox for seven seasons and hit .267/.357/.414 over that time. In his last Boston season he ruptured a nerve in his elbow and tore a joint capsule, and he needed surgery. The injury weakened his throwing and put his career as a catcher in danger.

Boston traded him to Colorado on 19 December. Two days later Colorado declined to offer him salary arbitration, and he became a free agent. Oakland signed him to a one-year contract with a 950,000-dollar base salary plus incentives. Hatteberg was 32 and had never played first base in his life.

What Oakland looked at was neither the injury nor the position he lacked. It looked at one number, his high on-base percentage. Billy Beane said it was the skill management believed correlated most often with runs scored, and one of the cheapest skills available to a small-market club like Oakland. For a catcher the throw is essential. At first base it matters much less. Hatteberg's damaged arm was a serious problem behind the plate, and at the new spot the weight of that problem shrank considerably.

Infield coach Ron Washington taught him the position. In 2002 Hatteberg hit .280/.374/.433, with career highs of 15 home runs and 61 runs batted in. On 4 September 2002, in the game that gave Oakland its 20th straight win, he hit a walk-off home run against Kansas City.

The club kept him for four seasons in all. Over those four years he earned 7.05 million dollars, hit 49 home runs and held a .355 on-base percentage. His mark in Boston had been .357, so the skill Oakland paid for survived the move to a new position and a new team.

What stands out is what the decision was built on. The elbow could no longer carry a catcher, but it could still carry a man who draws walks and hits. The club found the spot where the missing piece mattered least and used the rest as it was. A salary under a million dollars made it a calculated try instead of a gamble.

[[lessons]]
$$**When someone is struggling, ask what they can still do well and rebuild their role around that.** A warehouse worker with a hurt back may not be able to lift heavy boxes, yet he is as good as ever at stock counts, order tracking and showing newcomers the routine. If you see him as a problem to remove, you lose that. If you see him as someone who would be useful at a different desk, you keep his years of knowledge, and you only need to find the one thing he must learn in the new role and someone to teach it.$$

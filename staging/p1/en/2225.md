[[story]]
When Hadrian died in July 138, he left a condition behind. His chosen successor, Antoninus Pius, had been adopted that February on the understanding that he would in turn adopt two boys, Marcus Aurelius and Lucius Verus, and make them his heirs. Marcus moved into the household of the man who would be emperor for the next twenty-three years. The chronological table in Haines's edition lists him as Caesar the following year, 139, the same year Fronto became his tutor. From then on the young man had a palace on one side and a classroom on the other.

What that household was like, we can read in Marcus's own words. In his letters to his tutor Fronto, written around 144 or 145 and preserved in Haines's translation, he describes an ordinary day. He slept a little late with a cold, then read some of Cato on farming, then went to greet his father. He gargled with honey water, and he attended Antoninus at a sacrifice. At lunch he ate a bit of bread while others devoured beans, onions and herrings full of roe. In the afternoon the family worked hard at the grape harvest, got a good sweat and were merry. Nothing in the letter mentions guards or ceremony.

Decades later, in the *Meditations*, Marcus thanks the gods for that father. He says Antoninus took away his pride and vanity and showed him that a prince can live at court without a troop of bodyguards, extraordinary clothes, torches and statues. A ruler could scale himself back almost to the condition of a private man, Marcus writes, and still act with full authority in public business.

He adds a long portrait of how Antoninus worked. The emperor listened to anyone who had something useful to say for the common good. He put a stop to acclamations and flattery. He kept an account of public expenses and bore the criticism he got for being so strict. He was not praised as a brilliant orator but as a mature, sound man who could not stand to be flattered. After his bad headaches he came back fresh to his usual work.

One scene from the letters stands out against that portrait. Marcus once read a piece of writing aloud to his father, and he tells Fronto he could not help doing so. He describes a young man who wanted the emperor in the room.

[[lessons]]
$$**Watch how the person you admire spends an ordinary afternoon.** A new manager learns more about running a team from sitting through one routine Tuesday of a respected director, with its small refusals of ceremony and its patient listening, than from a stack of leadership advice. Marcus absorbed a way of holding power lightly mostly by being nearby while it was practised, and he could still describe it in detail years later. When you pick a model, notice what they decline to do, because those refusals are easier to copy than a style. Then ask the person one plain question about a choice you saw them make.$$

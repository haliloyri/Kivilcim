Same furniture website, same sofas. The only difference sits in the background of the page. On one version, fluffy clouds drift across a blue sky. On the other, coins lie scattered on green. Visitors look at the sofas, not at the backdrop.

[[story]]
Robert Cialdini's *Pre-Suasion* argues that the moments just before you make a request can shape the answer you get. One study often used to show this comes from Naomi Mandel and Eric Johnson, who published it in the *Journal of Consumer Research* in 2002. They prepared the web page of a furniture store in two versions.

The two pages were identical except for the background. One showed a blue sky with fluffy clouds. The other showed coins on a green field. The store and the sofas on offer were the same on both.

The choices included a sofa that was more comfortable but more expensive, and one that was cheaper but less comfortable. People who had seen the cloud page leaned toward the comfortable, pricier sofa. People who had seen the coin page leaned toward the cheaper one.

What makes the result striking is that nobody wrote a word on the background. The page never said "think about comfort" or "save money". It held only a picture. The explanation is simple: clouds bring softness and comfort to mind, coins bring price. At the moment of choosing, whichever yardstick is closest to hand is the one you are most likely to measure the sofas with.

According to the summary of the paper, the effect showed up in experts and novices alike, though it worked through different routes. In novices, the picture changed how much they searched outside the page, and the choice followed. In experts, the outside search stayed the same and the choice shifted anyway.

Notice how little the experiment needed. None of the sofas changed: not their prices, not their cushions, not the store itself. The only thing that moved was the picture behind them, and that picture had nothing to do with furniture. An argument gives people a reason to weigh things differently. A backdrop gives them nothing to evaluate and still tips the scale.

This is where Cialdini's interest lies. What stands on the screen, in the room or in the conversation just before the persuasive words matters as much as the words. The only difference between the cloud page and the coin page was which question was waiting in the visitor's mind: is this comfortable, or is this cheap? The sofas stayed the same, but the question they were asked changed.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you ask someone to decide, look at what you have put in front of them.** Someone presenting a budget proposal in a meeting who opens with the savings table starts a conversation about price, while opening with the customer satisfaction chart starts one about quality, and the same proposal gets weighed differently. You can also check your own choices by noticing which yardstick happens to be on your screen at that moment. That lets you build your next presentation on purpose and ask yourself in which light you are making your own decisions. Checking what sits on the screen just before a rushed choice is often a small enough first step.$$

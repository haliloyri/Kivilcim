About two million standardized tests, all taken by Danish schoolchildren. Line the scores up by the hour and a pattern appears: the later in the day the test, the lower the results. Same children, same kind of test, and the clock alone was pulling scores down. Then the researchers spotted something that pushed them back up, and it was surprisingly small.

[[story]]
Daniel Pink describes the Danish data in *When*, as part of his case that the early-to-mid afternoon is a real and measurable low point. He calls it the trough: the stretch of the day when attention and mood dip for most people.

The schoolchildren were one piece of evidence. Their test scores declined as the day went on. But the same data held a second finding. When children had a break of 20 to 30 minutes before a test, their scores went up. The boost more than offset the afternoon decline.

In other words, the slide was real, and a short break before the test beat it.

Pink doesn't rest the case on classrooms. He cites a study at Duke Medical Center that looked at anesthesia. Errors and adverse events were considerably more likely in procedures that started in the 3 p.m. hour than in procedures that started in the morning. These are trained specialists doing work they know well, in a setting built around safety. The afternoon trough still showed up in the numbers.

That changes how the afternoon slump looks. It is easy to treat it as a matter of willpower, something you push through with enough discipline. Pink's evidence suggests it is closer to a pattern in the day itself, one that shows up in children and in operating rooms alike.

##The fix Pink finds isn't grinding harder; it's a well-timed pause.##

He recommends short breaks and naps to counter the trough. The ideal nap, in his account, is about 10 to 20 minutes long. That's short enough to avoid slipping into deeper sleep, which is what leaves people groggy when they wake.

Then he adds a trick of his own, which he calls the nappuccino. Drink a cup of coffee. Then lie down straight away. Caffeine takes roughly 25 minutes to take effect, so set an alarm for about 25 minutes. You nap while the caffeine is on its way, and wake just as it kicks in.

It sounds almost too simple to take seriously, which is part of Pink's point. The afternoon costs something measurable, in scores and in errors. The remedies he describes cost very little: moving the demanding task earlier, taking a proper break before it, or timing a short nap and a coffee so they work together.

[[lessons]]
$$**The afternoon dip is measurable, not a character flaw.** Anesthesia errors at Duke rose in the 3 p.m. hour, and Danish test scores slid as the day went on. When trained doctors and ordinary schoolchildren show the same pattern, pushing through with willpower looks like the wrong tool. The afternoon becomes a scheduling problem as much as a personal one.$$

$$**A break is a repair, not an indulgence.** In the Danish data, a 20 to 30 minute break before a test raised scores by more than the afternoon had taken away. The pause wasn't time lost from work. It was what made the next piece of work go better.$$

$$**Timing coffee and a nap together avoids grogginess.** Pink's nappuccino works because caffeine takes about 25 minutes to arrive. Drink first, sleep briefly, and the alarm and the caffeine land at the same moment, before deeper sleep can set in.$$

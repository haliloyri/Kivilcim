At six in the morning a special train pulls out with mattresses laid inside to soften its rocking. Its passenger is a wounded President. At the far end of the journey stands a cottage by the sea, and between the station and its door lies a stretch of rough road that local residents fear will be too much for him.

[[story]]
Candice Millard's *Destiny of the Republic* follows James Garfield through his last weeks, and one episode in them is the journey to the New Jersey shore. On 6 September 1881, about two months after he was shot, Garfield left the White House for Elberon, near Long Branch, in the hope that the sea air would help his recovery. A twenty-room seaside mansion, the Francklyn Cottage, had been offered to the family by Charles Francklyn, an executive of the Cunard Line.

The train that carried him was a special one, supplied by the Pennsylvania Railroad and fitted with mattresses to reduce the rocking. According to a New Jersey history account, it left the White House at six in the morning and reached Elberon Station at 1:09 in the afternoon.

The trouble was the last stretch. Elberon residents feared that the half-mile carriage ride from the station to the cottage, on a rough road, would be too much for him. So they decided not to use the road at all. According to the same history, about two thousand men, railroad workers and local volunteers, laid more than half a mile of new track along what is now Lincoln Avenue, from the station to within a hundred yards of the Atlantic, in less than twenty-four hours. Much of the work was done in the dark, and another account says the crews worked by torchlight.

When the train arrived, twenty or more men pushed his car along the new spur to the cottage door. The National Park Service records that he reportedly said it was good to be there.

He did not recover. Garfield died at Elberon on 19 September 1881, at 10:35 in the evening, with his wife and daughter at his side. The track was taken up afterwards, and a spike from it was later acquired by George Zabriskie Gray.

Consider what the effort addressed. The track could not heal him. It answered one specific problem, the final jolting half-mile of a sick man's day, and railroad workers and local volunteers built it in under a day. The spur served one journey and was then taken up. Its purpose, as far as the accounts show, was to make that single arrival gentler than it would otherwise have been.

[[lessons]]
$$**Find the last stretch of someone's hard journey, where one small friction hurts most, and remove that one.** You cannot fix a person's illness, a colleague's grief or a relative's exhaustion, and insisting on a fix will often leave you offering nothing at all. What you can usually do is ask where the next hour will be hardest for them: the walk from the car, the form at the desk, the meeting they must attend alone. Take that one obstacle out of the way, and let the help be temporary and plain, like a spur laid for a single journey. Small, specific help is easier to accept than a promise to make everything better.$$

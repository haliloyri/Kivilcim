A captain sits in the control room of a nuclear submarine, bored, turning his flashlight over in his hands. The reactor is shut down on purpose and everyone is busy with a drill. He wants the room to feel a little more urgent, so he passes a short instruction to the officer on watch, and a sailor at the helm goes very still.

[[story]]
In *Turn the Ship Around!*, L. David Marquet tells of a drill aboard USS Santa Fe, the submarine he had been sent to command at the last minute. The engineer had shut down the reactor deliberately, to see whether his department could find and fix a simulated fault. The alarm came over the line: "Conn, maneuvering, reactor scram!"

With the reactor off, the ship was running on the EPM, a small auxiliary electric motor. It can only push the submarine at low speed, and it drains the battery. The boat was coming shallow so it could run its diesel engine and charge that battery.

Troubleshooting dragged on. Marquet grew bored, fiddled with his flashlight, and decided the drill needed urgency. At "ahead 2/3" the battery's amp-hour meter clicks almost without pause, a constant reminder that time is running out. So he had the officer of the deck, Lieutenant Commander Bill Greene, the senior department head aboard, raise the speed from "ahead 1/3" to "ahead 2/3."

Greene gave the order. Nothing happened. The helmsman squirmed in his seat, then called over his shoulder: "Captain, there is no ahead 2/3 on the EPM!"

Marquet admitted he had made a mistake. On the submarines he had served in before, the EPM had that setting. On Santa Fe it had only 1/3. He praised the helmsman, then asked Greene quietly whether he had known the setting did not exist. "Yes, Captain, I did." Marquet asked why he had given the order anyway. Greene answered, "Because you told me to."

Look at how far the sentence travelled. It went from the captain to the senior officer on watch to a junior sailor, and the only one who stopped it was the one who physically could not carry it out. Greene knew the order was impossible and passed it on, because it came from the top. The drill had been built to test the engineer's department, and it ended up testing the captain.

Marquet's conclusion was that his single casual order had pulled the crew back into top-down command. He vowed never to give another order. From then on he wanted people to come to him and say "I intend to…"

[[lessons]]
$$**The higher your rank, the less a casual remark stays casual.** People below you rarely hear a passing thought as a passing thought, and they often assume you know something they do not. A manager who muses in a team chat that a weekly report might be dropped can find it gone by the afternoon, with nobody asking whether it mattered. Say out loud which kind of sentence you are speaking, a guess or a decision, and invite someone to correct you. Then the person who sees the missing 2/3 setting has permission to say so before the ship stalls.$$

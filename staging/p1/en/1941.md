[[open]]
In a museum storeroom, small lumps of clay sit in rows. Each one carries an agreement in wedge-shaped script: a debt, a field, a shipment of barley. Nobody wrote these up in a book afterwards. A family pressed the record of its own business straight into clay.

[[story]]
The family is the Egibis of ancient Babylon, and the archive that carries their name is about 1,700 clay tablets spanning five generations. They date from roughly 600 to 482 BCE. According to the Assyriologist Caroline Waerzeggers, it is the largest private archive from the Neo-Babylonian period known to date.

In 1876 George Smith acquired the bulk of the archive for the British Museum. That purchase is the subject of a 2014 paper by Panayotov and Wunsch. So this is no legend: what survives is contracts, loan records and partnerships. A debt made by word of mouth vanishes when the debtor and the lender die, whereas a debt pressed into clay outlives both of them.

Older writing often calls the Egibis a "banking house". Waerzeggers questions that label and argues that banking was not among the family's activities. In her account they first made their fortune in the commodity trade, then expanded into the food business, tax farming and the management of farmland. Credit to farmers was part of the mix. The label matters here. Calling them "bankers" suggests people who only lent money, but the picture that emerges is one of enterprise, in which trade, food and land were tied together.

Look at the spread. If drought hits a field, the grain trade suffers, but tax farming and food processing run on different logic. The family did not let its money depend on a single channel. A 2023 article on the tax-farming texts of Marduk-nasir-apli, who headed the family in the third generation, confirms that this side of the business was real work with paperwork behind it.

What remains of all this is as much the record as the business. Each agreement was written, sealed and kept. Unlike paper, clay survives fire and damp, so centuries later a reader can follow, line by line, who gave what to whom. Five generations of one family passed those records on to each other. A younger member could read what an older one had done from the tablets rather than from memory. It was a plain arrangement that made the continuity of the business independent of any one person's lifetime.

That is why the Egibi archive reads less like the story of an ancient fortune than the story of how a fortune was tracked.

[[lessons]]
$$**Write every agreement down, keep the paper, and let your money come from more than one source.** The Egibis worked in trade, food, taxes and farming, and they kept records of their dealings across five generations. If you put a loan, a partnership or a rental deal in a short note instead of leaving it as a spoken promise, nobody has to rely on memory later, and if a dispute comes up you have a text to talk from. A few separate sources of income also shrink the damage when one door closes. Together they leave you, in an unexpected year, with both proof and a fallback.$$

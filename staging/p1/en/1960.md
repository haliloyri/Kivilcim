The torches burn, but they do not light the way; they only show how thick the dark is. The dust rolls back on itself in the closed passage and blows into the faces of the people who stirred it up. A traveller who has just come through mud now feels his body shudder, and he did not decide to.

[[story]]
Seneca, in Letter 57, has to get from Baiae back to Naples, and he would rather avoid another trip by sea. So he finds it easy to persuade himself that a storm is raging. The road turns out to be so deep in mud that he might as well have made a voyage anyway. He compares the day to an athlete's fate: the anointing at the start was mud, and the sprinkling of sand that follows came in the Naples tunnel.

That tunnel, today called the Crypta Neapolitana, runs for more than 700 metres, and according to modern histories it was cut around 37 BC for Agrippa. Seneca gives no measurements, only his impression. No place could be longer than that prison, he says, and the torches do not let you see in the darkness, only see the darkness. Even if there were light, the dust would smother it. Dust is unpleasant in the open air; shut in without ventilation, it rolls back on itself and blows into the faces of those who raised it. So the traveller fights mud and dust, two opposite nuisances, on the same road on the same day.

The gloom, though, gives him something to think about. He feels a thrill, a change in himself caused by the novelty and unpleasantness of the place, but no fear. He is quick to add that he is not talking about himself: he is far from perfect, not even a man of middling qualities. He means a man over whom fortune has lost her control, and even such a man will feel a shiver and change colour.

For some feelings, Seneca says, no courage can help. Nature reminds courage how fragile it is. A brave man will frown when the outlook is forbidding, shudder at sudden apparitions and grow dizzy when he stands at the edge of a high drop. That is not fear but a natural reaction that reason cannot rout. Some men who would gladly shed their own blood cannot bear the sight of another's, and some collapse at the sight of a fresh wound.

At the first glimpse of restored daylight, his good spirits return without forethought or command. Walking on, he starts to think how foolish it is to fear certain things more than others when all of them end the same way. What difference does it make whether a watchtower or a mountain falls on us? None, he decides. Still, some people will fear the mountain more, because fear looks to the cause of the harm and not to the harm itself.

[[lessons]]
$$**The first shudder belongs to your body, and the test of your character begins with what you do next.** Someone whose hands tremble before speaking to a full room can read that as proof of cowardice and back out, or treat it like weather and start the first sentence. As Seneca found in the tunnel, you cannot talk this reaction down with reason, but you do choose the name you give it. A person who reads the tremor as information rather than a verdict goes into the next room calmer, and the people around them borrow some of that calm.$$

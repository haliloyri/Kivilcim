A man's notebooks are full of chemical experiments, page after page of them, dated and numbered. Then the entries simply stop. For almost two years the pages that should hold his chemistry stay empty, because his mind is somewhere else entirely.

[[story]]
In early 1684 the astronomer Edmond Halley argued with Christopher Wren and Robert Hooke about the motion of the planets. None of them could prove what sort of force would keep a planet in its orbit. Halley went to Isaac Newton and asked for his view. Newton replied that he had already worked out the answer but could not find his papers.

In November 1684 Newton sent Halley a manuscript of nine pages, *De motu corporum in gyrum*, deriving Kepler's laws of planetary motion from a force that weakens with the square of the distance. Halley was impressed enough to return and urge that the Royal Society receive more of the work. Newton agreed to write it out properly, and that is when he stopped doing anything else.

His chemical notebooks contain no entries between May 1684 and April 1686. Historians read this as a sign that he set his other pursuits aside while he concentrated on the book. The text of the first book was presented to the Royal Society in late April 1686.

Then came trouble. Hooke claimed priority for part of the idea, and the dispute was bitter enough that Newton threatened to withhold the third book. Halley persuaded him to let the work go on. On 30 June 1686 the Society's president, Samuel Pepys, licensed it for publication.

There was a money problem too. The Society had just spent its book budget on a natural history of fishes, so it could not pay for the printing. Halley paid the costs himself, at his own financial risk. The *Principia* appeared in July 1687, about three years after Halley's first approach.

Count the time involved. From Halley's first approach to the printed book was about three years, and roughly two of them were a stretch of near-total focus. The remaining year went to disputes, licences and money, which no amount of concentration could have shortened. Newton's part of the work could be slowed by none of this, because Halley absorbed it.

What is striking is how little of this was visible from outside. The great book was the result of an empty notebook, a short manuscript, a stubborn dispute and a friend with a printing bill. For two years one man did one thing, and another man made sure it reached print.

[[lessons]]
$$**For a major piece of work, clear the table of every side pursuit during its peak stretch, and let a trusted ally handle the logistics.** Newton's notebooks show his chemistry stopping for nearly two years while the book grew, and Halley took on the problem of getting it printed. You can do something smaller: choose the one important project of the season, pause the hobby projects and extra commitments for a defined period, and ask someone you trust to carry the administrative tail. In practice, tell people the dates in advance so that nobody expects a quick reply. A clean calendar, even a short one, gives the main work room to take its full shape.$$

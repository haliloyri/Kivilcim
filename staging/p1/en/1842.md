A questionnaire goes out in the post to thousands of doctors. It asks a short list of questions about smoking, and then the researchers wait. They will keep waiting for fifty years. The first test of whether their hunch is right is simply to count who falls ill.

[[story]]
Richard Doll and Austin Bradford Hill were British epidemiologists, and this story is a real episode from the history of cancer, the subject of Siddhartha Mukherjee's *The Emperor of All Maladies*. It is about how two men turned a suspicion about smoking and lung cancer into numbers that could not be waved away.

In 1950 they published a case-control study. It drew on 709 patients in 20 London hospitals, and it compared the smoking habits of the people who had lung cancer with those of the others. That was a method for looking backward, asking patients about their past. It pointed at smoking, but a skeptic could still say that memory is unreliable, or that the hospital patients were not typical.

So they designed a way of looking forward. In October 1951, with the Medical Research Council, they mailed a questionnaire to doctors across Britain. The group they followed was made up of 34,439 male doctors, though some sources give a figure above 40,000. The idea was to record who smoked and who did not, and then let time show who died of what.

Nobody had to rely on anyone's memory of the past, because the smoking habits were written down before the illnesses appeared. In 1956 a follow-up of the doctors was published, and it is cited as the statistical proof linking smoking to lung cancer. The study itself did not stop there. It ran from 1951 until 2001, for fifty years.

Look at the structure of the work. The first study raised the alarm, the second tested it in a different and harder way, and each answered an objection to the one before. Neither relied on a vivid case or a strong feeling. Both relied on counting, and counting is slow, dull and difficult to argue with.

That last quality is the point. A doctor's impression of what he sees in the clinic is shaped by who walks in. Counting a defined group over time removes that filter, and it lets a plain table of numbers do the persuading.

[[lessons]]
$$**When you suspect something is harming your results, count it across a defined group over time before you argue about it.** If you think a certain kind of meeting, order or customer is behind a problem, write down the exact question, decide what counts as an instance, and begin tallying now instead of trading anecdotes. Then, when someone raises an objection, answer it with a second kind of count rather than a louder opinion. Numbers gathered patiently turn a hunch into something that others can examine, check and challenge, and that you yourself can honestly change your mind about.$$

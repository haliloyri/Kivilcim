[[open]]
A newspaper headline counts the people who watched a young woman die and did nothing. The number is exact, the verdict is harsh, and it fits in one line. It will be repeated in lecture halls and textbooks for decades.

[[story]]
In the early hours of 13 March 1964, Kitty Genovese, aged 28, was attacked and killed near her apartment building in Kew Gardens, a quiet residential district of Queens, New York. The man who attacked her was later convicted of her murder.

Two weeks later, on 27 March, *The New York Times* published a story under the headline "37 Who Saw Murder Didn't Call the Police". The article itself spoke of 38 witnesses. Both versions carried the same claim: a crowd of neighbours had watched the killing from their windows and chosen not to act.

The story struck a nerve. It became shorthand for urban coldness, and psychologists turned it into a question about why people fail to help. In 1968, John Darley and Bibb Latané published laboratory experiments showing that people are less likely to act when others are present. The bystander effect, as it came to be called, entered every introductory textbook with Genovese on the first page.

The facts of the night turned out to be different. A police investigation found that about a dozen people had heard or seen some part of what happened, and none had seen all of it. A prosecutor later said that only about half a dozen witnesses were usable, and that none of them reported seeing the stabbing. The attack unfolded in more than one place, and the people who knew about one part did not necessarily know about the other. Some people shouted at the attacker, and at least two called the police. One witness described the two figures as standing close together, not fighting, which suggests how unclear the scene could look from a window.

In 2007, Rachel Manning, Mark Levine and Alan Collins reviewed the record in the journal *American Psychologist*. They found no evidence for the presence of 38 witnesses and called the story more parable than fact. In 2016 the *Times* itself described its original reporting as flawed, saying it had grossly exaggerated the number of witnesses and what they had perceived.

These authors did not claim that the bystander effect was a fiction. The laboratory findings stand on their own. Their objection was to the story, which had narrowed the field's attention to the ways that groups hold people back, and away from the ways that groups can help.

[[lessons]]
$$**When a figure arrives with a moral attached, ask who counted it and what each counted person actually knew before you repeat it.** A manager hears that "most of the team" dislikes a new process, and repeats it in the next meeting, when the real count was a few people who spoke up loudly. A neat number travels because it saves effort, and the correction travels badly because it is long. Spend a minute on the source, and you can still draw a sound conclusion from what remains, or at least say honestly that you are unsure, which is a useful answer in a meeting.$$

[[open]]
Inside a hollow tree in Costa Rica, a bat hangs head down in the daylight, hungry after a night that gave it nothing. A roostmate hanging nearby leans in, opens its mouth against the hungry one's mouth, and pushes a mouthful of warm blood across.

[[story]]
The biologist Gerald Wilkinson went to find out who gets fed in such a roost and why. His work appeared in the journal *Nature* in 1984, and it speaks to a question that Richard Dawkins's *The Selfish Gene* (1976) made famous: why would an animal pay a cost to help another one?

The animals were common vampire bats, which live on blood and cannot go long without it. In the wild he followed groups of eight to twelve adult females that roosted in hollow trees. A bat that has found no meal one night may be given part of another bat's meal the next day, brought up from the stomach and passed mouth to mouth. For the donor it is a real loss, since blood is the only thing it lives on.

The obvious explanation was family. Helping a relative passes on copies of your own genes, so favouring kin makes sense. Wilkinson found that relatedness did matter. But it was not the whole picture. Sharing also depended on whether the two bats had the chance to repay each other. Bats that kept roosting together, and so would meet again, shared more than bats that were only distantly connected. He also tested bats in captivity, including unrelated ones, which let him watch who fed whom under controlled conditions.

The pattern fits two stories at once. One is about kinship. The other is about favours that are given in the expectation of being returned, and that work only when the same individuals keep meeting and can recognise one another. A bat that receives and never gives has little reason to be fed a second time. Each explanation also predicts something different. If kinship were everything, a bat would feed a cousin and turn away an unrelated roostmate however often they met. If repayment were everything, relatives would get no special treatment at all. The data showed both forces at work, which is why neither story could be dropped.

Later work by Gerald Carter and Wilkinson, published in 2013, pointed the same way. They found that sharing is predicted by kinship, by how closely two bats associate, and by help given earlier. Among bats that knew each other well, the best predictor of what bat A gave bat B was what bat B had given bat A.

Few cases of animals paying a real cost for others have been checked this carefully. Here the cost is blood, and the bookkeeping is visible in the data.

[[lessons]]
$$**Keep a loose tally of who has helped you, and help them back.** A shift worker who once covered for a colleague on a sick day will usually find that colleague ready to swap a weekend shift later. Favours work between people who will keep meeting, and they fade when one side only takes. Notice who has been generous to you lately, and look for a concrete way to return it soon, without turning it into an account book that either of you resents. A tally kept loosely and in good faith tells you where the relationship is lopsided long before anyone feels used.$$

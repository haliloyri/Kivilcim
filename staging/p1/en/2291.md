[[open]]
A telephone handset rests in a cradle. When the person on the telephone line starts to speak, a circuit switches a radio transmitter on, and when that person falls silent it switches the radio back to listening. A small box is joining two separate networks, and nobody asked the owner of one of them.

[[story]]
One of the turning points of the telephone story in Tim Wu's *The Master Switch* is the Carterfone. Thomas F. Carter invented it, and Carter Electronics Corp., of which he was president, made and sold it from 1959. The device linked a mobile two-way radio to the public telephone network. The operator of a radio base station set the handset in the device's cradle, and the voice of the person on the telephone switched on the transmitter.

According to the commission's own record, about 4,500 units were produced between 1959 and 1966, and about 3,500 were sold to dealers and distributors in the United States and abroad. AT&T's objection came from a tariff filed on 16 April 1957. It said that no equipment, apparatus, circuit or device not furnished by the telephone company could be attached to the company's facilities, and the Carterfone was read as falling under it.

Carter's private antitrust suit was sent to the Federal Communications Commission under the doctrine of primary jurisdiction, and the Fifth Circuit upheld that. The commission ordered a hearing in October 1966. Carter filed a formal complaint that December, and the two dockets were joined.

The examiner who ran the hearing found a need for the device and no material harm to the telephone system. He did not, however, find the old tariff unlawful. The commission went a step further, because it chose to look backwards as well as forwards. It agreed the Carterfone met a need and did not harm the network, and it found the ban unreasonable and unduly discriminatory, in the past as much as the future. It also cited the 1956 Hush-A-Phone decision.

The core of its finding was that the tariff prohibited "harmless as well as harmful" devices. A customer could attach his own device, the commission said, so long as the connection did not adversely affect the telephone company's operations. The decision came on 26 June 1968, and the two tariff paragraphs at issue were struck down effective 29 July 1968.

The ruling did not throw the doors wide open. Carriers could still block devices that did harm, and they could set reasonable technical standards for equipment from outside. What changed was the direction of proof. Before, every device was forbidden until the company approved it. Now a ban held only if a harm could be shown.

The form of the rule is what stands out. The old tariff did not ask what a device did. It asked only whether the company had supplied it. Defining the same protection by what a device does, not by who made it, kept the network safe and let the harmless in.

[[lessons]]
$$**Write each rule with the harm it prevents named, and drop any rule whose harm you cannot name.** A system that says "nothing without our approval" holds good ideas at the same gate as bad ones and leaves the whole burden on the person who applies. A system that says "allowed unless it causes this harm" still protects, yet it hands the burden of proof to whoever made the rule. If you are writing a tool, access or budget rule for your team, first write one sentence about the concrete harm it prevents, then cut every clause that fails that sentence.$$

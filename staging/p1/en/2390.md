A medal and no money. That was the prize that six teenagers from a West Virginia coal town carried home from a national contest. They had spent years on a project that seemed to point toward a payoff, and the payoff turned out to be something else.

[[story]]
Homer Hickam's *Rocket Boys* leads up to the National Science Fair in Indianapolis in 1960. The members of the Big Creek Missile Agency had first won their county science fair with an exhibit titled "A Study of Amateur Rocketry Techniques," according to the West Virginia Encyclopedia. The national fair came near the end of their high-school years.

They were there because of one person. Hickam says the group did not enter science fairs for their own reasons. They went only near graduation, he explains, because their teacher Miss Riley wanted them to try.

According to published summaries of the chapter on the fair, trouble came on the second night. Parts of Sonny's exhibit, including rocket nozzles, were stolen from the hall, leaving him with his display case, photographs and equations. The same summaries say he got replacement parts delivered the next morning, then faced a judge from the team of Wernher von Braun, the rocket engineer the boys admired. Von Braun himself, according to the summaries, stopped at the display and called the nozzles "marvelous," although Sonny only heard about it afterwards.

The result is on record in Hickam's own guide: the group won the gold and silver awards at the 1960 National Science Fair. Both the Christian Science Monitor and ASME's magazine report the gold medal.

The same summaries say that back in Coalwood he learned Miss Riley was in hospital with cancer, and that she was overjoyed when he showed her the medal.

Then comes the detail the film leaves out. Hickam says the fairs they entered offered no scholarships, which is not how the movie *October Sky* shows it. All of the boys still went to college, some through the military and some with help from their families.

So here is the accounting. Years of work, a national prize, and no cash. Hickam says he began for fun and kept going because of a need to learn things that had seemed hidden from him. That need was met long before the judges spoke. The exhibit was a summary of three years of development, not a project built for the occasion, so the fair rewarded work that already existed. The medal was the part other people could see.

[[lessons]]
$$**Choose work you would still finish if the prize turned out to be only a medal.** Contests, promotions and launches pay in recognition far more often than in money, and recognition arrives late, if at all. The boys had been rewarded long before Indianapolis, by what they learned and by what they could now build, so the contest only confirmed a payment already made. Before you commit months to a goal, ask what you will have gained if the outcome is applause and nothing else, and whether that is enough to keep going when the next hard week arrives and nobody is clapping.$$

In a hotel room, webcams are taped to the wall with duct tape. A guest accepts a toothbrush from a delivery robot, and the robot is a rough stand-in steered by two engineers with a game controller. The guests are enthusiastic.

[[story]]
In May 2014, John Zeratsky, a Google Ventures design partner who would later write *Make Time* with Jake Knapp, visited a company called Savioke in Sunnyvale. Its founder, Steve Cousins, had designed a robot for hotel deliveries, called Relay. A week-long sprint was planned, and it ended on Friday with a test in a real hotel, with real guests. The question was simple: would guests find the robot scary or confusing, and how much personality would they want from it?

The team did not build a working robot. In about a day they put together a stand-in that was not autonomous and only had to carry one toothbrush to one room. Tessa Lau, the chief robot engineer, and engineer Allison Tse steered it with a laptop and a PlayStation controller. Its face was an iPad mock-up, and it spoke in beeps, chimes and a small "happy dance."

The test took place on Friday at a Starwood hotel in Cupertino. The team turned a hotel room into a makeshift lab, with webcams fixed in place by duct tape. The first interview began at 9:14 in the morning. Michael Margolis, the Google Ventures research partner, asked each guest about their travel habits, then had the front desk send up a toothbrush. The robot delivered it, and the guest confirmed the delivery on the touch screen.

The findings were clear. Guests were enthusiastic, had no trouble using the robot, and some asked for a second delivery. A few took selfies with it, and none tried to talk to it. One guest said, "Oh my god, it's a robot!" The personality worked. The touch screen was sluggish, the timing of some sounds was off, and the game idea did not appeal to guests.

About three weeks later the robot entered full-time service at the hotel. In a single week, with no finished product, the team had learned which parts were solid and which were weak. What made that possible was a Friday nailed to the calendar. Friday was fixed. A guest would open the door, so everything built that week only had to be good enough for that door.

[[lessons]]
$$**Fix a date on the calendar and build only what answers your question by then.** A caterer thinking about a new menu could serve a single dish at a friends' dinner and watch the table before printing anything. A date like that trims the "what if we also added this" ideas by itself, because each extra piece makes it harder to arrive on time. Write down the one question you have, and build the cheapest version that can answer it. Everything else can wait until you have seen a real person react and know what is worth adding.$$

[[open]]
A Boston pathologist is about to give a child with leukemia a chemical that blocks folic acid. No chemotherapy has ever produced a remission in this disease. He cannot know whether the child will improve, grow worse, or feel nothing at all, and he has decided to find out.

[[story]]
Sidney Farber was a pathologist at Children's Hospital in Boston, a doctor trained to study diseased tissue rather than to treat patients. This story is a real episode from the history of cancer, the subject of Siddhartha Mukherjee's *The Emperor of All Maladies*. It concerns acute leukemia in children and a compound called aminopterin, which works against folic acid.

Treating patients was not what a pathologist was trained for. Pathologists work with what a disease leaves behind: a slide, a tissue sample, a record of what went wrong. Farber's question was a different one, about what a drug might do to the disease while the child was still alive, and he set out to answer it with a real compound and real patients.

In late December 1947, Farber began treating his first patient with aminopterin. Over the following months he treated sixteen children in all. Ten of them went into remission. Two did not respond. Four died within months.

He reported the results in the New England Journal of Medicine on 3 June 1948. Those remissions were the first that chemotherapy had ever produced. They were also short. Aminopterin cured no one, and it was later replaced by a related drug, methotrexate.

The count is worth reading slowly. Ten out of sixteen is a majority, in a disease where nobody had seen a drug make the illness retreat at all. The two non-responders and the four deaths belong to the same tally, so the result was small, temporary and uneven. Because the remissions were short, the claim that held up was a narrow one: in this group of sixteen children, ten showed the disease retreating, and nothing larger was promised. It still mattered, because it answered a question no one had been able to answer: can a chemical push leukemia back?

That narrowness is part of why the work could be trusted and repeated. A claim of a cure would have invited disbelief, while a plain count of what happened to sixteen named patients invited other doctors to try the same thing and compare notes.

A remission that lasts a few months is not a cure, but it is proof that the mechanism works. It showed that a drug could change the course of the disease in a living child, and that was enough to open a field. Methotrexate and the other drugs in the same line came after a pathologist who was willing to try one chemical and then count, child by child, what it did.

[[lessons]]
$$**When a problem has no known treatment, run a small trial and record every result honestly, including the failures.** Suppose you are trying a new way of onboarding customers or running a team meeting that nobody around you has tested, and the old way is clearly not working. Write down in advance what you will count, then report the wins and the losses together. A partial, short-lived result still tells you whether the idea works at all, and an honest count of it is what lets other people repeat your attempt, improve it and trust what you report.$$

In five districts of a Midwestern city, marked police cars stop cruising the streets and now drive out only when someone calls. In five others, the cruising doubles or triples. The department keeps score for a full year, with surveys, crime reports and arrests as the tally.

[[story]]
The idea behind routine patrol is simple: a marked car driving through a neighbourhood keeps crime down and makes people feel safe. In Kansas City, Missouri, the police department and the Police Foundation decided to test it, and Malcolm Gladwell's *Talking to Strangers* is among the places that discuss the result. The experiment ran for a year, ending on 30 September 1973.

They took 15 patrol districts, called beats, in the south of the city and sorted them into three groups of five. In the first group routine patrol was removed, and officers entered only when residents called. In the second, patrol continued at the usual level. In the third, it was raised to roughly two or three times the usual level.

Some patrol officers were uneasy about the first group, because they believed that pulling patrols out would endanger residents. The worry was strong enough that the experiment was stopped and restarted three times.

The researchers did not trust one number. They used victim surveys, reported crime, arrests, a survey of businesses, surveys of attitudes, and trained observers who watched how police and citizens dealt with each other. The authors were George Kelling, Tony Pate, Duane Dieckman and Charles Brown, and the Police Foundation published their report in 1974.

The results across the three groups were nearly flat. Levels of crime did not differ significantly, and neither did residents' fear of crime, their satisfaction with the police, or police response times. Residents did not even notice that patrol levels had changed. The Police Foundation concluded that routine patrol in marked cars had little value in preventing crime or making citizens feel safer, and an official summary noted that officers' uncommitted time could be used for other purposes without harming public safety.

One more finding stands apart. According to a summary of the study, crime prevention depended more on whether citizens were willing to report suspicious or criminal behaviour than on how much patrolling went on. A routine that sounds obviously right contains a claim that can be tested, and the way to test it was to change it in some places, leave it alone in others, and compare. The comparison asked the same question of every beat, which is why the answer could be trusted more than anyone's opinion.

[[lessons]]
$$**Test the routine you defend by changing it in one small place.** A practice that has run for years feels proven simply because it is familiar, and familiarity is not a measurement. Say your team sends a weekly status report that everyone assumes people rely on. If you skip it for one group for a month and track the questions that come in, you learn whether it was doing the job or just filling a slot in the calendar. Either result helps: you either keep the routine with a reason, or you free the hours it was taking, and you no longer defend it on habit alone.$$

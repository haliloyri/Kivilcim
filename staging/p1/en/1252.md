[[open]]
Open a page of Leonardo da Vinci's notebooks and you won't find tidy paragraphs. You'll find a flying machine, a swirl of water, a muscle, a face, and arrows and tiny notes weaving between them. Tony Buzan looked at those pages and saw a different way to think.

[[story]]
Leonardo da Vinci left behind thousands of pages of notebooks. Many of them survive today, and they cover an almost unbelievable range: human anatomy, the flight of birds, the movement of water, war machines, geology, light, painting. They're also famous for how they look. Leonardo wrote much of his text in mirror writing, from right to left. And on page after page, words and images live side by side.

A sketch of a heart sits next to notes on how blood might move. A drawing of a wing sits beside calculations. Arrows connect ideas across the page. Diagrams, notes, questions and pictures all tumble together.

Tony Buzan, the creator of the Mind Map, often pointed to these notebooks in *The Mind Map Book* and elsewhere. To him, they were a clue.

Buzan argued that most of us were taught to take notes in a very narrow way. At school, notes are usually lines of text, written from left to right and top to bottom, often in a single color. That format, he believed, uses only part of what our minds can do. It captures words but leaves out images, color, space and, most importantly, connections between ideas.

Leonardo's pages did the opposite.

##They looked less like essays and more like a mind thinking out loud: seeing, linking and questioning all at once.##

Buzan suggested that great thinkers like Leonardo naturally combined images, words and associations when they worked on problems. Whether that fully explains Leonardo's genius is Buzan's interpretation, and a bold one. But the notebooks themselves are real, and anyone can see how differently they're organized from a standard page of notes.

This became part of Buzan's inspiration for the Mind Map. A central image or idea in the middle of the page. Branches radiating out, each with a key word. Colors, small drawings and arrows linking related ideas. The goal was to give ordinary people a practical way to take notes that looked a little more like Leonardo's, visual, connected and alive.

You don't need to be a genius to try it. You just need a blank page, a few colors and the permission to stop writing in straight lines.

[[lessons]]
$$**Mix images and words.** Leonardo's pages combine drawings with notes, and each makes the other clearer. Buzan argues that using images alongside words helps memory and understanding. Even simple sketches can make an idea stick far better than a sentence alone.$$

$$**Connections matter as much as content.** Arrows and links across Leonardo's pages show how ideas relate. Linear notes often hide those relationships. Mapping connections visually helps you see the bigger picture and spot new links you'd otherwise miss.$$

$$**Linear notes aren't the only way.** School teaches us one format and rarely questions it. Buzan invites us to experiment with different ways of putting thinking on paper. Trying a more visual approach, even once, can reveal how much the format of our notes shapes how we think. The page becomes a place to think, not just a place to store what someone else said.$$

[[reflect]]
- What do your notes usually look like, and do they help you think or just record?
- When have you understood something better by drawing it or mapping it out?
- What topic are you struggling with that might become clearer as a picture?

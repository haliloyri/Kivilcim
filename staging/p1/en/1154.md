On the East Coast of the United States stands one of the most famous statues in the world, a symbol of liberty. Viktor Frankl admired what it stood for. But he thought it told only half the story, and he had a suggestion for the other coast.

[[story]]
Toward the end of *Man's Search for Meaning*, Viktor Frankl turns to a word that modern life celebrates perhaps more than any other: freedom.

Frankl was not against freedom. He had seen, in the camps, what it meant to lose it. And he believed deeply in the one freedom no one can take away, the freedom to choose one's attitude. But he also saw a danger in treating freedom as the final goal.

Freedom, he wrote, is only part of the story and half of the truth. It is the negative side of something larger, the side that says what you are free *from*. The positive side, what you are free *for*, is responsibleness. Without it, he warned, freedom threatens to degenerate into mere arbitrariness: doing things simply because you can, with no reason that matters.

And then he made a proposal that has been quoted ever since.

> That is why I recommend that the Statue of Liberty on the East Coast be supplemented by a Statue of Responsibility on the West Coast.

##Liberty on one coast, responsibility on the other, holding up the same country.##

It sounds like a clever line, but it follows from the core of his thinking. Frankl believed people don't find meaning by keeping every option open. They find it by committing to something: a task, a person, a value. That commitment limits their freedom in one sense. It's also what makes their life mean something.

He also turned the usual question about meaning around. People often ask, *what is the meaning of my life?* Frankl said that's the wrong way round. It is not we who ask life. It is life that asks us. Each situation puts a question in front of us, and we answer it not with words, but with our actions, by being responsible.

To make that concrete, he offered a maxim for living. It's worth reading slowly:

> Live as if you were living already for the second time and as if you had acted the first time as wrongly as you are about to act now!

Imagine you've already lived this moment once, and made the wrong choice. Now you get to choose again. That thought, Frankl believed, makes the weight of each decision suddenly clear. Freedom is still there. You're simply aware of what it's for.

[[lessons]]
$$**Freedom needs a partner.** Frankl called freedom "only part of the story and half of the truth." Without responsibility, it drifts into doing things just because you can. His imagined Statue of Responsibility isn't a rejection of liberty. It's a reminder that freedom only becomes meaningful when you use it for something.$$

$$**Life is asking you.** Instead of waiting to find the meaning of your life, Frankl suggests you're already being asked by each situation you face. The answer isn't a philosophy. It's what you actually do: how you treat people, what you take responsibility for, what you commit to.$$

$$**Imagine a second chance.** Frankl's maxim asks you to picture that you've already lived this moment and got it wrong. It's a strangely effective thought. It turns vague good intentions into a clear sense of which choice you'd regret, before you make it.$$

[[reflect]]
- Where do you use your freedom mainly to keep options open and avoid committing?
- What question is your life putting in front of you right now, and how are you answering it through your actions?
- What responsibility have you been avoiding that might actually give your days more meaning?

&&If this were your second time living today, what would you do differently?&&

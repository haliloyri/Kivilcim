Aviator. Film producer. Record-breaking pilot. One of the richest men in the world. Howard Hughes looked like the very definition of success. Ryan Holiday thinks the picture was misleading, and that the real story is about what ego can do to someone with every advantage.

[[story]]
In *Ego Is the Enemy*, Ryan Holiday returns several times to Howard Hughes. On the surface, Hughes's life reads like a list of triumphs. He inherited a successful tool company from his father, poured money into Hollywood films, designed and flew record-setting aircraft, and built a business empire. For decades, the public saw him as a daring genius.

Holiday asks us to look more closely.

Much of Hughes's fortune, he points out, came from the tool business he inherited, not from his own ventures. Many of those ventures were badly managed. Films went wildly over budget. Business decisions were delayed or overturned on a whim. Huge sums were lost. Hughes's reputation as a brilliant businessman was built on image as much as on results.

At the heart of the problem was a need for control. Hughes wanted to decide everything himself, down to small details. He didn't trust others to run what he owned. He ignored advice that didn't match his own views. Capable people found it almost impossible to work for him, because at any moment he might interfere or reverse their decisions.

##The same qualities that made Hughes daring also made him impossible to help.##

Over time, Hughes withdrew. In his later years, he became a recluse, living largely out of view in hotel suites, including one in Las Vegas, and controlling his businesses through a small circle of aides. The man who had once been one of the most visible people in America was now almost invisible, cut off from the world he had once tried to dominate.

Holiday doesn't tell this story to mock Hughes. Hughes was genuinely talented and genuinely brave. The point is what happened to that talent. Holiday argues that success can make ego stronger, not weaker. When you've achieved a lot, it's easy to start believing you're always right, that you deserve special treatment, that you must control everything, and that others are against you. Holiday names these dangers: entitlement, control and paranoia.

Hughes's life shows where they can lead. Not usually to a sudden fall, but to a slow narrowing, as a person stops listening, stops learning and pushes away the people who might have helped.

[[lessons]]
$$**The image can hide the results.** Hughes's fame as a brilliant businessman concealed years of losses and poor management. Holiday's point is that an impressive public image is not the same thing as real success. Looking honestly at results, especially your own, protects you from believing your own legend.$$

$$**Needing total control is a warning sign.** Hughes interfered in details, overruled capable people and ignored advice. That need for control made him hard to work for and harder to help. When you find yourself unable to let go of anything, it's worth asking whether it's about quality or about ego.$$

$$**Success can strengthen ego.** Holiday warns that entitlement, control and paranoia often grow with achievement. The more you've accomplished, the easier it becomes to stop listening. Staying humble after success is harder than staying humble before it, and even more important.$$

[[reflect]]
- Where does your need for control slow down or frustrate the people around you?
- Whose honest advice have you quietly stopped listening to?
- Which of your achievements do you tend to talk about more than you examine?

&&What part of your success might be feeding your ego more than your work?&&

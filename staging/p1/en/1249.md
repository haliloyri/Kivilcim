Somewhere in the small print of many investment statements is a number that looks harmless: an annual fee of one or two percent. Tony Robbins argues it may be one of the most expensive numbers in your financial life, precisely because it looks so small.

[[story]]
In *Money: Master the Game*, Tony Robbins spends a lot of time on a subject most investors find boring: fees. He argues it's anything but.

Many people save through mutual funds or retirement plans managed by professionals. These funds charge fees every year, often expressed as a small percentage of the money invested. One percent. One and a half. Two. Next to the returns people hope for, these numbers seem trivial. Few people even know what they're paying.

Robbins wants readers to see fees the way they see returns. We all know compounding makes money grow: returns earn returns, year after year. But fees compound too, in the other direction. Every year, a fee takes a slice of your money. That slice is then no longer there to grow in the following years. Over decades, the missing money, and all the growth it would have earned, adds up.

To make the point, Robbins draws heavily on John Bogle, the founder of Vanguard and the pioneer of low-cost index funds. Bogle had a phrase for this effect: **the tyranny of compounding costs**.

##Over a lifetime of investing, Bogle argued, seemingly small annual fees can quietly consume a very large share of an investor's total returns.##

The investor takes the risk and provides the money. Yet a surprisingly large part of the long-term reward can end up with the fund managers instead.

Robbins adds a second problem. People often pay those higher fees in the hope that professional managers will beat the market. But he reports that, over long periods, most actively managed funds fail to beat the simple index they're measured against. In other words, many investors pay more for results that are, on average, worse.

That's why Robbins, following Bogle, encourages many investors to consider low-cost index funds. Instead of trying to pick winners, an index fund simply aims to match the market, at a very low cost.

His practical advice is simple, and something almost anyone can act on. Find out exactly what fees you're paying on every investment and retirement account. Compare them with low-cost alternatives. It isn't a thrilling task. But he argues it may be one of the most valuable hours you ever spend on your money.

[[lessons]]
$$**Fees compound against you.** Returns grow by building on themselves; fees shrink your savings the same way. A one or two percent annual fee takes money that would otherwise have grown for decades. Bogle's "tyranny of compounding costs" is a reminder that the smallest-looking numbers can have the largest long-term effect.$$

$$**Paying more doesn't guarantee more.** Robbins reports that most actively managed funds fail to beat their index over the long run. Higher fees often buy hope, not results. That makes simple, low-cost index funds a strong option for many ordinary investors who don't want to gamble on picking winners.$$

$$**Know exactly what you're paying.** Many people have never checked their fund fees. Robbins's advice is practical and immediate: find the numbers, compare them with alternatives, and act if needed. It's unglamorous work, but an hour spent here can matter more than years of chasing tips.$$

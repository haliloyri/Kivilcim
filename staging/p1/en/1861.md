A company's sergeants tell their superiors that they will hand back their stripes unless the commander goes. In an army that sounds like mutiny, and the usual answer to mutiny is punishment. Yet in a small English village that winter, the commander was removed by an order, and the question behind the order was harder than it looked.

[[story]]
Stephen E. Ambrose's 1992 book *Band of Brothers* follows Easy Company of the 506th Parachute Infantry Regiment, and one of the company's crises came in England, not in battle. In the winter of 1943–44, in the Wiltshire village of Aldbourne, the paratroopers were waiting for the invasion of Europe. Their commander was Captain Herbert Sobel.

The company's sergeants and other noncommissioned officers decided they could not go on under him. They announced that they would give up their stripes unless Sobel was replaced. Noncommissioned officers run a company's daily routine, and handing back the rank means stepping out of that work. They had no vote on who commanded them, and the only thing they could put on the table was their own rank. A unit did this while it was preparing for war, and the price was the stripes on its own sleeves.

The decision fell to the regiment's commander, Colonel Sink. He took Sobel out of the company and sent him to a training school at Chilton Foliat. Lieutenant Thomas Meehan took command of Easy in February 1944. Accounts differ on what happened to the men seen as the ringleaders, but every version has Sobel leaving the company.

The second half of the story comes years later. Dick Winters, an Easy officer, said afterwards that Sobel's tough training had helped the company succeed. So one man was both the commander the company could not bear and the trainer who prepared it for combat. The two statements do not cancel each other out.

Look closely and two different questions were tangled together. The first is whether this man can lead us, and the sergeants answered no. The second is whether his training worked, and Winters's later view answers yes. Sink's decision can be read as pulling those questions apart: Sobel left the company but stayed in the army, at a training school, which is a place where toughness is the job itself. Whether that was Sink's intention is not recorded. All we can see is that the outcome answered each question on its own.

It is easy to judge a person's method and their results on one scale, and to let a single feeling decide both. At Aldbourne the scales were used separately. The sergeants weighed the method, and Winters, years later, weighed the result.

[[lessons]]
$$**Weigh a boss's method and results as two separate questions.** People can dislike how a demanding manager treats them and still have learned something real from that manager, and both judgments can stand. An apprentice who remembers a harsh workshop master may never forgive the shouting yet still owe that master the steadiness of their hands. Keeping the two apart means you do not have to praise everything about someone to admit what they taught you, or condemn everything to defend your own limits. A fair assessment writes down which item belongs to the method and which to the result.$$

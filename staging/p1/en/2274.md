[[story]]
The man was Douglas Engelbart of the Stanford Research Institute, and the stage was the Civic Auditorium in San Francisco, at the Fall Joint Computer Conference. The date was 9 December 1968. According to published accounts, about a thousand computer professionals sat in the hall, and Engelbart had ninety minutes. The session was titled "A research center for augmenting human intellect." Walter Isaacson's *The Innovators* lists the demo among the milestones of the digital revolution.

Instead of lecturing on his ideas, he ran them. On the screen the audience saw windows, text that could be edited and moved around, and underlined words that, when clicked, jumped to another page. The pointer was moved by a box with a button on top, an early mouse that Bill English had built from Engelbart's sketches. Colleagues in another place appeared on part of the screen and edited the same text at the same time.

The staging was as ambitious as the software. The computer, an SDS-940, was at the lab in Menlo Park. Two homemade 1200-baud modems on a leased phone line carried keyboard and mouse signals in each direction, and two microwave links brought live video between the lab and the hall. English worked a video switcher that chose what appeared on the big screen, which an Eidophor projector threw onto a screen about twenty-two feet high. At the lab, Stewart Brand, editor of the *Whole Earth Catalog*, ran a camera and had advised the team on how to present the demo.

Everything depended on those links holding for the whole ninety minutes. Accounts say that before that day many in the field regarded Engelbart as something of a crackpot. When it was over, the audience gave him a standing ovation. Andy van Dam, who had begun work on a similar system the year before, questioned him closely and later called it the greatest thing he had ever seen.

Part of what made the hour persuasive was that every feature appeared as something in use. A word processor, a link between documents and a shared screen were worked in front of a thousand people, by a team that had to make every wire hold at the same moment.

[[lessons]]
$$**Let people watch the idea work instead of hearing you describe it.** Engelbart's audience did not have to take his word for hypertext or shared editing, because they saw a link get clicked and two people change one document, with their own eyes. If you want your team to try a new way of keeping the project notes, open the real document in the meeting and edit it together for ten minutes before anyone votes. A live demonstration also shows you where it breaks while the audience can still help, which a slide never does. And people who have watched something work will usually ask better questions than people who have only heard of it.$$

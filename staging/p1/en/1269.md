People lie in a brain scanner and look at a set of upsetting pictures. Twelve hours later, they see the very same pictures again. Nothing about the images has changed. For some of the volunteers, though, the second viewing lands differently, and their brains show it. The difference comes down to how they spent the twelve hours in between.

[[story]]
The study comes from Matthew Walker's lab, and he describes it in *Why We Sleep*. It starts from a peculiar fact about REM sleep, the stage of sleep in which most vivid dreaming happens.

REM, Walker explains, is the only time the brain is essentially free of noradrenaline, a chemical tied to stress. Yet the regions that handle emotion and memory are highly active. The emotional machinery is running while the stress chemical is switched off.

Walker wanted to know what that state does to the memories of a hard day. So participants in his lab viewed emotional images while lying in an MRI scanner. Twelve hours later, they viewed the same images again. Some had spent those twelve hours asleep overnight. Others had spent them awake during the day.

The people who slept in between reported that the images felt less intense the second time. Their brain scans matched what they said. The amygdala, a region closely involved in emotional reactions, responded less. Prefrontal regions, which help put a reaction in context, re-engaged.

The group that stayed awake did not show this change. They had the same pictures and the same stretch of time, and their reaction stayed much where it was.

Walker then looked at the sleepers more closely. The amount of REM sleep, and the specific brain activity during it, tracked how much the emotional intensity had dropped.

##The memory was kept, but its emotional charge was softened; Walker calls this "overnight therapy."##

He connects the idea to post-traumatic stress disorder, where people often have recurring nightmares. In Walker's view, those repeated dreams suggest the overnight process is failing to do its work. He describes research by Murray Raskind, who gave veterans a drug called prazosin that lowers noradrenaline. The drug reduced their nightmares.

Walker presents these as links and a proposed mechanism, not a settled explanation of every emotional difficulty. But the scanner result on its own changes a familiar piece of folk wisdom. We usually say that time takes the edge off a bad experience. In this study, time alone didn't do it. Time spent asleep, and in particular in dream sleep, is where the change showed up.

[[lessons]]
$$**The memory stays; the sting fades.** The sleepers in Walker's study didn't forget the images. They recognised them and still rated them as less intense after the night. That is a different kind of relief from forgetting: you keep what happened and lose some of its grip.$$

$$**Stress chemistry shapes how a memory gets filed.** Walker describes REM as a window when noradrenaline is almost absent. In Raskind's work, lowering noradrenaline with prazosin reduced veterans' nightmares. How a memory is reprocessed seems to depend on what state the brain is in while it does it.$$

$$**A night between the event and the response changes the response.** Only the group that slept showed a calmer amygdala the second time. The awake group had the same twelve hours and no such shift. Reacting in the evening means reacting before that overnight step has had a chance to happen.$$

[[reflect]]
- Think of something upsetting from a recent evening. Did it read differently to you the next morning?
- When you're going through a hard stretch, what usually happens to your sleep first?
- Which kind of message do you most often answer late at night that might look different after breakfast?

&&The next time something stings late in the day, could you hold off on replying or deciding until after one night's sleep, and notice how the same thing looks in the morning?&&

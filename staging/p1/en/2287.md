[[open]]
A questionnaire reaches thousands of households, and one question is about work: are you satisfied with your job? Most people tick a box without thinking much about it. Added together, the boxes become a single number, and when that number is announced it turns out to be the lowest anyone has ever recorded for it.

[[story]]
On 5 January 2010 the Conference Board, a US research group, published its annual survey of job satisfaction. About 5,000 households had been questioned by the polling firm TNS. Only 45 percent of respondents said they were satisfied with their jobs. When the survey began in 1987 the figure was 61.1 percent. News reports described it as the lowest reading in more than twenty years of records.

Lynn Franco, who directs the Board's Consumer Research Center, pointed out something odd about the trend. The slide had gone on through economic booms and through busts. If dissatisfaction were mostly a product of the recession, the numbers would have recovered in the good years, and they had not. According to coverage of the report, about 22 percent of the people questioned did not expect to still be in their current job a year later.

Newport brings figures of this kind into *So Good They Can't Ignore You*, according to published accounts of the book, as part of the case that something is wrong with how people choose and shape their work. A fall of sixteen points over more than two decades is hard to explain with one cause, and the survey does not try to. It records how people feel. It does not say which advice they followed, what they did all day or how good they had become at it.

That limit is useful to see. A statistic like this one is a reason to ask a question, and it cannot supply the answer. Everyone who reads it feels it applies to someone else, or to everyone. Both reactions skip the same step: looking at your own work and asking what, in particular, is making you dissatisfied.

Seen from inside your own week, the distinction matters even more. If you write down the three moments that drained you most, you may find most of them fall under one heading. That heading says what a national survey cannot: the name of the problem.

Is it the tasks, the boss, the pay, the lack of control, or the feeling of not being good at anything yet? Those are five different problems, and the same number hides all of them. The survey says nothing about which one is yours, and that is why the number can start a conversation but cannot end one.

[[lessons]]
$$**Turn a gloomy statistic into a question about your own work.** A national number tells you that many people are unhappy, and it cannot tell you which ingredient of unhappiness is in your week. Break your own dissatisfaction into parts such as tasks, people, pay, control and your sense of getting better, and score each from one to five this evening. The lowest score is where to experiment first, with one small change over the next month. Averages describe a crowd, and a change has to be made in a single person's calendar, starting with the next small thing you can try.$$

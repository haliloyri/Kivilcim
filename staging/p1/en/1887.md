[[open]]
A program is shown two options each round: cooperate or betray. On its first move it always cooperates. After that it only copies what its opponent did a moment ago. Placed among rivals written by clever specialists, it looks almost too naive to take seriously.

[[story]]
Richard Dawkins's *The Selfish Gene* (1976) asks how self-interested genes can still produce cooperation. This story is about a famous experiment on the same question: the computer tournaments of the political scientist Robert Axelrod. In 1980 he invited game theorists to submit programs for a repeated Prisoner's Dilemma. In each round a program could cooperate or defect. Betraying a cooperator paid best in a single round, yet two programs that kept betraying each other did worse than two that kept cooperating.

Fourteen entries arrived, and Axelrod added a program that played at random. Every program met every other one in a round robin, and each pairing lasted 200 rounds. Some entries were elaborate, built to study the opponent and exploit it. The winner was the shortest idea on the table: Tit for Tat, sent in by the mathematical psychologist Anatol Rapoport. It cooperated on the first move, then repeated whatever the other side had done on the previous move.

Axelrod published the results and ran a second, larger tournament. This time the entrants had read the first results and could design against Tit for Tat. Tit for Tat won again.

Axelrod drew out what the top programs had in common. They were nice: they never defected first. They were retaliating, so a betrayal was answered at once. They were forgiving, going back to cooperation as soon as the other side did. And they were clear, which meant an opponent could work out the pattern quickly. A program that was hard to read could not earn trust, and without trust the cooperation never started. The same traits explain why Tit for Tat never lost badly: it was never exploited twice in a row, and it never started a fight it could not finish.

Later researchers added a caution. Reanalyses dispute how robust Tit for Tat's victory is, and against other mixes of opponents other strategies score higher. The tournament does not show that copying always wins. It shows that where the same players meet again and again, a simple and readable rule can beat clever ones, and that an entrant does not need to beat any single opponent to come out on top. It only needs a good score with everyone.

[[lessons]]
$$**Start friendly, answer in kind, and make your pattern easy to read.** A small business that pays its supplier on time, speaks up politely the first time an invoice is wrong, and then goes back to normal terms once it is fixed gives the supplier a rule it can understand. Both sides can predict the other, so favours are easy to offer and slights are easy to repair. You can use this in any relationship that will continue: begin with goodwill, let a clear lapse have a clear and proportionate response, and say plainly when the matter is closed so that nobody keeps waiting for the next blow.$$

[[open]]
Picture a royal court. The king has just laid out his plan for war, and every courtier nods along. Then the jester steps forward and turns the whole plan into a joke. The room goes quiet. The king laughs, or at least doesn't punish him, and the jester walks away unharmed. It's tempting to say his wit saved him. But it wasn't the joke that saved him. So what did?

[[story]]
Start with Robert Greene. In *The 48 Laws of Power*, published in 1998, Law 6 reads:

> Court Attention at All Cost.

Greene's argument is blunt. Invisibility is powerlessness. If nobody notices you, nobody even weighs what you say. You aren't rejected. You're simply never considered. So Greene tells you to stand out, even through controversy or scandal, rather than fade into obscurity. One of the figures he uses to make the point is the showman P.T. Barnum, who worked hard to keep the public's eyes on him.

For Greene, attention is a personal strategy. You grab it yourself, and you use it to make room for your own power. The logic is hard to argue with. Being noticed comes before being taken seriously.

Now go back to that jester. What follows is not from Greene's book. It's a piece of general history set beside his law as a contrast. In some European courts, jesters could mock the king and his decisions and often went unpunished. Customs varied a great deal from court to court, and it was never true immunity. A jester could still go too far. But the protection he did have didn't come from being clever or loud. It came from a role the court itself recognized. The courtier standing next to him might have had sharper jokes, a bigger reputation and more attention than any fool. He still wouldn't dare say what the jester said, because he didn't hold the office.

##The jester's license to speak belonged to the job, not to the man wearing the bells.##

That leaves you with two very different ways of getting a hard truth heard.

Greene's way depends on you. You make yourself impossible to ignore, and your visibility buys you a hearing. It can work. But it's tied to one person, and it has to be earned again and again.

The jester's way depends on design. The institution sets aside a place where dissent is expected. The person in that place isn't being brave. They're doing what the role asks of them. And if they leave, the role is still there for the next person.

Carry that into a modern workplace. Think of the team where one outspoken colleague always raises the uncomfortable point. People admire that person, and they lean on them without noticing. Then that colleague leaves, and the hard questions quietly leave too. Nothing was built. It was all one person's nerve.

Or think of the manager who says "my door is always open, anyone can say anything." It sounds generous. In practice it asks each person to find their own courage, alone, with no cover. Mostly, people stay quiet.

So here's the question the jester raises against Greene. When a hard truth finally gets spoken, is it because someone made themselves visible enough to risk it, or because someone was given a place where saying it was simply their job?

[[lessons]]
$$**Two routes to being heard.** Greene's Law 6 says the unnoticed person isn't even evaluated, so you have to make yourself seen. That's a real route. The jester points to a second one. His privilege belonged to the office, not to his personality. A court that wanted to hear mockery of the king's plans created a place for it. You can win a hearing by being visible, or an institution can hand you one by design.$$

$$**Courage leaves when the person leaves.** Visibility-based influence walks out the door with whoever had it. That's why the outspoken colleague's departure takes the hard questions with it. A role works differently. The jester's seat outlasted any single jester, and it still carried permission to speak. If truth-telling rests on one person's nerve, it's fragile. If it rests on a defined role, it lasts.$$

$$**Name the slot, or expect silence.** "Anyone can say anything" gives nobody cover, so nobody speaks. The jester had a specific job, and that specificity is what protected him. Narrow, named openings like "fifteen minutes of this meeting are for objections" turn dissent from an act of bravery into an item on the agenda.$$

[[reflect]]
- Think of the last time bad news reached the people who needed it where you work. Was someone being brave, or was it their job?
- Have you tried to get heard through sheer visibility, pushing harder and louder, in a situation that really needed a proper channel?
- Picture the most outspoken person on your team leaving tomorrow. Who would say the uncomfortable things then?
- In your family or friend group, is there anyone who stays quiet and resents it later because no one ever gave them a turn?

&&Where you work, is there a named place for hard truths, or does everyone have to rely on their own courage?&&

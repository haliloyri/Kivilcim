[[story]]
In late 1954 the CIA accepted a proposal from Lockheed for a high-altitude reconnaissance aircraft, a single-seat, single-engine jet meant to carry cameras over the Soviet Union. With President Eisenhower's approval the CIA took the offer, and Allen Dulles put his special assistant, Richard Bissell, in charge on the agency's side. The plane became the U-2. Ben Rich and Leo Janos's *Skunk Works* places it among the early triumphs of the group Kelly Johnson led, and the story has a simple spine: a short, fixed time to go from paper to flight.

Lockheed's own profile of Johnson says the U-2 launched nine months after the official contract. Other accounts describe it as roughly eight months to design the aircraft, build it and fly it, so the exact count depends on where you start the clock. The first flight took place at a remote test site in Nevada in early August 1955, with the Lockheed test pilot Tony LeVier at the controls.

A CIA article from its journal Studies in Intelligence sums up the result: the team came in on time and under budget. The CIA's later history of the A-12 repeats the point, saying that Lockheed had produced the U-2 on time and under budget. Two separate CIA documents make the same statement, which makes it easy to trust.

Johnson also left a small mark on the place where the plane was tested. According to CIA accounts, he called the remote site "Paradise Ranch," a name meant to make a bleak desert camp sound more inviting to the people sent there. It was soon shortened to "the Ranch."

What the record shows is a team that held to its dates and its money in a program that began as a few pages of proposal. A date you keep is a form of credibility that costs nothing extra to display, because everyone waiting for the result can see it. From the customer's side, a contractor who names a schedule and a price and then meets both is simple to trust again.

That record mattered beyond 1955. When the CIA later wanted an aircraft that would fly higher and faster than the U-2, the aircraft that became the A-12, the work went to the same company and the same chief designer.

[[lessons]]
$$**Deliver the small promise exactly as made, because that record is what earns you the next big request.** A caterer who handles a modest office lunch on time and within the quoted price is the one invited to cater the company party. A freelancer who returns a first draft on the day agreed gets the longer contract without having to argue for it. The point is that people judge your next promise by how the last one ended. Before you ask for trust on something large, look for a small task where you can show, in numbers, that your dates and budgets hold, and let the result speak for you when the bigger conversation comes.$$

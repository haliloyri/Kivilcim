In 1938, researchers at Harvard began following a group of young men to find out what makes a life go well. They kept asking questions as those men went to war, built careers, married, divorced, raised children and grew old. More than eighty-five years later, the study is still running, and its answer is surprisingly simple.

[[story]]
*The Good Life* is written by Robert Waldinger, the current director of the Harvard Study of Adult Development, and Marc Schulz, its associate director. Together they tell the story of one of the longest studies of human life ever conducted.

It began in 1938 with 268 sophomores at Harvard. Around the same time, a separate study began following 456 boys from some of Boston's poorest neighborhoods, many from immigrant families living in tough conditions. The two studies were later brought together, giving researchers a remarkable range of lives to compare: privileged and poor, successful and struggling.

For decades, the researchers kept checking in. They sent questionnaires. They conducted interviews in participants' homes. They collected medical records. Later, they took blood samples and brain scans. Eventually, they included the participants' wives, and then their children. They watched lives unfold in real time, rather than asking people to remember them years later.

Over time, a clear pattern emerged from all that data.

##Good relationships keep people happier and healthier. That was the single clearest message of the entire study.##

The quality of people's close relationships, with partners, family, friends and community, turned out to be a stronger predictor of long, happy lives than wealth, fame, social class or even IQ.

One finding stands out. The researchers looked at participants at age 50 and then again at age 80. The people who had been most satisfied in their relationships at 50 were the healthiest at 80. In fact, relationship satisfaction at 50 predicted physical health at 80 better than cholesterol levels did.

The flip side was just as clear. Loneliness was linked to poorer health, earlier decline and shorter lives.

What makes this so striking is that many participants, when they were young, believed success, money and recognition would be what made them happy. Many worked hard for those things. The study suggests that the ones who fared best were those who also invested in their relationships.

Waldinger and Schulz stress that this isn't only about having many friends. It's about the quality of connection. And like physical fitness, they argue, what they call "social fitness" needs regular attention. Relationships don't maintain themselves. They need time, care and effort, especially when life gets busy.

[[lessons]]
$$**Relationships matter more than achievement.** After more than eighty-five years, the study's clearest finding was about connection, not money or fame. Many participants spent their youth chasing achievement. The ones who flourished were often those who also invested in the people around them.$$

$$**Relationships shape physical health.** Relationship satisfaction at 50 predicted health at 80 better than cholesterol did. That turns relationships from a "soft" topic into a serious health issue. Time with the people you love isn't a luxury to fit in after everything else. It's part of taking care of yourself.$$

$$**Social fitness needs practice.** Waldinger and Schulz compare relationships to physical fitness: they weaken without regular attention. A friendship neglected for years doesn't stay the same; it fades. Small, repeated acts of connection are what keep relationships strong enough to support you later in life. The effort is small; the payoff lasts decades.$$

- Who would you want around you when you're eighty?

Mid-morning at an Israeli prison parole board, the hearings stop for a snack of a sandwich and a piece of fruit. Before the break, a prisoner's request was refused. After the break, a prisoner with a very similar file might hear yes.

[[story]]
In 2011 Shai Danziger, Jonathan Levav and Liora Avnaim-Pesso published a study of 1,112 parole rulings. Eight judges had made them, sitting on two boards that served four Israeli prisons, over 50 days spread across ten months. The judges handled about 22 cases a day, roughly six minutes each, and took two food breaks: a snack in the morning and a longer lunch.

The authors had set out to test an old caricature, that justice is whatever the judge ate for breakfast. The lawyers waited in a separate room and could not watch the hearings, and the judge decided when the break came.

The authors split each day into the stretches between breaks. At the start of a stretch, about 65 percent of rulings were favourable. By the end of the stretch the figure had dropped to nearly zero. After the break it jumped back to about 65 percent. It looked like a tired mind running out of fuel, and the finding fitted neatly beside Baumeister's ego depletion work, which is why Tierney and others repeated it so widely.

Then the doubts came in. Two other researchers, Keren Weinshall-Margel and John Shapard, wrote to the same journal that the order of cases is not random. According to summaries of their letter, prisoners without a lawyer usually go last in a session, and such prisoners are less likely to be granted parole. The original authors themselves wrote that the order was set, with rare exceptions, by when each prisoner's attorney arrived.

In 2016 Andreas Glöckner ran simulations of a judge who plans ahead. If favourable rulings take longer and the judge wants to finish before the break, cases likely to be granted would be pushed behind it, and the pattern could appear with no weakening of anyone's mind. In 2017 Daniël Lakens added a blunter argument: an effect that large, dropping from 65 percent to zero and back, is implausible for a human mood.

Nobody has shown that a sandwich does not matter. What the critics show is that the same numbers can come from a queue. A real timetable of lawyers, files and breaks may produce what looks like fatigue, and the dispute over which story is right is still open.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before blaming people for a pattern, check how the queue was built.** A clean curve in the data can come from the order in which work arrives rather than from anything the worker does. If a support team's replies seem worse late in the afternoon, look at whether the hardest tickets are saved for last before you decide the team is tired. The same trap catches anyone who reads a daily chart, a weekly report or a monthly average. Once you have checked the order, you can ask the human question, whether it is rest, food or workload, with some chance of getting the right answer.$$

In a room where an agreement has just come apart, the Treasury Secretary asks his opponents not to tell the press how badly it went. Then he gets down on one knee in front of them, to lighten the mood. Four days later, the biggest rescue request in the country's history loses a vote on the House floor.

[[story]]
Andrew Ross Sorkin's *Too Big to Fail* follows the weeks in which Treasury Secretary Henry Paulson and Fed Chairman Ben Bernanke asked Congress for a rescue of the financial system. On 19 September 2008 they met congressional leaders and asked for legislation. Over that weekend an unofficial draft of about three pages circulated. It would let the Treasury buy up to $700 billion of mortgage-related assets outstanding at any one time, and it said the Secretary's decisions could not be reviewed by a court or an administrative agency, according to a Congressional Research Service analysis.

On Thursday 25 September President Bush gathered John McCain, Barack Obama and congressional leaders at the White House to show a united front. The meeting broke up badly. House Republican leader John Boehner voiced concerns about the emerging plan and asked that conservatives' alternative be considered. Barney Frank reacted angrily. According to press accounts, Paulson asked the Democrats not to disclose how poorly it had gone, and then got down on one knee to lighten the mood. Senator Richard Shelby said the announced agreement "is, obviously, no agreement."

Monday 29 September brought the vote. The House rejected the bill 205 to 228. Democrats voted 140 to 95 in favor, and Republicans 65 to 133 against. Leaders held the vote open for another 20 to 25 minutes to try to turn votes, and failed. The Dow fell about 700 points while the votes were being counted and closed down 777.68 points, at 10,365.45. A White House spokesman said the president was "very disappointed."

Four days later, on 3 October, the House passed a revised bill 263 to 171, after the Senate had amended it. The law created two bodies to watch over the program, a Financial Stability Oversight Board and a Congressional Oversight Panel.

Look at the order of events. The leaders had announced a deal, and a White House meeting had broken it apart within days. When the full House voted, more than ninety members of the president's opposition party sided with the Republican rebels against a plan their own leaders backed. The first draft asked for a very large sum and very little review. The version that finally passed named the people who would check on how the money was spent.

[[lessons]]
$$**Present the checks together with the request.** A large ask is easier to approve when the person deciding can see who will review it, how often and with what power to stop it. A manager asking for a big budget for a new project gets a better hearing by bringing a short review schedule and a point at which the money can be pulled back. Offering the limits first shows that you have thought about being wrong, and it saves the decision-maker from having to ask. It also moves the conversation from whether you can be trusted to how the arrangement will work, which is a question you can answer with a plan.$$

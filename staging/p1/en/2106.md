A general holds a signed order that will set a million men moving toward the frontier. Before it goes out, his emperor calls him back to the room. A telegram from London has arrived, and it seems to offer a way out of the war everyone has spent years preparing for, the one fought on two fronts at once.

[[story]]
On 1 August 1914, General Helmuth von Moltke, chief of the German General Staff, was carrying the signed order to mobilise the German army. In *The Guns of August*, Barbara Tuchman describes what followed: the Kaiser recalled him, and the plan he had spent years refining was suddenly up for discussion.

The reason was a telegram from Prince Lichnowsky, the German ambassador in London. He reported that Britain's foreign secretary, Sir Edward Grey, would guarantee French neutrality. Grey had said nothing of the kind. He had asked a vague, hypothetical question, and the ambassador had read it as an offer. Wilhelm, relieved to see a war on one front instead of two, told Moltke that Germany could now fight Russia alone and send the whole army east.

Moltke refused. By his own account, written in November 1914, he told the Kaiser that an army of a million men could not be turned around from west to east, and that plans worked out in minute detail over years could not be scrapped at a moment's notice. In Tuchman's version, he said the war plan could not be altered. The Kaiser gave way, and the mobilisation went ahead as written.

There is a catch, and later historians have pointed to it. An alternative deployment against Russia alone, called Aufmarsch II Ost, had existed on paper. Moltke himself had set it aside in 1913, and, as one historian notes, he did not mention it to the Kaiser that night. The one-front option was not impossible in principle. It had been dropped, with no way back.

The plan he did have had been altered before. Schlieffen's memorandum of 1905 called for a vast sweep through the Netherlands, Belgium and Luxembourg, and Moltke had left the Netherlands out and strengthened the German left in Alsace-Lorraine. So he had reshaped the plan more than once over the years, and yet in the decisive hour he spoke of it as untouchable. A plan with only one branch tends to be defended that way. Even good news from the outside, like the telegram from London, arrives as a threat to the plan instead of a chance.

According to one account, he left the room shaken. Britain's ultimatum over Belgium followed within days, and German armies marched in anyway.

[[lessons]]
$$**A plan with only one branch turns every surprise into a crisis, so keep a second option alive and rehearsed while the first one runs.** A caterer who has planned an outdoor wedding for months learns the night before that rain is coming. If the plan has no indoor version, a forecast she should have welcomed as a warning becomes a disaster for the whole day. A fallback only needs to exist, be written down and be walked through once by your team. Ask of any plan you are proud of: what would we do if the main route closed tomorrow, and who would know?$$

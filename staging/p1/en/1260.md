[[open]]
A stack of sealed letters sits on a desk. Some of them may carry real news. The man they are addressed to is Napoleon, and his secretary has instructions not to open them. Not today. Not next week either. The letters will wait for about three weeks. What happens to them in that time is the whole point.

[[story]]
The story comes from Louis Antoine Fauvelet de Bourrienne, Napoleon's secretary, who wrote about his years working for him. In 1850, Ralph Waldo Emerson retold it in an essay on Napoleon, and it has been passed along ever since.

According to Bourrienne's account, Napoleon had a standing instruction about his mail. Incoming letters were to be left unopened for about three weeks.

It is worth pausing on how strange that is. This was not a man with a quiet life and a few bills to pay. He was running an empire. Letters in his world could carry reports, requests, complaints and real news. Leaving them sealed looks, at first glance, like negligence.

Then the letters were finally opened.

##By the time anyone read them, a large share of the matters had already resolved themselves and no longer needed a reply.##

Whatever had prompted them had been dealt with, or had simply passed. The delay had done a job no clerk could do: it sorted the pile for him.

Ryan Holiday picks up this practice in *Stillness Is the Key*, in a part of the book about limiting inputs. His argument is that much of what arrives marked urgent is not actually important. If you respond to everything the moment it shows up, your mind fills up with other people's priorities. You spend the day reacting, and there is no room left to think about what really matters.

The obvious objection is that some things truly can't wait. Holiday doesn't suggest ignoring genuinely important matters. The delay in Napoleon's story is not a trash can. It is a filter. Whatever still needed attention after three weeks was still sitting there, now easy to see because the noise around it had cleared. The important letters did not disappear. They were the ones left standing.

That is the quiet logic of the practice. Answering immediately treats every message as equally urgent, and so it gives the trivial the same weight as the serious. Waiting lets time do the ranking. What resolves on its own did not need you. What comes back probably does.

You don't need an empire, or three weeks, to recognize the pattern. Most of us have answered a message in a rush, only to find an hour later that the question had already been settled without us.

[[lessons]]
$$**Time is a filter.** A large share of Napoleon's correspondence no longer needed an answer once the delay had passed. Many things that feel urgent on arrival shrink or settle when they are left alone for a while. Waiting is not the same as doing nothing. It lets events do some of the sorting you would otherwise do by hand.$$

$$**Instant replies hand over your agenda.** Holiday's point about limiting inputs is that every immediate response lets someone else decide what you think about next. Answer everything as it lands, and your day is assembled from other people's priorities. Choosing when to respond is one of the simplest ways to take some of that back.$$

$$**What matters comes back.** The letters that still needed Napoleon after three weeks were the ones that truly needed him. Delay did not bury the important matters. It made them easier to spot, because the smaller ones had fallen away. A short wait can reveal priorities instead of hiding them.$$

- Think of something that felt urgent last week. Did it still matter a few days later?
- Who in your life expects an instant answer, and have you ever checked whether they actually need one?

An electric sports-car company has let a quarter of its staff go in the last months of the year. The founder who has just taken over as chief executive is chasing a funding round at the same time. Before the year is out, 40 million dollars of debt will land in the account, and that money will help decide whether the company survives.

[[story]]
By the start of 2009, Tesla had delivered 147 cars. It had also raised 187 million dollars, and according to Bloomberg, 70 million of that came from Musk's own pocket. Those two numbers, one small and one large, show how far spending had run ahead of sales: 147 cars delivered against 187 million dollars raised is a lopsided ratio.

Musk told Bloomberg that the company's burn rate was "out of control" in 2007. In January 2008, after a review by the new chief executive, Ze'ev Drori, the company cut about ten percent of its workforce, by Musk's account, and several early key people left. In October 2008 Musk replaced Drori as CEO, and published accounts say a quarter of the employees were let go around then. It was a hard decision, made in plain sight of everyone who stayed. A company asking for new money looks more credible when it can show it has already cut its own costs, and here the staff shrank in two steps, roughly a tenth and then a quarter.

December 2008 brought the fifth funding round. It was structured as debt and brought in 40 million dollars; accounts say it helped the company avoid bankruptcy. Walter Isaacson's *Elon Musk* is a biography of the founder of SpaceX and Tesla, and this was the stretch in which both of his companies were short of money.

Time had been bought, though the crisis was not over. In May 2009 Daimler took a stake of under ten percent, reportedly for about 50 million dollars. In June 2009 the US Department of Energy approved a 465 million dollar loan for Tesla, which the company repaid in May 2013 with 12 million dollars in interest.

What matters is that the 40 million dollars was never the whole answer. It was enough to carry the company to the next door. Looking back, the money reads like a bridge: costs were cut first, then the founder's own money and a loan arrived, and months later Daimler and the Energy Department followed. Each step bought just enough time for the next, and the 465 million dollar loan could only be discussed because the company was still there to discuss it. For a firm in trouble, the question is rarely how much money it needs in total, but how long it has to survive until the next opening.

[[lessons]]
$$**Size your money to the bridge that reaches the next milestone, not to the whole dream.** When cash is shrinking week by week, the goal is not to fix everything but to reach the next door, whether that is a contract, a loan or a product delivery. Cutting your own costs first and hard strengthens your hand when you ask others for money, because whoever is listening looks at what you did before they decide to trust you. Tesla's 40 million dollars at the end of 2008 carried it to those later doors, and money taken as a bridge is gone quickly if it gets spent like a permanent fix.$$

[[open]]
Three open boats lie close together on the Pacific, and the captain and both mates have climbed into one of them. They have two books of navigation tables and no charts. The books give them a short list of places where land might be, and each name on it carries a cost.

[[story]]
In *In the Heart of the Sea*, Nathaniel Philbrick follows the crew of the Essex after the whale sank their ship. This episode comes from the account of the first mate, Owen Chase. On 22 November 1820 the captain, George Pollard, called the mates into his boat to decide where twenty men should sail, and Chase wrote down how they weighed it.

The tables showed the nearest land to be the Marquesas Islands. The Society Islands came next. Of those, Chase says, the crew knew nothing at all. If anyone lived there, they assumed the worst of them, and they feared those unknown islanders as much as the sea itself. The captain added another worry: it was hurricane season near the Sandwich Islands, so steering that way looked unsafe.

##So they set a course away from every island within reach, south to about 25 or 26 degrees, to find the variable winds and then work east toward the coast of Chile or Peru.##

That plan had a logic. They expected to pick up variable winds in the south that would carry them east, with a known coastline at the end. It also meant the whole voyage would be made in three crowded boats on six hundred pounds of bread.

Another account exists, written much later. Thomas Nickerson, who had been the youngest on board, wrote his version in 1876. He says Pollard's first wish was to make for the Society Islands, which lay to the southwest and downwind. According to him, the officers argued for the route along the coast instead, and Pollard, not wanting to oppose two against one, gave way reluctantly. Nickerson adds that the boats could have landed in ten days at the farthest, probably without losing a man.

The two writers differ on who argued for what. They agree on the choice, though, and Chase's own words show how it was made: about the Society Islands the council was, in his phrase, entirely ignorant. Nobody could say what lay there, so the word "unknown" quietly took on the weight of the worst case.

When a name on a list is filled in with an assumption, the assumption starts to vote. Here it voted for the longer, harder passage, without anyone having checked what it was based on.

[[lessons]]
$$**Check what you fear before you let it choose for you.** A new hire might skip the one department that could answer her question because she has heard it is difficult, when a single short message would show her whether that is true, and in the meantime she loses a week. Fear fills the gap wherever facts are missing, and it speaks with the same confidence as knowledge. Next time you rule out an option, take a minute to write down one thing you actually know about it and one thing you are only assuming, and see which list is longer.$$

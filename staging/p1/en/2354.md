Before she filled in a single job application, a writer in Key West had settled three rules for herself. Her car keys were in her pocket, and she had already decided what she would never go without. The rules were about what to leave at home, and the pay was still to come.

[[story]]
In *Nickel and Dimed*, Barbara Ehrenreich asks how a woman leaving welfare could live on six or seven dollars an hour. She was a successful writer, and Lewis Lapham, the editor of *Harper's*, urged her to find out firsthand. The experiment began as an article in that magazine in January 1999 and grew into the book of 2001. The question was simple.

She gave herself three rules. She would not fall back on any skill from her education or her writing career, however much a job might invite it. She would take the highest-paying job she was offered and do it as well as she could. She would live in the cheapest housing she could find.

The rules came with limits, and published accounts of the book list them. She always had a car, she never became homeless, and she never went without food. She was also allowed a small reserve of starter cash, which a review in Salon mentions. The same accounts add that she did not always follow her own rules. Even a careful plan has leaks, and she says where they are.

Then she went to work. In Key West, Florida, she waited tables at two restaurants and cleaned hotel rooms. In Portland, Maine, she worked weekends as a dietary aide in a nursing home and weekdays for a house-cleaning service, scrubbing floors with a crew. In Minneapolis, she took a job as a sales clerk at Wal-Mart. Six jobs, three cities.

She planned to stay about a month in each place, a stretch long enough to hold a job and short enough to leave. That is a modest frame, one writer and three cities, and nobody could mistake it for a census of the working poor.

Look at what each rule does. The first strips away her advantages, so the pay she earns is the pay a person without them would earn. The second stops her from picking an easy job, and the third stops her from living better than the wage allows. Each rule closes a door through which anyone could have said afterwards that the test was not fair.

The limits are just as plain. A car, a meal and a roof are the floor under the experiment, and she says where they are instead of pretending they do not exist. An experiment that admits its exceptions is easier to trust than one that claims to have none.

[[lessons]]
$$**Write your rules before the test begins, and write the exceptions down with them.** Anyone who tries a new diet, a month of early mornings or a different way of running a team tends to decide the rules halfway through, and then every result can be explained away. Fix in advance what you will not use, what you will accept and where you will stop, and the outcome means something afterwards. Naming the exceptions at the start also lets you see later, without excuses, which of them you actually used. A rule you can check is worth more than a resolution you can only feel.$$

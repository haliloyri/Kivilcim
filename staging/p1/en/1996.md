[[open]]
A surgeon tells a man that his prostate cancer can reasonably be watched rather than cut out, because such tumors grow very slowly. The man nods and goes home to talk it over. A few days later he is back in the surgeon's office, asking for the operation.

[[story]]
Charles Duhigg tells this story in *Supercommunicators* (2024), and the surgeon is Behfar Ehdaie of Memorial Sloan Kettering in New York. He treated prostate cancer, and for some of his patients he told them that doing nothing was a reasonable choice. He explained why: these tumors grow very slowly.

Patients usually heard him out politely, went home, talked it over with their spouses, and returned only a few days later asking for surgery. He had given them correct information and sound advice, and they were walking straight past it, which puzzled him more each time it happened.

Professors from Harvard Business School told him what was wrong. He was opening the conversation with advice before he knew what the patient needed. His patients, in Duhigg's account, were not ignoring him. They were having an emotional conversation while he was having a practical one.

So he changed his first minute. He stopped starting with the facts and asked a single deep question, in effect, what the diagnosis meant to the person in front of him. Some answers had little to do with tumors. One patient told him that his own father had died when he was a child, and that he did not want to put his kids through the same thing. No statistic about slow-growing tumors could answer a worry that was about his children.

Ehdaie found that the answers sorted patients into two groups. Those who replied in an emotional frame needed to be heard first, and only then could they take in practical advice. Those who replied in a practical frame wanted information, and he could simply give it to them. The same sentence about slow-growing tumors, spoken after the right question, landed differently.

The medicine did not change, and neither did his facts. What changed was the order. He learned what kind of conversation the patient was in, and then he answered that one.

It did not always work on the first try. According to published accounts of his approach, he often had to put the same basic question to a patient several times, in different words, before the person said what was really on his mind.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask what it means to them before you give advice.** Say a friend tells you he is thinking about quitting his job, and if you start listing pros and cons at once, you may be answering a practical question while he is asking an emotional one about fear or pride. One open question at the start, such as what the decision means to him, shows you which kind of help he wants. If the first answer is polite and vague, ask again in other words, since people often need a second invitation. Then your advice has a place to land.$$

Look at who runs their first marathon and a strange cluster appears. An unusual number of first-timers are 29, 39 or 49. Nothing about the race changes at those ages. The distance is the same, the training is the same. Something about the number itself seems to get people to the starting line.

[[story]]
Daniel Pink tells this story in *When*, drawing on research by Adam Alter and Hal Hershfield. They looked at the ages of marathon runners and found that people whose age ended in 9 were overrepresented among first-time marathoners by 48 percent. Pink gives this group a name: the "9-enders."

The pattern didn't stop with beginners. Among people who had run marathons before, 9-enders also tended to run faster at those ages than in the years just before or after.

Pink's explanation is that approaching a symbolic ending energizes people. Turning 40 is just another birthday. But the end of a decade feels like a line, and seeing a line ahead makes people want to act before they cross it. A 39-year-old signing up for a first marathon is responding to that line, not to anything that has changed in their legs.

Endings aren't the only point in time with this kind of pull. Pink also looks at the middle.

He cites Connie Gersick's research on project teams. She found that teams often did little in the first half of their time. Then, near the midpoint of their deadline, came a burst of activity. The halfway mark acted as an alarm: time is running out, and we need to move.

Pink adds a result from sports. In a study of basketball games, teams trailing by one point at halftime were more likely to win than teams leading by one. Being slightly behind at the break seems to have sharpened them, where being slightly ahead didn't.

##A midpoint can slump us or spark us, and Pink's argument is that we can choose which.##

Pink sees the middle of anything, a project, a game, a year, as a moment with two possible effects. Left alone, it can drift into a lull. Noticed, it can work like the half-time buzzer, a signal to wake up and change pace.

His practical advice follows from that. Set interim goals, so there is a nearer line to aim at than the final deadline. And treat the midpoint as a wake-up call rather than just another week.

Put the two halves of Pink's argument together and the marathon data and the project teams tell the same story. In each case, what seems to get people moving is noticing where they stand in time: halfway, a point behind, or one birthday away from a new decade.

[[lessons]]
$$**A symbolic ending can move people as much as a real one.** Nothing about a race changes at 29, 39 or 49, yet 9-enders were 48 percent overrepresented among first-time marathoners. An approaching line, even one that only exists on a calendar, makes people want to act before it passes.$$

$$**Being slightly behind at the halfway mark can help.** Basketball teams trailing by one point at halftime were more likely to win than teams leading by one. A small deficit at the midpoint seems to focus effort in a way that a small lead doesn't.$$

$$**The middle is a checkpoint you can use on purpose.** Gersick's teams often did little until their midpoint, then surged. You don't have to wait for that alarm to go off by itself. Marking the halfway point in advance and setting interim goals turns it into a planned moment to change pace.$$

[[reflect]]
- Have you ever made a big decision because a birthday or the end of a year was approaching?
- In a long project, where do you usually lose momentum?
- Which of your current projects is nearest its halfway point, even if nobody has said so?

&&For one project that's drifting, could you mark its halfway point on the calendar, plan a short check-in for that day, and notice whether it changes the pace?&&

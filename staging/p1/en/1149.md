Every year, West Point admits some of the most carefully selected young people in the United States. Top grades, top scores, proven leaders, strong athletes. And every year, some of them quit before their first summer is over. For a long time, nobody could predict which ones.

[[story]]
Angela Duckworth opens *Grit* at the United States Military Academy at West Point.

Getting in is brutally hard. Applicants need excellent grades and test scores, a nomination, usually from a member of Congress, and evidence of leadership and athletic ability. West Point folds all of this into a single number called the **Whole Candidate Score**. It combines SAT or ACT scores, high school class rank, expert assessments of leadership potential, and measures of physical fitness. It is, in other words, a very thorough measure of talent.

Then the admitted cadets arrive for their first summer, and they meet **Beast Barracks**.

Beast is seven weeks of intense training designed to push new cadets past their limits, physically, mentally and socially. Days start before dawn. There's constant physical strain, little sleep, and very little room for mistakes. Some of these carefully chosen cadets decide it's not for them and leave.

Here is the puzzle that interested Duckworth. West Point had put enormous effort into selecting its cadets. If the Whole Candidate Score was measuring the right things, the cadets with the highest scores should be the ones most likely to make it through.

##They weren't. The Whole Candidate Score couldn't predict who would get through Beast.##

Duckworth, a psychologist, had developed a short questionnaire she called the **Grit Scale**. It asks people how they approach long-term goals: whether they finish what they start, whether setbacks discourage them, whether their interests stay steady or change every few months. It measures, in her words, passion and perseverance for long-term goals.

She gave it to incoming cadets before Beast began. Then she waited to see who stayed.

The grit scores did what the admissions data couldn't. Cadets with higher grit were more likely to complete Beast. And there was a second surprise. Grit had almost nothing to do with the Whole Candidate Score. The grittiest cadets weren't necessarily the most talented, and the most talented weren't necessarily the grittiest.

For Duckworth, that was the beginning of years of research. Talent, she concluded, is real and it matters. But it isn't the same thing as the ability to keep going when things get hard. And it was that second quality that decided who was still standing at the end of the summer.

[[lessons]]
$$**Talent predicts who gets in, not who stays.** The Whole Candidate Score was excellent at picking impressive applicants. It could not tell West Point who would quit during Beast. Being capable enough to begin something is a different question from whether you'll keep going when it becomes miserable.$$

$$**Grit is its own ingredient.** Duckworth's short Grit Scale, measuring passion and perseverance for long-term goals, predicted who finished Beast. That quality doesn't show up in test scores or class rank. It shows up when you're tired, behind and tempted to leave.$$

$$**Talent and grit don't always travel together.** One of the most surprising findings was that grit was barely related to the talent measures. Assuming the most gifted person will also be the most persistent is a mistake. The quiet cadet with average scores might be the one still standing in August.$$

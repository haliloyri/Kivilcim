The train slides toward downtown Chicago in the grey of early morning. Nearly everyone aboard has earbuds in, eyes on a phone, shoulders turned to the window. Beside each of them sits a stranger. Each person has quietly decided that the best commute is the one with no conversation in it.

[[story]]
In 2014 two researchers from the University of Chicago Booth School of Business, Nicholas Epley and Juliana Schroeder, published a paper called "Mistakenly Seeking Solitude". It reported nine experiments, some run in the lab and some in the field. In the field the participants were ordinary commuters on trains and buses, including people riding a train into downtown Chicago.

The design was simple. Each rider was given one of three instructions: connect with a stranger, sit in solitude, or do whatever you normally would. When the ride was over, they filled in a survey about how it had felt. Beforehand the expectation was clear. Participants predicted that talking to a stranger would give them a worse trip than sitting alone.

The result went the other way. The riders who had connected with a stranger reported the most positive experience of the three groups, and a significantly more positive one than the riders who had sat in solitude. People had been wrong about their own commute, and wrong in a consistent direction.

According to David Brooks, in an interview with the Templeton Foundation, the idea grew out of Epley's own daily train ride. Epley noticed that the people around him seemed happiest when they were talking to one another. Brooks adds that introverts and extroverts alike benefited from the conversation, and that he now starts talking to people on planes and trains instead of putting in his headphones.

The odd part is not that talking helped. It is that nobody saw it coming. The cost of a conversation is easy to picture: the awkward opening, the chance of being brushed off, the long ride with someone who will not stop. The reward is almost impossible to picture in advance, because it only exists once the talking has started. So we judge the choice on the part we can imagine, and silence wins every morning.

That is why the experiment needed an instruction. Left to themselves, riders chose the habit they had always chosen. Only when they were asked to try the other thing did the evidence show up. Each of them was then a small proof that the guess had been wrong, and the guess was the only thing standing between them and a better ride.

[[lessons]]
$$**Test the conversation you expect to dislike, because your forecast of it is usually too gloomy.** Before a long train ride, a coffee queue or the first minute of a meeting, you probably predict that silence will be more comfortable. The study suggests that guess leans the wrong way, so treat it as a hypothesis to check rather than a fact. Try one real question this week, such as "What takes you into the city today?", and see how the ride feels. One small trial can correct a belief that years of habit never questioned, and it costs you only a minute of mild awkwardness.$$

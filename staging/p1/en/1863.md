A mountain in Georgia stands apart from the hills around it, and its Cherokee name is usually translated as "stands alone." A hundred and forty raw recruits gather at its foot several times a week to run the same route, over the same distance, against the same clock. What that repetition did for a unit is still talked about today.

[[story]]
Stephen E. Ambrose's 1992 book *Band of Brothers* is about Easy Company, and the company's story begins long before the fighting, in America. The first Easy, formed at Camp Toccoa in Georgia in 1942, held a hundred and forty men. The best-known part of its training was a run up the mountain that rises beside the camp, Currahee.

Currahee means "stands alone" in Cherokee. By the usual telling, the men ran three miles up and three miles back down. That makes six miles, about nine and a half kilometres. Sources say the run came several times a week, and that the aim was to finish in under fifty minutes.

Work out the pace and it comes to a little better than eight and a half minutes a mile, held on the slope of a mountain. But the numbers matter less for their difficulty than for their fixedness. The mountain was the same mountain, the distance was the same distance, and the measure was the clock. A man who ran well one morning was measured against the same line the next. Every run could be compared with the one before.

That arrangement had a second effect: everyone ran the same road. One man's bad day happened in front of the man beside him, and no one could hide his speed, because the clock wrote the result. Trust between people builds in a setting like that, not through big speeches, but through doing the same work side by side, again and again.

A hundred and forty men on the same slope on the same morning was the plainest experience the company shared. Inside it, nobody's rank, background or talent for talking could change the measure, because the measure was the mountain and the watch. Today that route is a memorial trail carrying Colonel Sink's name. Easy's later battle stories are far better known, but they rest on this ordinary, fixed repetition. The camp is gone and the mountain remains.

One detail in the way the route is remembered varies by source: how much of the three-mile refrain is literal and how much comes from the later television series is not settled. The basic arrangement is clear all the same. One mountain, one distance, one frequency, a hundred and forty men.

[[lessons]]
$$**Pick one measurable route and run it again and again.** A team or a person who can follow progress against the same yardstick starts to see improvement instead of merely feeling it. A teacher who times a class's reading speed on the same short text every Monday can compare where the children stumble this week with last week. A fixed measure puts everyone's eyes on the same line and moves the discussion from opinion to a number. The hard part is returning to it unchanged, because once the measure shifts, progress goes back to being a feeling.$$

A man is facing seven years for an assault. He decides the way out is to play mad, and he borrows his act from a film character. Months later, behind the locked door of a secure ward, he works out that the plan has run in exactly the wrong direction.

[[story]]
Jon Ronson's *The Psychopath Test* begins with a man called Tony. By Ronson's account it was Scientologists who led him to Tony, a man held in Broadmoor, the high-security hospital in England. Tony had been convicted of grievous bodily harm and was expecting a seven-year sentence.

Tony says he faked madness to avoid it. He built the act on the Dennis Hopper character in the film *Blue Velvet*. On paper it worked, because he was sent to Broadmoor instead of prison. But the ward was a unit for patients considered to have a severe personality disorder.

Once inside, Tony saw that his plan had backfired. A prison term has an end date; this had none. So he began arguing the opposite case: that he was normal, that he had been acting, that he had never been ill. The hospital classified him as a psychopath, using Robert Hare's psychopathy checklist.

Ronson also spoke to Professor Antony Maden, Broadmoor's head clinician, who stood by the classification. In Maden's view, faking madness was exactly the kind of deceitful, manipulative act you would expect from a psychopath. Tony's claim to be sane went into the file as a finding.

Maden's portrait was more mixed. He said Tony scored very high on some psychopathic traits: he never took responsibility, he blamed others, he lied. Yet in Maden's description Tony did not set out to do serious harm for its own sake.

Tony kept appealing to the tribunals that review such detentions. Ronson reports that this went on for about fourteen years and at first got him nowhere. In the end he won an appeal and was due to be freed.

Tony also told Ronson that everyone is a bit psychopathic, the author included. Ronson wondered whether the main difference between a psychopath in Broadmoor and one on Wall Street might be the kind of home each grew up in.

The trap had a simple shape. If Tony said he was ill, he was ill. If he said he was well, that was the deceit. Whichever way the evidence pointed, it led to the same conclusion, and Tony had nothing left to break the circle. Ronson found him charming. For the people on the other side of the door, the arithmetic was simple: a checklist, a label, and then years.

[[lessons]]
$$**Distrust any judgment that no counter-evidence could ever change.** Picture a manager who says a colleague is always defensive, and who reads every objection the colleague raises as one more proof of it. A verdict that survives every possible answer has stopped listening to the person it describes. When you form a view of someone, ask which observation would prove you wrong; if you cannot name one, you are holding a habit rather than a judgment. From there you can go looking for a different kind of evidence, such as what the person does on a good day, or what they do when nobody is watching them.$$

[[story]]
The question of how to treat psychopaths, which runs through Jon Ronson's *The Psychopath Test*, leads to Oak Ridge, a maximum-security psychiatric unit at Penetanguishene in Ontario, Canada. In 1965 a newly qualified psychiatrist named Elliott Barker arrived there with a plan to combine coercive techniques with encounter groups run by the patients. According to accounts of the program, it drew on the "therapeutic community" model of Maxwell Jones, in which patients take an active part in their own treatment.

The program became known as the Social Therapy Unit. Canada's federal health department supplied Barker with LSD, and patients were given other drugs as well. The best-known feature was the "Total Encounter Capsule": a windowless, padded room, lights on around the clock, food passed through a tube. According to CBC, groups of prisoners were locked in for up to ten days. The programs ran for more than fifteen years and ended in 1983.

Researchers later asked what the program had achieved. In 1992 Rice, Harris and Cormier published a study in *Law and Human Behavior*. They compared patients who had spent at least two years in the program with matched patients who had not, and followed them for more than ten years on average. Whether a man counted as a psychopath was decided with Hare's checklist.

The result ran against what had been hoped. For men who were not psychopaths, treatment was linked to lower rates of reoffending, violent reoffending above all. For men the checklist classed as psychopaths, violent reoffending was higher. Later reviews report that the authors concluded this was the wrong type of program for serious psychopathic offenders.

In June 2020 a judge of the Ontario Superior Court ruled for the 28 former patients who had brought a lawsuit. The judge found the treatment amounted to assault and battery and that the defendants had breached their fiduciary duties. Eight of the plaintiffs had died before the judgment. Compensation came to about $10 million plus interest, with individual awards from $1,000 to $2.7 million.

What stands out is how long a method built with good intentions went on without anyone measuring it. When it was finally measured, the results pointed in opposite directions for two groups of patients.

[[lessons]]
$$**Measure a well-meant program separately for each group it touches before you scale it.** Say a company starts open, blunt feedback sessions for every team. Some employees speak up for the first time, while others go quiet, and an average of the two hides both, so before you extend a practice, write down how the people you most wanted to help are doing, and ask them directly, even if nobody asked at the start. That way you find out whom the good intention actually served, in names and numbers. Sometimes that means dropping the practice, and sometimes it only tells you for whom it needs to change.$$

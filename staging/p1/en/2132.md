A Harvard lecturer is gone from the campus in the middle of the semester. Then word comes that he has told a television talk show the university fired him. A colleague goes to the president's office to report his absence and find out what the file says.

[[story]]
Timothy Leary and his colleague Richard Alpert ran psilocybin research at Harvard's Center for Research in Personality in the early 1960s. Their story is part of the background to *How to Change Your Mind*. This episode is about the end of it, and about how many versions of that end exist.

Trouble had been building for more than a year. According to the Harvard Crimson, colleagues in the Center disputed in March 1962 whether the pair should continue. Herbert Kelman said that "this work violates the values of the academic community." In 1962 Leary and Alpert pledged not to give the drugs to undergraduates, and the Massachusetts Food and Drug Division ruled that they could be given only in the presence of a physician.

In spring 1963 the situation broke open. By the Crimson's later account, Leary left Harvard for Hollywood in the middle of the semester, and President Pusey then granted him leave without pay, though his contract was due to expire at the end of the term anyway. The Crimson reported at the time that Leary was relieved of his teaching duties and his salary suspended for "absenting himself from the University without permission." Alpert's case was different and clearer. Harvard announced that the Corporation had terminated his appointment for giving a hallucinogenic drug to an undergraduate, in violation of an agreement with the university. It was, the Crimson noted, the first dismissal in Pusey's ten years at Harvard.

For years afterwards Leary said Harvard had fired him. Maher, the Harvard figure who had reported Leary's absence to Pusey after hearing the television claim, denied that Leary's position was ended because of the experiments. The Crimson, writing in 1996, said the circumstances remained a matter of debate.

What makes the episode useful is the gap between a dramatic story and a plain file. Leary's version, in which Harvard fired him, is stronger as a story. The paperwork says something smaller: unpermitted absence, leave without pay, an expiring contract. Both versions sit inside more than a year of open dispute about the project. The rest depends on whose account you read first, and a story told often enough starts to feel like a record.

[[lessons]]
$$**When your own account of an exit or a setback is more dramatic than the paperwork, check the paperwork before you retell it.** Leary told one version for years, while the record shows leave without pay and a contract about to expire. It is easy to improve a story about yourself, because every retelling makes the heroic version a little easier to believe. Before you repeat your version of why a project, a job or a relationship ended, read the actual emails, dates and decisions. You may find the real story is smaller, and a smaller true story is a better thing to build on.$$

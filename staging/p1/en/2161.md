A former college football coach sits down with the founders of some of the biggest companies in Silicon Valley. He has been a chief executive himself, and he has a habit of steering the conversation away from the founder's own brilliance. What he keeps pointing at is the people in the next room.

[[story]]
Bill Campbell coached Columbia's football team from 1974 to 1979, then moved into business. He ran Apple's Claris software unit and was chief executive of the software maker Intuit from 1994 to 1998. Over the years he coached some of the best-known figures in technology, among them Larry Page and Sergey Brin of Google, Jeff Bezos of Amazon and Steve Jobs of Apple. He died in 2016.

Liz Wiseman, author of *Multipliers* with Greg McKeown, worked with Campbell and points to him as an example. According to her account, Campbell said he began his career as one of the great Diminishers. He told everyone what to do, micromanaged, and stayed in the details of other people's business.

Then, as she retells it, a member of his team confronted him. In that telling, the confrontation was the wake-up call. Campbell recognized the effect he had on the people around him and changed the way he led. Wiseman says he went on to become a Multiplier, and then what she calls a "multiplier of multipliers."

The second part is why the story matters in Silicon Valley. As Wiseman tells it, Campbell said he was trying to teach the founders he coached, including Bezos, Page and Brin, to stop being the lone genius. He wanted them to draw on the intelligence of their teams, the very thing he had once failed to do himself.

Wiseman draws a specific conclusion from him. A leader who starts out as a Diminisher can change for good, and she cites Campbell as proof that "real and lasting change is possible." The claim is about leaders who start in the worst place, not only those who start from strength.

Look at what Campbell's story contains. There is no moment of genius or sudden gift. There is a habit, the habit of telling people what to do, and one person willing to say so to his face. Everything after that is a person deciding to act differently.

The man who once micromanaged a team later tried to teach founders of some of the largest companies of their time to do the opposite, and the ordinary start of his own change makes that credible. A team member's direct remark mattered more than any program, at least in the way Wiseman retells it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask for one person on your team to tell you how you shut them down.** Bad habits in a leader survive because the people affected hardly ever say anything, and a boss with good intentions rarely sees the damage from the inside. Campbell's turning point, as Wiseman retells it, was one direct comment from a team member. Invite that comment on purpose, once a quarter, and treat the first answer as useful information rather than an insult. Habits that others can name are far easier to change than habits only you can see. That is also why one honest comment is worth more than a long training course.$$

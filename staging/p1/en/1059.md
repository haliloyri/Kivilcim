A surgeon was hired to teach professional cyclists how to wash their hands. The inside of the team truck was painted white. Every rider got the pillow and mattress that suited them best. None of it had anything to do with pedaling, and all of it was deliberate.

[[story]]
In 2003, British Cycling hired Dave Brailsford as its performance director. The job did not look promising. The team had a long record of mediocrity: since 1908, British riders had won a single Olympic gold medal, and in 110 years no British cyclist had won the Tour de France.

A history like that leaves no obvious single fix to hunt for. No secret training method or miracle bike was going to undo a century of losing. Brailsford built his approach on something he called *the aggregation of marginal gains*: find a margin of improvement of about 1 percent in everything the team did.

The obvious places came first. The bike seats were redesigned to be more comfortable. Alcohol went onto the tires for better grip. Riders wore electrically heated overshorts to keep their muscles warm, and the team used biofeedback sensors and tested fabrics in a wind tunnel. It also noticed that the indoor racing suits were lighter and more aerodynamic than the outdoor ones, so riders started wearing the indoor suits outside.

Then the list moved to places other teams were not looking. They tested massage gels. They hired a surgeon to teach riders the best way to wash their hands, so they would catch fewer colds. They worked out which pillow and mattress let each rider sleep best. And they painted the inside of the team truck white, because white made it easy to spot the bits of dust that could get into the bikes and wear them down.

##No single one of these changes was going to win a race. The bet was that hundreds of them might.##

Five years after Brailsford arrived, at the 2008 Beijing Olympics, British cycling won roughly 60 percent of the gold medals available. In London in 2012 the team set nine Olympic records and seven world records. That same year Bradley Wiggins became the first British cyclist to win the Tour de France, and Chris Froome won it in 2013, 2015, 2016 and 2017.

James Clear opens *Atomic Habits* with this story and adds some arithmetic. Get 1 percent better every day for a year and you end up about 37 times better. Get 1 percent worse every day and you sink almost to zero.

[[lessons]]
$$**Effort and payoff run on different clocks.** The seat and tire changes could not have moved any scoreboard in the first months. A program judged on its first season would have looked like a lot of fuss for no medals.$$

$$**The unclaimed gains sit where nobody is bothering.** Aerodynamics was crowded. Hand-washing and truck dust were empty. If everyone around you is competing over the obvious improvements, look at what seems too trivial to try.$$

$$**Clear's math is a model, not a forecast.** The 37 times figure comes from the same arithmetic that sends a daily 1 percent decline to nearly zero. Use it as a reason to take small slips seriously, not as a promise about any one habit.$$

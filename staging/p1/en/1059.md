Picture a coach whose job is to make his team the best in the world. You'd expect him to obsess over training plans and race tactics. He did. He also cared which pillow each rider slept on and what color the inside of the team truck was painted. It sounds absurd. It turned out to be the point.

[[story]]
In 2003, British Cycling hired Dave Brailsford as its performance director. It was not a job with much promise. The team had a long record of mediocrity. Since 1908, British riders had won just a single Olympic gold medal. In 110 years, no British cyclist had ever won the Tour de France.

A team with that history has no single big fix waiting to be found. There's no secret training method, no miracle bike that turns a century of losing into winning. So Brailsford went the other way. His strategy had a name:

> The aggregation of marginal gains.

The idea was simple. Look for a tiny margin of improvement, about 1 percent, in everything you do. Not one dramatic change. Hundreds of small ones.

The team started with the obvious places. They redesigned the bike seats to make them more comfortable. They rubbed alcohol on the tires for better grip. Riders wore electrically heated overshorts to keep their muscles warm. The team used biofeedback sensors, and they tested fabrics in a wind tunnel. They also noticed that their indoor racing suits were lighter and more aerodynamic than their outdoor kit, so riders started wearing the indoor suits outdoors.

Then they went further, into places nobody else was looking. They tested different massage gels. They hired a surgeon to teach riders the best way to wash their hands so they'd catch fewer colds. They worked out which pillow and mattress helped each rider sleep best. They even painted the inside of the team truck white, so they could spot the bits of dust that might otherwise get into the bikes and degrade them.

##None of these changes would win a race on its own. That was never the idea.##

The question was whether all of it could add up to anything. The answer came faster than almost anyone expected. Just five years after Brailsford arrived, at the 2008 Beijing Olympics, British cycling won roughly 60 percent of the gold medals available. At the 2012 London Olympics, the team set nine Olympic records and seven world records.

That same year, Bradley Wiggins became the first British cyclist ever to win the Tour de France. Then Chris Froome won it in 2013, 2015, 2016 and 2017. Between 2007 and 2017, British cyclists won 178 world championships, 66 Olympic or Paralympic gold medals and five Tour de France victories.

In *Atomic Habits*, James Clear uses this story to open the book, and he adds some simple math. If you get 1 percent better every day for a year, you end up about 37 times better. If you get 1 percent worse every day for a year, you decline nearly to zero. The same small number, pushed in opposite directions, gives you two completely different outcomes.

[[lessons]]
$$**Results lag, then arrive all at once.** British cycling spent almost a century with a single Olympic gold. Five years after Brailsford started stacking tiny gains, the team won roughly 60 percent of the cycling golds in Beijing. The early tweaks, like a comfier seat or better tire grip, wouldn't have shown up on any scoreboard. The effort came first. The payoff came later, and it was far bigger than any single change would suggest.$$

$$**The best gains hide in the boring places.** Any cycling team would think about aerodynamics. Very few would hire a surgeon to teach hand-washing, test pillows and mattresses, or paint a truck interior white to spot dust. Those overlooked details were where Brailsford found room nobody else had claimed. If everyone around you is fighting over the obvious improvements, the next ones are probably sitting somewhere that looks too trivial to bother with.$$

$$**The math works against you too.** Clear's numbers cut both ways: 1 percent better each day for a year makes you about 37 times better, and 1 percent worse brings you almost to zero. A rider who skips hand-washing once loses nothing noticeable. Skipping it all season is a different story. Small habits build decline just as quietly as they build success.$$

[[reflect]]
- Where are you holding out for one big breakthrough when a string of tiny adjustments would get you there?
- Think of your own version of the white truck interior. What small detail in your work or home have you never looked at closely?
- Which "unimportant" habit in your day is quietly compounding against you, the way skipped hand-washing might cost a rider a week to a cold?
- If you've been putting in effort with nothing to show for it, could you be in the early years of a five-year lag?

&&What is one thing you could do just 1 percent better today, and how will you notice it tomorrow?&&

[[story]]
In November 1973 the journal *Nature* printed a short paper called "The Logic of Animal Conflict". Its authors were the biologist John Maynard Smith and the American geneticist George Price. Richard Dawkins's *The Selfish Gene* (1976) belongs to the same line of thought, and this story is about the idea the paper introduced: the evolutionarily stable strategy, or ESS.

The puzzle was why so many animal contests end without a killing. Rival males often display, posture and back off. The old answer was that it benefits the species. Maynard Smith and Price asked a harder question: what keeps such restraint in place when an individual could gain by cheating?

They reasoned with strategies. A hawk always escalates and fights until it is hurt or the other retreats. A dove displays, and runs if the opponent escalates. Take the simplest version of the model, with a prize worth 50 points and an injury that costs 100. In a population of only hawks, every contest is a fight, and the average result is a loss of 25. A single dove would do better, because it retreats and scores zero. So hawks alone cannot last.

Doves alone fare better, and each gets 25 on average, since they share the prize. But one hawk arriving in that crowd wins every contest and takes the whole 50. So doves alone cannot last either.

What survives is a mix. When half the population plays hawk, a hawk and a dove both average 12.5, and neither can gain by switching. That equilibrium is the stable strategy. Notice the cost of it. Everyone would earn 25 in an all-dove world, yet the population settles at 12.5, because nobody can move to the better outcome alone. Stable does not mean best.

That distinction is what the paper gave biology. It explained how a population can sit at an outcome that is worse for everyone, simply because no individual gain is available from leaving it. The model also made predictions that could be checked against real populations. Biologists could now ask of any behaviour whether a rare cheat could invade it. It also depends on the numbers. If the injury were cheaper than the prize, hawks would take over completely.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you call a habit the best way, ask what keeps it in place and whether everyone would gain if all of you changed together.** In a department where every message is marked urgent, nobody can stop marking theirs urgent without being ignored, even though everyone would read calmer mail more carefully. The habit is stable because leaving it alone costs you, and that tells you nothing about whether it is good. When you see such a pattern, you can propose that the whole group changes on one agreed date, which removes the penalty for going first and shows each person that the others will follow.$$

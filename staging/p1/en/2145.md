[[open]]
A man walks into a bank in the middle of the afternoon with no mask, no hat and no sunglasses. His face is slick with lemon juice, and he walks straight past the security cameras. He is sure they cannot see him.

[[story]]
On 6 January 1995, a man pointed a handgun at a teller in the Mellon Bank branch in Swissvale, a suburb of Pittsburgh, while his partner waited in the queue. According to published accounts of the case, neither wore a mask. Both had rubbed lemon juice over their faces. The gunman was McArthur Wheeler, and he had been told that the juice would make him invisible to cameras.

Wheeler did not simply take that on faith. The same accounts say he photographed his own juice-covered face first, and the picture came out with no face in it. Why it came out that way is unknown. He read the blank picture as proof, and that afternoon he robbed a second bank as well.

The cameras recorded him perfectly well. On 19 April, a surveillance image of his face aired on a Pittsburgh Crime Stoppers segment during the eleven o'clock news. Tips came in, and police arrested him at ten past midnight, within an hour of the broadcast. Shown the pictures, Wheeler is reported to have said: "But I wore the lemon juice."

A short account of the case ran in the 1996 *World Almanac*, and a Cornell social psychologist named David Dunning came across it. Dunning and his graduate student Justin Kruger took a question from it. Do people who are bad at something also lack the ability to notice that they are bad at it?

They published their answer in 1999 in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, after four studies with undergraduates. Students took tests of logical reasoning, grammar and humour, then estimated how they had done. Those who scored in the bottom quarter landed around the 12th percentile, yet they placed themselves around the 62nd. The authors argued that the skills needed to do the task overlap with the skills needed to judge it, so a weak performer has no good instrument for seeing the weakness.

Later researchers dispute how much of that pattern is self-blindness and how much is a statistical artefact, and Dunning has defended the finding. Wheeler's test, at least, shows the mechanism at human scale. He did check his idea before acting on it. But he checked it with a method that could not have told him he was wrong, and the blank photograph gave him exactly the answer he already wanted.

[[lessons]]
$$**Test an idea in a way that could show you are wrong, and ask someone who knows the field to design the test.** A cook who tastes his own soup alone and always finds it perfect has tested his own palate, not the soup. Checking your own work feels like evidence, yet it uses the same judgment that produced the work, so any blind spot passes through unchanged. Put your draft, your forecast or your repair in front of someone more experienced and ask what would make it fail. That one outside look is what a lemon-juice photograph can never give you.$$

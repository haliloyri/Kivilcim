On a Saturday, a big parade moves down a city's main street, with the pavements packed shoulder to shoulder. In another city that same autumn, officials ban public gatherings almost as soon as the first cases are reported. By the end of the year the two cities' death charts will sit side by side.

[[story]]
In Philadelphia the first civilian flu cases were reported on 17 September 1918. The city went ahead with its large parade on 28 September anyway. School closures and bans on gatherings began only on 3 October, sixteen days after those first reports.

In St. Louis the first civilian cases were reported on 5 October. Two days later, on 7 October, the city began a broad set of measures. The same epidemic had reached two very similar American cities, and each answered at a different speed. Philadelphia had the head start, since its first cases came nearly three weeks earlier. St. Louis had a late arrival and used the time, acting within two days of its first reports.

When the numbers were later set side by side, the gap was stark, and the Philadelphia figure was more than eight times the St. Louis one at the peak. "Excess deaths" means deaths above what a normal week would bring. Philadelphia's weekly excess death rate peaked at 257 per 100,000 people, and its cumulative excess deaths reached 719 per 100,000. St. Louis peaked at 31 per 100,000 while its measures were in force, and ended at 347 cumulative, about half of Philadelphia's total.

The comparison became a study thanks to Richard Hatchett, Carter Mecher and Marc Lipsitch, and Michael Lewis's *The Premonition* (2021) counts Hatchett and Mecher among its main characters. Their 2007 paper in *PNAS* examined seventeen American cities and nineteen kinds of intervention between 8 September and 28 December 1918.

The pattern held beyond two cities. Cities that put several measures in place early had peak death rates about 50 percent lower. Cities with three or fewer early measures had a median peak of 146 per 100,000, against 65 for those with four or more.

The authors were careful about what this proved. They had no case counts, so they used excess deaths as a stand-in for illness. Only seventeen cities were studied, and causation stayed uncertain. Most measures were relaxed within two to eight weeks, and second waves followed the relaxing. The paper did not announce a law. It showed a strong pattern: timing can separate two cities that faced the same epidemic.

[[lessons]]
$$**Act on the first small warning sign, because the cost of waiting grows after the day you pass it.** A customer complaint, a late payment or a drip under the sink is cheap to deal with in the first few days. Waiting feels comfortable because nobody is hurt yet and acting looks like overreaction, but the same step gets harder and dearer once the problem has grown. Writing down in advance the point at which you will act lets you move early even while the evidence is still thin. It also spares you the argument on the day, because the decision was already made when you were calm.$$

Two radio astronomers climb into the throat of a horn antenna the size of a small room. They have a broom and a roll of metallic tape, and the afternoon ahead of them. Their goal is modest and practical: to get rid of a hiss that will not go away.

[[story]]
The antenna stood at the Bell Labs site in Holmdel, New Jersey, and had been built in 1960 for the Echo satellite project. Once Telstar was in orbit, the antenna was freed up for pure research, and Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson set out to use it for radio astronomy. Jon Gertner's The Idea Factory covers the Labs' science as well as its engineering, and this episode belongs to its scientific side.

To measure faint signals from the sky, they first had to know their own instrument's noise. Something was wrong: they kept finding a steady excess of microwave noise, the same in every direction and at every hour. They looked for the cause in the ordinary places. According to accounts of the episode, pigeons had been roosting in the horn, so Penzias and Wilson removed the birds and swept out the droppings. The hiss stayed. Wilson then taped over the rivets and joints in the horn. The noise dropped only slightly.

Meanwhile, at Princeton, not far away, a group around Robert Dicke was getting ready to search for exactly the kind of faint radiation the Big Bang would have left behind. When Penzias and Wilson learned of that work, the two puzzles joined. Their measurement, an excess antenna temperature at 4080 megacycles per second, matched what the Princeton theory predicted: a few degrees above absolute zero, about 3.5 kelvin.

In 1965 the two groups published back to back in the Astrophysical Journal Letters, Penzias and Wilson reporting what they had measured and the Princeton group explaining what it might mean. The signal is now known as the cosmic microwave background, the faint glow left over from the early universe. In 1978 Penzias and Wilson received the Nobel Prize in Physics for the detection.

The method deserves as much attention as the result, because it is the part anyone can copy. Neither of them declared the noise a discovery, and neither wrote it off as a nuisance. They ruled out one explanation after another, the birds, the droppings, the joints, until what remained was a signal that belonged to the sky. Each fix removed one more ordinary explanation and made the remainder harder to dismiss.

[[lessons]]
$$**Rule out the explanations you can control one by one, and then take the stubborn remainder seriously.** An annoying residual, a number that will not settle or a complaint that returns every week is easy to dismiss as noise, especially when you are busy with the work you meant to do. Write down each cause you have already checked and what changed when you fixed it, even when the answer is "nothing". If something is left over after the plain explanations are gone, your problem has narrowed to something new, and that leftover, small as it looks, may be worth more than everything you were originally trying to measure.$$

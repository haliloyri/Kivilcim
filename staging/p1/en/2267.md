A virus turns up in four throat swabs. Over two hundred recruits at one army base have been ill, and one of them has died. Then the American president asks Congress for the money to vaccinate every person in the country, and the whole decision rests on one camp.

[[story]]
Nate Silver includes the swine flu scare of 1976 among the forecasting failures he examines in *The Signal and the Noise*. It began at Fort Dix, an army base in New Jersey, where the state health department asked the CDC to identify an illness spreading among recruits.

The numbers were small. An influenza-like illness had hit more than 200 recruits and killed one. The CDC found a virus, then believed to be H1N1, in four throat cultures. Surveillance at the base suggested that it passed from person to person, which meant it could spread fast across the country.

The fear had a precedent. The great pandemic of 1918 and 1919 had also been H1N1, and CDC scientists testified before Congress. President Gerald Ford then asked for funds to vaccinate everyone in the United States.

The campaign moved quickly. In its first ten weeks nearly 43 million Americans were vaccinated, a record. Then a problem appeared, and it came from the vaccine itself: people who had received the vaccine showed a slightly higher risk of Guillain-Barré syndrome. Officials halted the programme before it was finished.

According to widely published accounts, the feared epidemic never spread beyond Fort Dix. A programme covering tens of millions of people had been mounted against a pandemic that did not come.

Look at the scale. The evidence was a camp with a little over 200 sick recruits, and the response reached nearly 43 million people, a ratio in the hundreds of thousands.

It is easy to blame the officials in hindsight, but their reasoning deserves a fair hearing. A new virus, a death, and the memory of 1918 made waiting look dangerous, and if they had waited and been wrong the cost could have been enormous. Every real epidemic also starts in a small place.

The weak point was the leap from thin evidence to a huge, hard-to-reverse commitment, made before anyone knew how likely the worst case was. Four samples and one base cannot draw a pandemic curve, however alarming each of them looks on its own. Silver's larger point about prediction applies: a story is complete and vivid, while the data behind it is full of gaps, so it is easy to stretch a little evidence into a big conclusion.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you commit big and irreversibly on a forecast built from thin data, build in one small step and one moment to look again.** Treat it like any planning problem: a new market, the word of your first four customers or a one-week jump in numbers is narrow evidence, and it cannot carry a year-long plan. Test at small scale first, then look at the results again at a checkpoint you set in advance. That way, if you are wrong, you lose the price of a trial instead of the price of a campaign, and you can change direction as new data arrives.$$

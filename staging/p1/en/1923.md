[[open]]
A conference center in Tarrytown, New York, half past six on a Friday evening. Sixty-two partners of one of Wall Street's strongest firms sit around the meeting table. Each of them suspects something is coming, and nobody has said what. By Sunday they will sign a piece of paper, and the partnership they have known will cease to exist.

[[story]]
In the summer of 1981 Salomon Brothers was still a partnership. The capital came from the partners' own pockets, and profits and losses landed in their own accounts. John Gutfreund had run the firm since 1978, taking over from Billy Salomon, who had led it for fifteen years. According to Fortune's report that year, Gutfreund announced record operating profits for the first ten months of the fiscal year and then proposed something unexpected: Salomon would merge into Phibro, a publicly owned commodities trader.

The seven-member executive committee had backed the idea unanimously, but the decision belonged to the partners. The 62 general partners got ten days' notice of a weekend meeting at a conference center in Tarrytown, starting at 6:30 on Friday, July 31. They had until Sunday to sign. One partner described the mood this way: "Everyone suspected something." Gutfreund said of his own approach: "I wanted an overwhelming consensus for the merger."

The numbers were attractive. Fortune reported that Phibro was paying twice book value and would put more than $300 million of fresh capital into Salomon. General partners could withdraw their capital in one lump sum, averaging $2.7 million, and would also receive $250 million in bonds convertible into Phibro stock. Gutfreund's own take was $32 million in cash and bonds, plus his share of 1981 profits.

Money was not the whole explanation. Fortune lists several reasons partners were willing to sell: they would no longer risk their own capital in trading every day, the bonus pool for vice presidents was being spread too thin, and the firm wanted a larger capital base. Henry Kaufman backed the deal, arguing that the financial system was changing and that standing still was not an option.

Billy Salomon, the story goes, learned of the deal only after lunch on the day it was finalized, and Fortune says he was angry at not being consulted. The partnership dissolved on September 30, 1981. Gutfreund said at the time: "But the firm won't be the same kind of savings bank that it was for many of us for years."

What stands out is a piece of logic that fits in one sentence. In the partnership, a trader's loss was literally the partners' own money. After the merger the same people sat at the same desks, but losses would come out of a large company's capital. That alone does not explain what happened to the firm later. It does put the right question on the table: is the person who takes the risk also the person who pays for it?

[[lessons]]
$$**Before judging a decision, ask who pays if it goes wrong.** A partner trading his own money and an employee managing someone else's capital look at the same opportunity differently. The same holds at work: a project funded from your own budget gets more caution than one funded from somebody else's. When a proposal looks cheap to the person making it, check whose pocket the cost would come from. That one question lets you read both your own choices and the incentives of the person across the table more clearly, and the question accuses nobody: it simply moves the discussion to where the real cost sits.$$

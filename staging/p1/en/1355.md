In the fable at the heart of Robin Sharma's best-known book, the sages use a set of strange symbols to carry their teachings. One of them is a gold stopwatch. Not a wall clock, not a calendar, not an hourglass on a shelf. A stopwatch. It is worth asking why, because the choice says a lot about how they want you to think about your time.

[[story]]
The book is *The Monk Who Sold His Ferrari*, published in 1997. In it, a former star lawyer named Julian Mantle passes on what he learned from the sages, and their teachings come wrapped in a fable full of images. The gold stopwatch is one of them.

It stands for time, and for a particular claim about time: that it is the most precious resource you have, and that it cannot be renewed. Money can be earned again. Possessions can be replaced. Time spent is simply gone.

A clock goes round and round and starts again every day. A stopwatch counts in one direction. It marks time running, and it makes you aware that it is running now. That is the feeling the book wants to create. It urges readers to treat time with urgency, and to live as though their time is limited, instead of pushing what matters off to "someday."

##"Someday" feels like a harmless delay, but on a stopwatch it is still time counting down.##

Almost everyone agrees, in principle, that time is limited. Very few people act on it. The call to a friend waits until things calm down. The family evening waits until the deadline passes. The change you keep meaning to make waits until next month. Each delay looks reasonable. Together, they assume a supply of time that does not exist.

The book does not need to reach far for an example of what that assumption costs. Julian's own story is one. He put his health and any sense of meaning to one side while he pursued career success. It was always something to deal with later. Later arrived in the form of a heart attack that forced him to stop.

It is worth being clear about what kind of claim this is. The book does not present research on regret or on how people feel at the end of their lives. It is a fable, and the stopwatch is a symbol. Its purpose is to prompt a decision, not to prove a finding: to make the limit on your time feel real enough, today, that you reorder what you are doing with it.

That is the whole weight of the image. Not a warning about the distant future, but a question about this week. If the watch is already running, what are you spending it on?

[[lessons]]
$$**Time is the one thing you cannot earn back.** The gold stopwatch sets time apart from money and possessions. You can recover from a bad year financially, and you can replace what you lose. Hours spent on things that do not matter to you are not refunded. Seeing time this way changes the question from "Can I afford this?" to "Is this worth what it costs me?"$$

$$**Putting things off is a choice with a price.** Julian treated his health and his sense of meaning as things he would get to later. The cost only became visible afterward, with a heart attack in court. Postponing rarely feels like a decision, because nothing happens at the moment you postpone. The book's point is that the bill still arrives, just on a date you did not pick. Choosing the date yourself is the alternative.$$

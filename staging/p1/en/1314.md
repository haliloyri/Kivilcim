An owner who has done everything alone finally hires help. They hand over a pile of tasks, relieved. A few weeks later, they are back doing those same tasks themselves. Michael Gerber has a name for the stage where this happens, and a name for the mistake that drives it.

[[story]]
In *The E-Myth Revisited*, Michael E. Gerber describes three phases a business can go through: Infancy, Adolescence, and Maturity.

Infancy is the stage where the owner and the business are the same thing. The owner does everything. Every task, every decision, every problem runs through one person. It works for a while. It ends when the owner realizes the business cannot keep running that way.

That realization starts Adolescence. The owner gets help. On paper, this should be the moment of relief. But Gerber says it often goes wrong in a specific way, which he calls "Management by Abdication."

The owner hands work off without a system. There is no clear description of how the job should be done, only the job itself. When things go wrong, as they tend to without a system, the owner rushes back in to fix them. The help did not free the owner. It added a new layer of chaos that pulled them straight back.

##Handing off work without a system is not delegation; Gerber calls it abdication.##

Gerber describes two common responses to this chaos. Some owners shrink back to a "Comfort Zone," a size of business they can personally control. It feels safer, but it caps what the business can become. Others try to grow without building any systems at all.

Maturity, in Gerber's view, is not simply the stage that comes after these. It is a way of thinking. A mature business is built from the start with a perspective on how it should work, not only on how to get through the day.

That leads to his key distinction. Working IN your business means doing the daily tasks. Working ON your business means designing the systems that do those tasks. An owner stuck in Adolescence spends almost all their time in the first mode, and every handoff that fails pulls them further into it.

The way out is not to stop getting help. It is to give the help something to hold onto: a clear, designed way of doing the work.

[[lessons]]
$$**Delegating without a system boomerangs.** Management by Abdication hands over a task but not the way to do it. When mistakes follow, the owner takes the work back, and the handoff has cost time without buying freedom. The fix is not less delegation. It is delegation with a written "how we do this" attached.$$

$$**The Comfort Zone feels safe and freezes growth.** Shrinking back to a size you can personally control is an understandable reaction to Adolescence. But it trades the chaos for a ceiling. The business stays exactly as big as one person's attention allows.$$

$$**Working ON is different in kind from working IN.** Doing the daily tasks and designing how those tasks get done are not two versions of the same effort. An hour spent fixing one process is a different kind of hour from ten spent pushing through it again. Without that first kind, the second never shrinks.$$

[[reflect]]
- When did you hand off a task, see it go wrong, and simply take it back?
- How much of your week is working in your responsibilities, and how much working on them?
- Is there a place where you have quietly stayed small because it was the size you could control?

&&The next time you delegate, what would you write down to hand over along with the task?&&

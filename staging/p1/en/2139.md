Late in spring, first-year students at a large American university wait to learn where they will live for the next three years. Many stay up all night for the news. When the news arrives, some jump up and down and some accept consolation from an older student.

[[story]]
A year later, the students who had landed in the houses they dreaded rated their happiness at 5.38 out of 7. Before the lottery they had predicted 3.43. Elizabeth Dunn, Timothy Wilson and Daniel Gilbert, the author of *Stumbling on Happiness*, had followed these students to see how well they could predict their own feelings.

At this university, first-years are assigned at random to one of 12 houses where they will spend the next three years, and the houses vary in how desirable they seem. One to two weeks before the draw, 174 students predicted how happy they would be a year later in each house. A year after that, 118 reported how happy they really were, and after two years 84 did.

The 53 students who landed in houses they had rated poorly expected a 3.43 and reported 5.38. The 56 who drew houses they had rated well expected 5.96 and reported 5.45. Two groups that had been so far apart in prospect were indistinguishable in practice, and the lucky ones were not significantly happier than the unlucky ones. Overall, forecasts and later happiness were only weakly related.

The researchers looked at what the students had used to make their forecasts. Predictions tracked a house's physical features, such as location, room size and facilities. They were unrelated to the quality of social life the students later found there. Actual happiness went the other way: in the third year it rose with good relationships with roommates, tutors who live or eat in the house and a sense of community, while physical features were unrelated or even pointed the other direction.

The students were not ignorant of this. Asked directly, they said social features would affect their happiness a little more than physical ones (4.91 against 4.67). But the physical features were where the houses visibly differed, and the social side felt the same everywhere, since each student entered the lottery with chosen roommates and a block of up to 15 friends who were assigned to the same house. The authors call this the isolation effect. A second experiment with 144 other first-years reproduced the pattern, with undesirable houses at 5.0 against a forecast of 3.82 and desirable ones at 5.41 against 6.02.

[[lessons]]
$$**When a draw goes badly, make up for it with the people who will still be around in a year.** Imagine you land the farther office, the worse desk or the team you did not want. What magnifies the disappointment is usually physical, like the distance, the noise or the view, and what carries your mood a year from now is who you eat lunch with. So spend the first two weeks deciding whom you will meet, ask questions of and have a meal with, and put your effort into people before walls. A year from now, that is most likely to have shaped how you feel.$$

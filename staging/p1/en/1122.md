Picture a country genuinely worried about its teeth. The warnings about bad dental health keep getting louder, and almost nobody picks up a toothbrush. Then, within roughly a decade, toothpaste turns up in most homes. The man who pulled it off believed he knew exactly why it worked. According to Charles Duhigg, he was missing the most important part.

[[story]]
In the early 1900s, brushing your teeth was not something most Americans did. That wasn't for lack of warning. Concern about the nation's poor dental health kept growing, yet the habit simply wasn't there.

Enter Claude Hopkins, one of the most famous advertising men of his day. He was persuaded to promote a toothpaste called Pepsodent. Hopkins didn't start by preaching about health. He went looking for a hook, and he found it in a stack of dental literature.

The reading described the mucin film, a thin coating that naturally forms on everyone's teeth. Hopkins saw in it something he could use. It was always there, anyone could feel it, and it could be framed as a problem. So he turned the film into a cue. His ads asked people to do something simple:

> Run your tongue across your teeth. Feel that film?

Once you'd noticed it, the ads offered the reward: beautiful teeth. Pepsodent, they said, removed the film.

It worked on a remarkable scale. In *The Power of Habit*, Duhigg reports that before the campaign only about 7% of Americans had toothpaste at home. About a decade later, roughly 65% did. Hopkins became wealthy, and when he explained his success, the recipe sounded tidy. Find a simple cue. Promise a clear reward.

Duhigg thinks that explanation leaves something out. Plenty of products had cues and rewards and never became daily rituals. What set Pepsodent apart was in the tube itself. The paste contained ingredients like citric acid and mint oil, and they left a cool, tingling feeling on the gums and tongue.

That tingle didn't clean anything. What it did was tell you, physically and right away, that the paste was working. Over time people came to expect it. Then they came to crave it. Skipping a brushing, or using a paste without it, left their mouths feeling somehow "not clean."

##The tingle cleaned nothing, yet it was the part people couldn't do without.##

In Duhigg's account, that craving is what turned brushing from an advertised suggestion into something people did without thinking. Competing toothpastes that lacked those irritating ingredients did poorly. And today, he notes, most toothpastes include ingredients whose main job is to produce that same tingle.

From here Duhigg draws the book's broader claim. Habits run on a loop: a cue, a routine, a reward. The loop only becomes powerful once the brain starts anticipating the reward before it arrives, and that anticipation is a craving. Hopkins built the loop. The tingle, which he didn't count as part of his formula, is what made people need it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Every habit has a loop.** Pepsodent's pitch followed a clear three-step sequence. Your tongue finds the film, which is the cue. You brush with Pepsodent, which is the routine. You get clean, beautiful teeth, which is the reward. If you name those three parts of any habit, you can see which one is weak or missing.$$

$$**A loop doesn't stick until you crave the end of it.** Hopkins had his cue and his reward, but what locked the habit in was anticipation. People expected the tingle, and when it didn't come, their mouths felt unfinished. A habit becomes automatic at the point where skipping it feels like something is missing, not when you simply know it's good for you.$$

$$**A small signal can do the heavy lifting, and its creator may not see it.** Citric acid and mint oil did nothing for the teeth. They only made the paste feel like it was working, and rivals without them struggled. Hopkins still credited his cue and reward. When something catches on, the reason people give may not be the real one, so look for the sensation people would miss.$$

[[reflect]]
- Pick one habit you'd feel uneasy skipping. What exactly would feel missing: a taste, a sense of order, a feeling of being done?
- What is your "tingle"? It might be a notification sound, a checkmark, the smell of coffee, or a streak counter ticking up.
- Consider a habit that worked for you. Is the reason you give for it the real one, or could it be a small signal you never counted?
- Look at a routine that keeps fading. Does it end with anything you can actually feel?

&&Think of a good habit you keep failing to build. What could you look forward to at the end of it, so that skipping it would leave something missing?&&

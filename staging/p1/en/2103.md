Halfway through a sea novel, a chapter stops and the narrator turns to the reader like a man about to swear an oath in court. He has just told them that a whale can sink a ship, and he knows how that sounds. He has heard the polite laughter before, when he told it to people ashore.

[[story]]
Herman Melville called the chapter in *Moby-Dick* "The Affidavit". In it he admits that people on land hear the size and strength of a whale as a tall tale. When he gave them a specific example, he writes, they complimented him on his joke, though he had no more intention of joking than Moses had when he wrote the plagues of Egypt. So he tells the reader he will prove his point on testimony that is entirely independent of his own.

The point is bold: a sperm whale can be powerful and knowing enough, and, in his words, judiciously malicious enough, to stave in and sink a large ship with direct aforethought. And, he adds, the sperm whale has done it.

His first witness is the Essex, the Nantucket whaleship at the heart of Nathaniel Philbrick's *In the Heart of the Sea*. Melville sets out what happened: boats lowered after a shoal of sperm whales, a very large whale coming at the ship, its forehead dashed against the hull, the ship down in less than "ten minutes". He mentions that Captain Pollard sailed again, was shipwrecked a second time, and gave up the sea for good.

Then Melville vouches for his sources in a sentence that reads like a sworn statement. He has seen Owen Chase, the Essex's first mate. He has read Chase's plain and faithful narrative. He has spoken with Chase's son. All this, he says, within a few miles of the scene of the catastrophe.

A footnote quotes Chase's own words, among them his conclusion that it was "anything but chance" that directed the whale and that he saw "decided, calculating mischief" in it. The novel's opening collection of extracts about whales already holds Chase's answer to his captain: "we have been stove by a whale."

##Melville then offers a second witness, and he admits right away that it is a thin one.##

It is the Union, also of Nantucket, lost off the Azores in 1807, thirteen years before the Essex, by a similar onset. He writes that he has never come across the authentic particulars, only casual allusions from whale hunters. He names the Union anyway, tells the reader how little he has on it, and leaves it to them to weigh the two cases unequally.

[[lessons]]
$$**When a claim sounds unbelievable, bring a witness who owes you nothing, and say which of your sources is thin.** A product engineer who says a new part will outlast the old one by years may meet polite doubt, because a bold claim from the person who built it carries little weight. A test run by another team, with the result written down, carries far more. Adding an honest line about your weaker source makes the stronger ones easier to believe, and it spares you the awkward moment when someone else finds the gap first, which usually costs more trust than the gap itself.$$

A man reads a sheet of paper with twenty lines on it, each naming a personality trait. He moves down the page, and at two or three lines he stops. A list meant to describe a psychopath has just described him, on a first read, and he does not like it.

[[story]]
The man is Jon Ronson, and the sheet is Robert Hare's psychopathy checklist, which Ronson learns in *The Psychopath Test*. In Ronson's telling, a first scan turned up two or three traits that applied to him as well. The result was a good deal less alarming than it felt, and the reason lies in how the list is built.

Hare, a Canadian psychologist who taught at the University of British Columbia for about thirty years, began working on it around 1980, drawing largely on the traits the psychiatrist Hervey Cleckley had described. He revised it in 1985 as the PCL-R, and a first edition followed in 1991. That is a long way from a magazine quiz.

The structure is simple. There are twenty items, and each is scored 0, 1 or 2: 0 if it does not fit, 1 if it fits partly, 2 if it clearly fits. Totals run from 0 to 40. The items include glibness and superficial charm, a grandiose sense of self-worth, pathological lying, lack of remorse and shallow emotions. Hare's manual suggests a cutoff of 30 for the label of psychopathy.

Scoring is harder than reading. The standard method pairs a semi-structured interview with a review of the subject's file: court records, reports, accounts from others. People who score high are, by definition, good at telling a smooth story, so what they say is checked against what the documents show. An item gets its points on evidence, not on an impression.

That is also why Ronson went on Hare's training course. According to published accounts it runs three days and teaches people to spot psychopaths from a reasonably safe distance. The list is presented as a skill to be learned, not a toy for anyone who picks up the paper.

The list is often called the gold standard in its field, and a 2005 review in the Buros Mental Measurements Yearbook judged it a reliable and effective instrument. It has also drawn criticism. In 2006 Hare and Paul Babiak published *Snakes in Suits*, which carried the idea into the workplace and focused on the charming, socially skilled "successful psychopath."

The arithmetic shows how little two or three matches mean. Three items can add up to at most 6 points, against a cutoff of 30. A handful of traits that fit you does not make a verdict. The checklist reads a person through a pattern that repeats across many items, not through a single moment.

[[lessons]]
$$**Count the concrete examples behind an impression before you act on it.** Say a colleague cuts people off in two meetings and you start calling him disrespectful. If you write down, for the last three months, how often it happened, in which situations and with whom, the verdict usually shrinks and gets more accurate. Make it a habit to attach one example to every claim you make about a person. Where you cannot find one, you have a feeling rather than a finding, and you can walk into the conversation that follows knowing which of the two you are carrying, so you speak about the person more precisely and they have something specific to answer.$$

Here's a puzzle. What would it take for you to gamble your freedom, your good name and your family's peace on money you would never spend? You'd probably say nothing could. Almost everyone says that. Morgan Housel tells the story of a man who had more than you or I will likely ever see, and who made that gamble anyway, with a single phone call.

[[story]]
The man is Rajat Gupta, and his story starts about as far from privilege as you can get. He was born in Kolkata and lost both his parents while he was still a teenager. From there he climbed, and kept climbing, until he became head of McKinsey, one of the most prestigious consulting firms in the world.

That wasn't the end of it. After McKinsey he sat on the boards of major companies, including Goldman Sachs. By 2008 he was reportedly worth around $100 million. Pause on that number. It's enough money that you could spend lavishly every day for the rest of your life and never see the bottom of it.

Then came the 2008 financial crisis. Banks were shaking, and in the middle of it Goldman's board learned that Warren Buffett was going to invest $5 billion in the bank. That news was worth a fortune to anyone who knew it before the rest of the market did.

Shortly after that board call, Gupta picked up the phone and called Raj Rajaratnam, a hedge fund manager. Rajaratnam's firm traded on the information.

It came out. Gupta was convicted of insider trading and sentenced to prison. The orphan who had reached the top of corporate life lost his freedom and his reputation, and he didn't lose them to desperation. He already had everything.

So why? Housel doesn't pretend to read Gupta's mind, but drawing on the reporting he offers an explanation. Gupta, he suggests, wanted to be a billionaire, not merely very rich. Next to the people he now spent time with, $100 million may not have felt like much at all.

##A man who already had everything risked all of it for more that he did not need.##

That's the part Housel wants you to sit with. The problem wasn't the size of Gupta's fortune. The problem was the ruler he was using to measure it. Social comparison, Housel argues, sets a ceiling so high that almost no one ever reaches it. There is always someone richer. Hit $100 million and the room fills with people who have a billion. Hit a billion and there's someone with ten.

In the same chapter, Housel sets a very different scene beside Gupta's. The novelists Kurt Vonnegut and Joseph Heller were at a party thrown by a billionaire. Talk turned to how rich their host was, and Heller, the author of a single famous book at a party full of money, answered calmly:

> "I've got something he can never have: enough."

Two men in rooms full of wealth. One was a novelist who felt he had enough. The other was a multimillionaire who apparently felt poor among billionaires, and it cost him nearly everything.

[[lessons]]
$$**The goalpost moves unless you stop it.** Gupta's $100 million was a fortune by any normal measure, yet it seems to have stopped feeling like success once his reference point became billionaires. Nothing about the money changed. His yardstick did. Housel's point is that the hardest financial skill isn't earning more. It's getting the goalpost to stay put, because left alone it slides forward every time you get close to it.$$

$$**Comparison is a game with no ceiling.** Housel's argument is that someone richer always exists, so "more" never actually arrives. If you measure yourself against the people above you, you've signed up for a contest you can't win, however far you climb. Gupta climbed about as far as anyone can, and the top still seemed to be somewhere above him.$$

$$**Some things are never worth the trade.** Housel lists what you should never risk for more money: your reputation, your freedom and independence, your family and friends, being loved by the people you want to love you, and your happiness. With one phone call Gupta put several of them on the table at once, for a gain he didn't need. Heller's "enough" isn't settling for too little. It's noticing when the appetite for more is heading somewhere you'll regret.$$

[[reflect]]
- Who is the person, or the group, you quietly measure your income or success against? Were they your reference point five years ago?
- Think of something you once badly wanted and now have. Has it started to feel ordinary?
- Go through Housel's list: reputation, freedom, family and friends, love, happiness. Are you trading any of them away, even a little, for more?
- If you had Gupta's fortune, whose table would you be looking at?

&&What would "enough" actually look like in your life, and which risks will you rule out today to protect it?&&

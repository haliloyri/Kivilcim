A president on a working vacation finishes a history book at his ranch. It is about an epidemic that swept the world a century earlier. A few weeks later, while under heavy criticism, he recommends that book to everyone at a press conference.

[[story]]
According to Time's reporting in autumn 2005, President George W. Bush started John Barry's *The Great Influenza*, about the 1918 epidemic, in July and finished it at his ranch during a working vacation. Time passes that on as his staff told it. In September, at a Rose Garden news conference, he recommended the book to everyone. At that moment he was being criticised over the handling of Hurricane Katrina.

Michael Lewis's *The Premonition* (2021) is about the people who tried to prepare the United States for a pandemic. This story goes back to 2005, when that effort was announced.

On 1 November 2005 Bush spoke at the National Institutes of Health in Bethesda, Maryland, and presented the plan. The White House published a booklet called "National Strategy for Pandemic Influenza" and launched pandemicflu.gov, run by the health department. The strategy rested on three pillars: preparedness and communication, surveillance and detection, and response and containment.

Bush asked Congress for $7.1 billion in emergency funding. Of that, $2.8 billion was for speeding up cell-culture technology, $1.029 billion for stockpiling antivirals, and $251 million for detecting and containing outbreaks. He also asked for liability protection for vaccine makers, arguing that lawsuits had driven manufacturers out of business.

His speech carried a plain warning too: in the first months of a new pandemic a vaccine might not exist, because scientists would first need a sample of the new virus. Officials described the aim as detecting and containing outbreaks overseas, showing the president in command, and informing the public so that reactions to any bird-flu cases would be calmer.

The sum Bush requested was more than the record amount the Senate had approved in September, according to Time. Criticism came quickly. House Democratic leader Nancy Pelosi called the strategy "several years too late" and said the country remained "unprepared".

The book cannot be said to have produced the plan on its own, and the reporting shows only a sequence: first the reading, then the recommendation, then the plan. The sequence is still striking. An abstract risk is easily shelved in a ministry report, but once a person has read what happened in 1918 page by page, the risk stops being a future possibility and becomes something that has already happened once.

[[lessons]]
$$**Describe an abstract risk to a decision-maker through something that really happened.** A manager who explains the danger of data loss only in figures watches the listeners nod and go back to work, because a figure puts no scene in anyone's head. The same manager who walks through a real outage at another firm, step by step, lets everyone see where their own desk would have sat in that story. The person deciding then weighs a concrete, lived example instead of a guess, adapts it to their own organisation, and finds it far easier to put budget behind a plan.$$

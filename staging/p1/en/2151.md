A father in Shrewsbury writes to his brother-in-law that he strongly objects to a voyage his son has been offered. Then he does something unusual: he leaves out his reasons, so that the man reading the letter can judge the plan without leaning on his own view.

[[story]]
The letter that sent Charles Darwin round the world was a recommendation, and it was not a flattering one. On 24 August 1831 his former teacher John Stevens Henslow wrote that Captain FitzRoy wanted a naturalist, and that Darwin was not a finished naturalist but amply qualified for collecting, observing and noting. FitzRoy, Henslow added, wanted a companion more than a mere collector. And Darwin was to put on no modest doubts or fears.

Darwin came home to Shrewsbury from a geological trip in Wales on 29 August and found the letter waiting. By the next day he was writing back to Henslow that he felt he must decline. The reason was his father, who had given strong advice against going. Darwin listed the objections: that the voyage would unfit him for settling down as a clergyman, the shortness of the time, and that he might not suit Captain FitzRoy. His father had not flatly refused, but going against him would take away Darwin's energy. Otherwise, he wrote, he would have taken all risks.

Robert Darwin was writing too. His letter to Josiah Wedgwood said he strongly objected to the voyage but would not say why, so that Wedgwood could give an unbiased opinion. If Wedgwood disagreed with him, Robert would follow his advice. A postscript dated 31 August reported that Charles had quite given up the idea.

On the same day Wedgwood replied, going through the objections one by one as Charles had summarised them. He admitted the voyage would be useless professionally. But for a man of wide curiosity, he wrote, it offered a chance to see "men and things" that few people ever get. His letter leaned toward accepting. According to a note Charles added later, his father agreed once those answers had been sent.

Winning the permission and the money still took several weeks. FitzRoy formally offered the post around 5 September, and the Beagle sailed from Plymouth on 27 December 1831 with Darwin aboard.

What makes the episode useful is the shape of the argument. A flat "I strongly object" would have ended it. Once the objections were written down, they could be taken separately: the career, the time, the fit with the captain. Each of those can be answered, or conceded, on its own merits. Darwin's father kept his own view out of the room, and the question that remained was about the voyage rather than about who had the stronger will.

[[lessons]]
$$**Split an objection into its parts, because a listed objection can be answered one point at a time.** "This feels wrong to me" and "this isn't for us" cannot be argued with, since there is nothing to hold on to. "The deadline is short, the team is untested and the cost is unclear" is three claims that you can check, answer or admit are right. When someone is against a decision, including the part of you that hesitates, write the objections down as separate lines and, where you can, show them to somebody who has not heard your opinion. That is a safer first step than saying yes or no on the spot.$$

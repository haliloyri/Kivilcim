[[open]]
A New York banker sits in a small room in the mountains of southern Mexico while a healer leads a ceremony around him. He is an outsider, and he knows it. In two years his account will appear in a mass-circulation magazine, and a village that has kept this practice among its own will be found by thousands of strangers.

[[story]]
Robert Gordon Wasson was not a scientist or a journalist. He was a banker, a vice president at J.P. Morgan, who with his wife Valentina, a physician, had been studying mushrooms in folklore and history for years. In 1955 the couple travelled to Huautla de Jiménez, a village in the Oaxaca region of Mexico, and Wasson took part in a ceremony led by the Mazatec healer María Sabina. This episode is part of the pre-history of the modern psychedelic revival that Michael Pollan traces in *How to Change Your Mind*.

Two years later, on 13 May 1957, *Life* magazine published Wasson's account under the title "Seeking the Magic Mushroom," part of its "Great Adventures" series. According to published accounts, the phrase "magic mushroom" was added by a *Life* editor against Wasson's wishes, and it stuck. The article was illustrated with pictures of the mushroom species, identified by the French botanist Roger Heim, which gave it the look of serious science. For a mass audience it was a thrilling story: a respectable American had gone to a remote village and found a sacred mushroom.

The consequences were not thrilling for Huautla. Accounts of the aftermath say that hundreds of Americans travelled to the village in the years that followed, among them the Harvard psychologist Timothy Leary, who went looking for the mushroom. María Sabina, according to the same accounts, was blamed by her own community for the attention and later regretted having introduced Wasson to the practice. Wasson, for his part, said that his only aim was to add to human knowledge. In the 1970s he voiced regret about what the publicity had done to the Mazatec people and their ritual.

The sequence is worth holding in mind because nobody in it is a villain. Wasson was curious and, by his own account, sincere. The magazine wanted a good story. The visitors wanted what they had read about. Each step looks reasonable. Yet the practice, which had been held inside one community under its own rules, ended up exposed to people who had none of those rules.

What Wasson could control was the accuracy of his own account. What he could not control was who read it, what they wanted from it and where they would go next. A writer decides the first of these once, at the desk, while the second and third unfold over years among people the writer never meets. That gap between what an author can check and what a story will set in motion is the real lesson of the episode.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you publish what someone trusted you with, ask what the attention will do to them, and ask them first.** Wasson saw a beautiful, hidden thing and showed it to millions, and the people who held it carried the cost. The same question applies at work. A customer story, a colleague's idea or a team's quiet method may be yours to describe, but the consequences will land on someone else's desk. If you cannot name what could go wrong for them, you have not finished thinking, and asking them early often changes what you write.$$

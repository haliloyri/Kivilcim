A young company's founders sit around a ping-pong table that doubles as the boardroom table. A visitor stands with a stack of slides and a single idea: say what you want, then say how you will know you got it.

[[story]]
The visitor was John Doerr, the venture investor, and the year was 1999. He was introducing the founding team of Google, among them Larry Page, Sergey Brin, Marissa Mayer, Susan Wojcicki and Salar Kamangar, to a way of running a company that he had learned from Andy Grove at Intel. According to the account Doerr's own organization publishes, he called it OKRs, short for objectives and key results.

Grove's version had a history. Peter Drucker had given management the idea of management by objectives, in which people agree on goals with their manager. Inside Intel, Grove reshaped it. His name for it was iMBOs, for Intel Management by Objectives. He tied each objective to key results, a term he seems to have coined himself, and the key results were the measurable proof that the objective had been reached.

Two of his changes carry most of the weight. The goals ran on quarters, not on a yearly cycle, so a team could see within three months whether it was on track and correct course. And the goals were kept apart from compensation. An objective was a statement of ambition, and if it was tied to pay people had every reason to set it low, so Grove separated the two.

Doerr's own demonstration at Google was deliberately small. His sample objective was to build a planning model for the company, with three key results to show whether it had been built. One of those key results, as he tells it, was that he would finish his presentation on time. The joke carries the point: a key result is something that can be checked later without an argument.

Google went on to set its company strategy with this framework. What travelled from Intel to a ping-pong table in 1999 was not a piece of software or a long manual. It was a habit of mind, to write an ambitious aim in plain words and attach to it a short list of facts that would settle whether you had reached it.

The pairing is the invention. An objective alone is a wish, and a list of measurements alone is a spreadsheet. Together they let a team aim high and still know, honestly, where it stands.

[[lessons]]
$$**Pair every ambitious goal with a few results that anyone could check without arguing.** An objective written in plain words tells people where to go, and the key results tell them, quarter by quarter, whether they have arrived. Keep the goals short-cycle so there is time to correct, and keep them apart from pay so that people dare to aim high instead of setting safe targets. If you cannot say what would count as proof, you do not yet have a goal, only a hope, and a hope is much harder to steer by when the quarter gets busy and everyone remembers the target differently.$$

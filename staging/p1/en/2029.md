The deadline falls on a Tuesday. By then every bidder has to name a final number, and the committee will hold two offers side by side: one from the men who run the company, one from an investment firm. One number is plainly higher than the other, and the higher one will lose.

[[story]]
Bryan Burrough and John Helyar's *Barbarians at the Gate* follows the RJR Nabisco fight to its last weeks, and this story is about its finish line. According to Time, the board had extended the deadline for offers to Tuesday, 29 November 1988. The price had by then passed $26 billion, more than the gross national product of Peru or Portugal, and lawyers and investment bankers stood to earn as much as $1 billion between them.

The field had been crowded. F. Ross Johnson's management group was in, backed by Shearson Lehman Hutton and, according to published accounts, Salomon Brothers too. So was Henry Kravis's KKR, the firm that had built its name on leveraged buyouts since the 1970s. A group led by First Boston, which included Jay Pritzker and Philip Anschutz, had offered up to $118 a share in what Time called a "Chicago submarine." When the final numbers came in, two bids were left standing.

According to published accounts, management's final offer was $112 a share and KKR's was $109. On paper that was a three-dollar lead for management. Still, the board's special committee, chaired by Charles Hugel, went with KKR.

Why? The same accounts say the board's advisers judged the two offers roughly equal in value. Management's structure allowed the final value to shareholders to come in below the stated $112. KKR's offer was guaranteed: $109 meant $109. Some observers thought the board's unease over the pay arrangements Johnson had arranged for himself made that technical point a convenient excuse.

Time's issue of 12 December 1988 reported that KKR had outfoxed Johnson's group and walked off with RJR Nabisco. The cash price was about $25 billion. Johnson left the company in February 1989.

Everything in the story turns on that three-dollar gap. A number in a headline cannot tell you what is written two lines below it. The worth of an offer depends on how firmly it will be paid, on the conditions under which it can change, and on how far the people at the table trust those conditions. The committee picked the firmer figure rather than the bigger one.

[[lessons]]
$$**When you weigh an offer, read the headline number together with the conditions and the certainty behind it.** A job offer with a high salary that depends on a bonus that can move, or on a vague promotion condition, may be worth less than a lower offer that is fixed. Asking what sits underneath the number lets you set two options side by side on the same scale. Doing it before you decide also lets you explain your choice afterwards without blaming anyone, and if the other side dislikes the question, that is information too.$$

[[open]]
A calculator maker in Japan sends an order to a small California chip company: build twelve custom chips, each one wired for a single part of the calculator. An engineer at the company reads the design and judges the whole approach too cumbersome. He has a different idea, and it will not need twelve chips.

[[story]]
The company was Intel, and the customer was Busicom. In April 1969 Busicom asked Intel to build a twelve-chip set for a desktop calculator. The engineer who started the project was Ted Hoff, and according to Intel's own history he judged Busicom's design too cumbersome. He did not simply build what was asked. He proposed an alternative, and that alternative is how Walter Isaacson's *The Innovators* arrives at the microprocessor.

Hoff's idea was to replace a pile of chips, each hard-wired for its job, with a smaller set built around one programmable chip. Instructions and data would sit in memory, so the same hardware could be told to do different work. Published accounts describe a four-chip family, with the central processing chip at its heart, the 4004. In the twelve-chip plan the calculator's logic lived in the wiring; in Hoff's plan it lived in instructions that could be changed without redrawing a chip. The point was that a chip you can program serves any customer who writes the program, while a chip wired for one calculator serves only that calculator.

Federico Faggin took over the design of the chip from Hoff, and Stan Mazor also contributed to the design. Masatoshi Shima, who worked for Busicom, later joined Intel. By March 1971, accounts say, Busicom had a complete working set of the chips. The result was a processor on a single piece of silicon, announced publicly in November 1971. A design that began as a calculator order had become a general-purpose component.

The contract complicated things. Busicom had paid for the work and held exclusive rights to the design. In May 1971 Robert Noyce, Intel's chief executive, bought back the rights to everything except calculators, in return for giving Busicom back its $60,000 investment. According to Intel, he did it at the urging of the design team.

Think about the order of events, because the order is the whole lesson. A customer asked for twelve chips. An engineer asked a different question first, whether the job needed twelve chips at all, and the answer produced a product that could be sold to many other customers. Intel then secured the freedom to do so. Neither step was dramatic, and the second made the first worth something.

[[lessons]]
$$**Question the size of the request before you start building it.** Hoff did not argue that Busicom's design was wrong in principle; he judged it too cumbersome and offered something simpler that could be reused. A web designer asked for ten separate page layouts for one client might ask whether a single flexible template would cover them and also serve the next client. You can ask the same question of any request: what is the smallest general thing that would do this, and what else could it do afterwards? Asking early costs a conversation, and asking late costs the finished work.$$

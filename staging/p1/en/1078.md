[[open]]
Picture a stranger at your front door. He wants to put a big, rather unattractive billboard on your lawn. You'd probably say no, and most people did. But another group of homeowners got the exact same request and said yes, overwhelmingly. What changed their answer was about three inches wide, and they had agreed to it two weeks earlier without a second thought.

[[story]]
This one comes from Robert Cialdini's *Influence*, in his chapter on commitment and consistency. He didn't run the experiment himself. He credits two psychologists, Jonathan Freedman and Scott Fraser, whose study was published in 1966 and carried out in a residential neighborhood in California.

The setup is simple. A researcher posing as a volunteer goes door to door with a request that is hard to love. He asks homeowners whether he can install a large billboard on their front lawn. It carries a worthy message:

> "Drive Carefully."

The message isn't the problem. The problem is that the thing is big, it's unattractive, and it's going on *your* lawn, where your neighbors will see it every day.

When residents heard this request cold, with no history and no warm-up, about 17% agreed. That's fewer than one in five. Most people weighed it the way you'd expect: a good cause, but an ugly sign, so no thanks.

Now for the second group. About two weeks before the billboard request, these residents had been approached with something far smaller. Would they be willing to put a little sign in their window, roughly three inches across, saying:

> "Be a safe driver."

That's easy to say yes to. It's tiny, it's in the window, and it asks almost nothing of you. Nearly everyone agreed.

Two weeks later, this same group got the same visit with the same billboard and the same request to put it on the lawn. The sign was just as big and just as unattractive as before. Nothing about the billboard had improved.

##This time, about 76% said yes.##

So you have one request and two groups. It went from under one in five to roughly three in four, and the only difference was a small, almost forgettable yes given two weeks earlier.

Cialdini's explanation is the part worth sitting with. He argues that the small sign did more than sit in a window. It changed how those residents saw themselves. Once they had agreed to display it, they began to think of themselves as civic-minded people, the kind who act on causes like safe driving. So when the big request arrived, they weren't just judging a billboard. They were facing a question about who they were. Saying no would have clashed with the person they now believed themselves to be. Saying yes kept them consistent.

This approach has a name: the **foot-in-the-door technique**. You win a small commitment first, and a larger one becomes much easier to get later. Cialdini presents it as one of the levers that people in the compliance business, meaning salespeople, fundraisers and recruiters, pull on purpose, because it works so reliably.

[[lessons]]
$$**A small yes rewrites your self-image.** The three-inch window sign asked for almost nothing, yet it changed how those residents saw themselves. They became "people who support safe driving." Once that label was in place, it went on making decisions for them. The billboard request two weeks later was answered by that new identity, not by a neutral homeowner looking at an ugly sign.$$

$$**Consistency can outrank the merits.** The billboard was identical for both groups. Its size, its looks and its spot on the lawn were all the same, and compliance still jumped from about 17% to about 76%. The second group wasn't judging a better offer. They were protecting a picture of themselves that they had committed to, both in front of neighbors and in their own minds.$$

$$**Seeing the lever lets you refuse to be pulled.** Cialdini doesn't treat this as a curiosity. He treats it as a tool that compliance professionals use deliberately. Once you know that a small first request can be a setup, you can separate the two asks and judge the big one on its own terms, before your earlier yes decides for you.$$

[[reflect]]
- Think about a small agreement you made recently: signing something, joining something, trying something once. Did it quietly change the label you give yourself?
- Recall a time you said yes to something big. Was it the request that convinced you, or the fact that you had already said yes before?
- Is there a "window sign" in your life right now, a small commitment someone might be counting on to lead to a larger one?

&&If that big request had arrived first, with no small yes before it, would you still have agreed?&&

[[open]]
In Dunedin, New Zealand, an adult watches a three-year-old and writes down how the child copes when something goes wrong. It is one child among a thousand being watched this way. Nobody in the room knows what these notes will be compared with, thirty years on.

[[story]]
The children of Dunedin were born in 1972 and 1973, 1,037 of them, and researchers followed them through life. Willpower discusses what the team found when the group reached 32, and the findings were published in 2011 by Terrie Moffitt and her colleagues. Self-control had been measured in childhood from nine different measures. Observers rated the children at ages three and five, and parents, teachers and the children themselves added reports at five, seven, nine and eleven. By 32, 96 percent of the group was still in the study.

The researchers then split the children into five equal groups by self-control and compared the lowest fifth with the highest. At 32, 43 percent of the lowest group had a criminal conviction, against 13 percent of the highest. Multiple health problems affected 27 percent of the lowest and 11 percent of the highest. An annual income under NZ$20,000 was the case for 32 percent of the lowest and 10 percent of the highest. And 58 percent of the lowest were raising children in single-parent households, against 26 percent of the highest.

The pattern was a gradient. It was not only the lowest group that fared worse; each step up in self-control went with better outcomes on average. The authors also checked whether intelligence or social class could be the real cause, and the links held after both were taken into account.

The paper also separated self-control from the mistakes people made as teenagers, and the link survived that check too. According to Willpower, low self-control did not predict depression in adulthood, but it did predict alcohol and drug problems.

They tried a second route. In a separate English and Welsh study of 2,232 twins, 509 pairs were same-sex fraternal twins. Within these pairs, the sibling with lower self-control at age five had worse outcomes at twelve, even though the two grew up in the same family.

This is still observational work. Nobody assigned children to low or high self-control, so the study alone cannot prove that self-control caused the outcomes. What it does show is that a measure taken before school age lined up with so many different parts of adult life.

[[lessons]]
$$**Count small gains in self-control as real, because the benefit rises step by step.** If outcomes improve across the whole range, then a person in the middle of the scale does not need a dramatic change to gain something. Someone who finishes the weekly report one day earlier, or leaves the phone in the hall most evenings, is moving up one step on a slope that counts at every height. You can aim for one notch up instead of waiting for a total transformation that will never arrive. And you can check the gain in something concrete, such as a finished report or an evening without the phone.$$

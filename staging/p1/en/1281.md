Picture a long page of good words, every one of them something you'd be proud to live by. Now the instruction: cross out everything except one or two.

The cut is harder than it sounds. According to Brené Brown, it's also the only way the list becomes any use to you.

[[story]]
The exercise comes from *Dare to Lead*, Brown's 2018 book, in a section called "Living into Our Values." She hands leaders a long list of values and asks them to choose the ones that matter most. Then she asks them to keep narrowing, until only one or two remain.

It can feel like a strange demand, since each word on the list sounds worth keeping. Brown's reasoning is blunt. When we have a long list of values, we don't really have any. A long list sounds admirable, but it can't guide a hard choice. When two good things pull in opposite directions, ten priorities give you ten answers, which amounts to no answer at all.

##A list of values only becomes useful when it's short enough to decide with.##

Brown does the exercise herself and names the result. Her two core values are faith and courage. Not a dozen. Two.

But narrowing is only the first half. A value you've named is still just a word. So for each value, she asks people to write down two kinds of behavior.

The first kind is behavior that supports the value: the concrete things you'd see someone do if they were living by it.

The second kind is what she calls "slippery behaviors." These are the signs that you're drifting away from the value, often without quite noticing. They're the early warning, the moment you catch yourself acting against the thing you said mattered most.

Her reasoning is that values have to be translated into specific, observable behavior. Saying a value is not the same as living it.

So the finished exercise is small on paper: one or two words, a few behaviors that show you're living them, and a few that show you're slipping. That small page is meant to be used in the moments when a choice is hard and the right answer isn't obvious.

[[lessons]]
$$**Fewer values guide better.** Brown's instruction to cut a long list down to one or two looks like a loss at first. It's the opposite. Her own pair, faith and courage, can actually settle a hard decision, because there's no crowd of other priorities to hide behind. A long list lets you justify almost anything; a short one makes you choose. The discomfort of crossing out good words is the price of having something that works under pressure.$$

$$**A value is a behavior, not a word.** Her exercise asks for supporting behaviors and "slippery behaviors" for each value. That second list is what makes the exercise honest. It's easy to say you value courage. It's harder, and more useful, to write down the specific things you do when you're backing away from it, so you can catch yourself before the drift becomes a habit.$$

[[reflect]]
- If you had to keep only two values, which would survive the cut?
- Think of a recent hard decision: which value actually drove it?
- What would a "slippery behavior" look like for the value you care about most?

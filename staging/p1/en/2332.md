A man in a London street stands at the kerb, waiting for a red light to change. In his head is a newspaper article he has just read, and it is about the atom and whether anyone could ever draw power from it. The light turns green. He steps off the kerb with a thought that will not leave him alone.

[[story]]
Richard Rhodes's *The Making of the Atomic Bomb* traces how a weapon went from an idea to a desert test, and the idea has a street address. In September 1933 the physicist Leo Szilard was walking along Southampton Row in London. According to Physics World's account of that day, he was musing about an article he had just read in The Times.

Published accounts link that article to Ernest Rutherford, the most famous experimental physicist of his age. Rutherford had said that anyone looking for a source of power in the transformation of atoms was talking "moonshine." When a man like that calls an idea moonshine, most people stop thinking about it.

Szilard did not stop. At Russell Square he waited at a set of traffic lights. Then the lights changed, he stepped into the road, and the thought arrived. Suppose there is an element that is split by neutrons and gives off more neutrons than it absorbed. A large enough mass of it could keep going by itself, each split setting off the next, and the energy released would be enormous.

Look at what he had and what he lacked. He had no such element. He had no experiment, no laboratory and no proof that nature offered one. What he had was a chain of reasoning with one clear condition: find the right element, gather enough of it, and the reaction sustains itself. That is a claim nature could answer with a yes or a no.

He also took it seriously enough to put it on paper in a form the law recognises. He applied for a patent on the neutron-induced chain reaction, and in 1934 he assigned it to the British Admiralty. A thought on a pavement had become a document with his name on it.

It is worth seeing how the two positions differ. Rutherford's remark was a verdict, loud and final, and it ended the conversation. Szilard's idea was quiet, but it had an opening in it, because it named the one thing that would settle the matter. A verdict closes a question. A condition leaves it open and tells you where to look.

Rhodes's history shows what followed over the next dozen years, but the beginning is only this: a pause at a red light, a dismissal from the most famous man in the field, and a physicist who heard "impossible" and translated it into "under what conditions?"

[[lessons]]
$$**Treat a confident "impossible" as a hypothesis with a missing condition.** When an expert, a boss or your own past self says something cannot work, ask what would have to be true for it to work. In a budget meeting that might turn "we can't hire this year" into "what would the numbers need to show?" That question turns a closed verdict into a search you can start this week. It also works when the person who said no is far more famous than you, because you are no longer arguing with them, only looking for the one thing that would settle it.$$

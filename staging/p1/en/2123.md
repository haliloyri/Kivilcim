At dawn, a small squid lies buried in the sand off a Hawaiian island, and a pouch in its body empties almost completely into the sea. Nearly everything that glowed there last night is gone. By evening the squid will be lit up again, as if nothing had happened.

[[story]]
Ed Yong's *I Contain Multitudes* includes the Hawaiian bobtail squid and the glowing bacteria it keeps in a light organ on its underside. This story is about how that partnership begins and how the squid renews it every single day. The best-known name in the work is Margaret McFall-Ngai, at the University of Wisconsin-Madison, who studies how the squid's cells and the bacterial cells communicate.

Researchers collect these squid in Kaneohe Bay, off the island of Oahu, and the first hours after hatching are where the partnership starts. A newly hatched squid has a light organ with no bacteria in it. Within hours it has taken in *Vibrio fischeri* from the seawater, helped by temporary appendages that steer water and the microbes in it toward the organ. The bacteria settle into nest-like pouches inside the organ, but they never enter the squid's own cells.

The squid hunts at night, and the glow is its disguise. The light under its body is nearly as bright as moonlight and starlight from above, so a predator looking up from below sees no shadow. A 2004 study by Jones and Nishiguchi found that the light the bacteria produce tracks the intensity of light coming from above, which suggests the squid adjusts its glow to the sky. The organ itself looks like an eye, with a lens, an iris-like structure and reflective tissue.

Then comes the part that surprises most people. At dawn the squid expels about 90 percent of its bacteria into the water; published estimates run as high as 95 percent. It spends the day buried in the sand while the few remaining bacteria multiply in their pouches. By the time the squid comes out at night, as McFall-Ngai puts it, it "will have a full complement of luminous Vibrio fischeri."

So the squid does not keep one lasting colony. It rebuilds the population from a small remnant every morning, over a life of up to about a year, and the bacteria it sheds go back into the sea where the next hatchling will find them. One question McFall-Ngai's group pursues is how a young squid tells a helpful microbe from a harmful one in the first place.

[[lessons]]
$$**Renew a shared arrangement on a fixed schedule and keep only what regrows.** A partnership that is emptied out and rebuilt at a set time stays fresh, because nothing in it can go stale unnoticed, and the people in it never have to guess which parts are still alive. A household that rewrites its chore list every Sunday and keeps the two or three habits that still work will usually spend less time arguing over old rules. Pick one arrangement you share with others, choose a day for clearing it, and see which parts come back on their own and which you never miss. What returns without effort is the part worth keeping.$$

[[open]]
Three men share a lunar module built for two, and its air filters are filling up. The spare filters in the command module are square, while the lunar module wants round ones. On the ground, a team has an answer made of two plastic bags, a cue card and a roll of gray tape, and it has to be explained over the radio to a man with a headset on.

[[story]]
Jim Lovell and Jeffrey Kluger's *Lost Moon* is the story of Apollo 13. This part of it is about air. After the oxygen tank failed in April 1970, the crew moved into the lunar module, Aquarius. It was designed to keep two men alive for about two days, and its lithium hydroxide canisters were meant to absorb the carbon dioxide of two people for roughly thirty hours. Now it held three men, and the trip home would take days.

The command module carried spare canisters, but they were square and the lunar module's system took round ones. Ed Smylie, an engineer on the ground, devised an adapter, according to NASA's account of the episode. Capcom Joe Kerwin then read the procedure up to the crew. The shopping list was short: two command module canisters, a roll of gray tape, two plastic bags from the crew's cooling garments, one cue card trimmed to about eleven by six inches, scissors, and something to plug a bypass hole, such as a wet wipe or a piece of sock. The suit hoses would carry the air through.

Jack Swigert put on the headset and did the work. He repeated each instruction back to Kerwin, who had asked him to speak up when answering. Lovell gathered the materials. Fred Haise taped the hoses.

By the timestamps in the transcript, the build took about 48 minutes. Swigert's report was plain: "Our do-it-yourself lithium hydroxide canister change is complete." He added that the arch in the cardboard was a little too small to hold the hose inlet pointing down. Kerwin's answer was just as short: "I concur. I think it'll work, too."

What makes the scene useful is its shape. The fix did not need an expert on board, and nobody had to invent anything while the carbon dioxide rose. It needed a list of things already in the cabin that anyone could find, steps in a fixed order, and a repeat-back after each one, so a mistake would show up before it was taped in place. Nobody had to guess whether the plan held, because each step was said aloud and answered aloud. Even the flaw was handled in one exchange: the crew said what was off, the ground judged whether it mattered, and they moved on.

[[lessons]]
$$**Give a hard instruction as short steps, and ask the listener to say each one back.** A repeat-back catches the misheard word while the tape is still loose, and it costs only a few seconds. Use it when you hand a task to a colleague by phone, when you talk a relative through a form, or when you brief a new hire on a procedure where one slip is expensive. Build the instruction from things the other person already has in front of them, so that they can act on it at once and you can hear whether it landed.$$

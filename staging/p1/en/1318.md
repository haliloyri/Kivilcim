You're in the middle of something hard. The sentence won't come, or the numbers don't add up. And your hand is already moving toward the phone. Here's the question worth slowing down for: what were you feeling one second before you reached? Nir Eyal thinks that feeling, not the phone, is where distraction really begins.

[[story]]
In *Indistractable*, Nir Eyal starts somewhere most advice about focus doesn't. The usual suspects are the phone, the notifications, the endless pings from email and chat. Eyal argues that most distraction actually starts from internal triggers. These are uncomfortable feelings: boredom, anxiety, fatigue, uncertainty.

The ping is often just the exit. The real push comes from inside.

He goes further. The drive to escape discomfort, he writes, is the root of human motivation. We move away from what feels bad. That leads to his most quoted line: "time management is pain management."

That changes what a fix can look like. If you delete the apps, block the sites and switch every notification off, you have removed the exits you know about. But the discomfort is still there. The difficult task still feels difficult. And people, Eyal points out, will find another way to escape it.

##Removing the technology alone doesn't solve the problem, because the feeling you're running from comes with you.##

So Eyal's first step is not a tool. It is noticing. When you feel the pull away from your work, identify the internal trigger. What was the feeling right before the distraction? He suggests writing it down, along with the time and what you were doing. Bored while formatting a report. Anxious before replying to a tricky message. Stuck at the start of a proposal.

Written down, the pattern becomes visible. You can start to see which tasks and which feelings send you looking for an escape.

Then comes the question of what to do once you've caught the urge. Eyal's answer is neither obeying it nor fighting it. He calls one approach "surfing the urge." You observe the craving with curiosity, the way you might watch a wave rise and fall, until it passes. You don't act on it, and you don't wrestle it down either.

His second tactic is the 10-minute rule. When you're tempted, you tell yourself you're allowed to give in. Just not yet. You wait ten minutes first.

The rule doesn't forbid anything. It only puts a small gap between the feeling and the action. Inside that gap, you get a chance to see the urge for what it is.

Taken together, the approach moves the focus from the device to the moment before the device. You still might check the phone. But you'll know what you were trying to get away from when you did.

[[lessons]]
$$**The escape starts inside.** "Time management is pain management" reframes every distraction as a move away from something uncomfortable. If you only look at what interrupted you, you miss what you were trying to leave. The phone gets the blame, while boredom or worry did the pushing.$$

$$**You can't manage what you haven't noticed.** Eyal's first move is almost clerical: write down the feeling, the time, and what you were working on. It sounds small, but an urge that goes unrecorded just repeats. A written note turns a blur of distractions into a pattern you can actually look at.$$

$$**An urge can be waited out.** Surfing the urge and the 10-minute rule share one idea. You don't have to obey the craving, and you don't have to beat it. Giving yourself permission to give in later, rather than now, is often enough to let the pull fade.$$

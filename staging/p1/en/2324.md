A man climbs a dusty hillside north of Lima with a backpack on his shoulders. Inside are pills, food supplements and the records of people who live in houses at the end of the path. He is not a doctor, and the medicine in the bag is the kind that was once judged too expensive to give to people like them.

[[story]]
In Tracy Kidder's *Mountains Beyond Mountains*, Paul Farmer and Partners In Health carry the fight against multidrug-resistant tuberculosis from Haiti to Peru. This story is about the Lima end of it. Socios En Salud, the Peruvian sister organization of PIH, was founded on 8 July 1996 in Carabayllo, a hillside community about 20 miles north of the capital.

According to PIH, the global health community of the 1990s had decided that MDR-TB was too expensive or too complicated to treat in a country like Peru. Jim Yong Kim, Father Jack Roussin and their Peruvian colleagues rejected that verdict. They did not argue it in a paper. They built a program in a neighborhood and treated the patients in it.

The program leaned on neighbors. Community health workers, called *agentes comunitarios de salud*, are hired locally, and they bring patients their medication, support and house calls. One of them, Hildo Miranda, a health promoter recruited by Father Roussin, was photographed in 2001 carrying food supplements across Carabayllo. Later came the backpack kits that give the TB program one of its names, and vans with rapid testing that reach the farthest hillside homes.

The drugs for MDR-TB are taken for a long time, and a patient who stops halfway can leave the infection stronger than before. A pill that sits in a clinic cabinet cures no one. The program's answer was to put a person, someone from the same slope, between the cabinet and the kitchen table.

By PIH's account, the organization has now treated more than 10,500 people for MDR-TB, with a cure rate of 83 percent, which it describes as among the highest in the world. These numbers come from the organization itself, as of an article published in 2021, and they cover its own program rather than all of Peru.

Think about how little of this was new medicine. The verdict that Peru could not afford the treatment was a judgment about delivery, and delivery turned out to be a job for people who knew every turn of the path.

[[lessons]]
$$**Give the last stretch of a hard job to the people who live closest to it.** A treatment, a policy or a training course can be perfect at headquarters and still fail in the last hundred meters, because the last hundred meters belong to someone else's daily life. Neighbors know when a person skipped a dose, which door to knock on and what the family eats. Before you roll out your next plan, find the person in the receiving group who already knows the way, and pay them for it, because their knowledge is not a courtesy but the delivery system itself.$$

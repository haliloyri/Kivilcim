Picture two shoppers at the same crowded shelf. One picks up the first option that does what she needs and heads for the checkout. The other keeps going: another label, another comparison, one more option that might be slightly better. Both leave with something decent. Barry Schwartz has a good idea which of them goes home happier.

[[story]]
In *The Paradox of Choice*, Schwartz gives these two shoppers names. The first is a satisficer. The second is a maximizer.

A maximizer is after the best possible option. Good isn't the target; best is. That means looking at as many alternatives as possible, because how else can you be sure you've found the top one?

A satisficer settles for an option that's good enough by her own standards. Schwartz borrows the term from Herbert Simon, and he's careful about what it means. A satisficer is not someone without standards. She has criteria, sometimes demanding ones. The difference is that she stops searching once an option meets them.

That difference sounds small. In practice it shapes how people feel about nearly every choice they make. Schwartz and his colleagues built a questionnaire to measure how strongly someone leans toward maximizing. People who scored as maximizers reported lower happiness and satisfaction, less optimism and more regret than satisficers did.

Why would trying harder for the best leave people less content? Schwartz points to the sheer abundance of options. Checking everything is impossible. There's always another product, another place, another possibility you didn't examine. For a maximizer, every one of those unexamined alternatives is a door left open for regret. Maybe the better one was right there.

##The satisficer closes that door on purpose.## Once her standards are met, the rest of the shelf stops being a threat. She doesn't need to wonder about the options she skipped, because finding the absolute best was never the goal.

Schwartz raises an uncomfortable possibility here. Maximizers may sometimes end up with objectively better results and still feel worse about them. The extra searching can pay off on paper and cost them in satisfaction.

His advice isn't to lower every standard. It's to choose when to choose. Accept good enough for most decisions, the everyday purchases and routine picks, and save the exhaustive search for the few decisions that really matter to you. That way your effort goes where it counts, and the rest of life stops being a string of comparisons you can never finish.

[[lessons]]
$$**Good enough has standards.** A satisficer isn't careless. She decides what she needs, and she stops once an option meets it. Schwartz's definition, borrowed from Herbert Simon, turns good enough into a deliberate rule for stopping, not a lack of ambition. The standards come first; the stopping follows from them.$$

$$**Chasing the best invites regret.** In Schwartz's questionnaire research, maximizers reported lower happiness, less optimism and more regret than satisficers. When you're hunting for the best, every option you didn't check can come back as a doubt about the one you picked.$$

$$**Decide which decisions deserve effort.** You don't have to be one type everywhere. Schwartz's advice is to choose when to choose: satisfice on most things, and reserve a full search for the few decisions that genuinely matter to you. That puts your energy where the stakes are, and lets the small choices stay small.$$

On a sloping deck, a lifeboat hangs from its davits. On one side of the ship it swings in over the rail, away from the water. On the other it dangles so far out that no one can step into it. The boats are in good order. The rope is sound. Nothing about them is wrong except the angle.

[[story]]
Erik Larson's *Dead Wake* follows the Lusitania's last crossing, and the arithmetic of her lifeboats is one of the plainest facts of the 1915 inquiry. She carried 48 boats, rated for 2,605 people. On 7 May 1915 there were 1,959 people on board. On paper, 646 places were left over. Cunard's ship had passed the Board of Trade's inspections in the weeks before, and the ship's carpenter had found the boats in good order on the day she sailed.

The torpedo made those numbers meaningless. Lord Mersey's report says that about twenty minutes passed between the first alarm and the sinking. The engines failed almost at once, so the ship could not be slowed and kept moving through the water, which made lowering boats harder. She also listed sharply to starboard. On the port side the boats swung inboard and were hard to launch. On the starboard side they hung too far from the rail for passengers to board easily.

The report adds that frightened passengers tried to push port-side boats off the ship. Some caught on the rail and capsized. Others reached the water badly damaged and leaked. Captain Anderson superintended the launching, and the captain's first order was to lower all boats to the rail, followed by "Women and children first." The inquiry found that the launching had been done as well as the short time, the moving ship and the heavy list allowed, and it found no negligence. Other accounts give different counts of how many boats were launched successfully, so no single figure is certain.

The cost is recorded in the report's own tables. Of 39 infants aboard, 4 survived. Of the children, 18 of 51 boys and 13 of 39 girls survived. About 1,195 of the 1,959 people on board died.

The lesson of the lifeboats is in the gap between the inspection and the emergency. Every check the ship had passed measured the boats while the ship was upright, still and calm: how many, how old, whether the ropes ran. The inspections described in the report covered the boats, the life-jackets and the buoys, which is the right list for a calm day. Boarding a boat from a steeply sloping deck, in minutes, with the ship still moving, is a different test. The safeguards existed, and they were real. They had been tested against the situation people expected, not the one that arrived.

[[lessons]]
$$**Test a safeguard under the conditions it exists for: rushed, tilted and short of hands.** A backup, a drill or a checklist that passes in good weather tells you only that it works in good weather, so run your recovery plan with the clock ticking, with half the team missing and with the system already degraded. The Lusitania's lifeboats were inspected and in order, and still could not be used as designed. Write down the one condition in which your safeguard would fail, and test that one first, because a passed inspection is a record of a single test and not a promise.$$

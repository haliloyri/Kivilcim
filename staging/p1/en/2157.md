At the London School of Economics, a student is allowed to choose his own tutor. He picks a philosopher whose book on open societies had left a deep impression on him. Decades later, that choice sits underneath the way he places bets with other people's money, and the first rule is that he expects to be wrong.

[[story]]
Nassim Taleb's *Fooled by Randomness* spends much of its time among traders, and traders are a good place to watch how people live with chance. George Soros is a well-known case. As a student he chose Karl Popper as his tutor, because Popper's *The Open Society and Its Enemies*, published in 1945, had made a profound impression on him. Popper held that empirical truth cannot be known with absolute certainty, and Soros took that idea into finance.

He built his own framework on two words, fallibility and reflexivity. In his own account, people's views of the world never perfectly match the actual state of affairs. Those imperfect views also feed back into the situation they describe, because people act on them. In markets this means prices do not simply reflect fundamental value, and the participants, himself included, are part of what moves them.

The practical part is plainer. On his own site he says that he must allow for the risk that he is bound to be wrong some of the time. If he finds himself proven wrong, he takes a loss. If he is right, he may increase the bet. Nothing in that sequence requires him to understand everything. It requires a rule for the day the market disagrees.

Then there is 16 September 1992, known in Britain as Black Wednesday. The pound left the European Exchange Rate Mechanism that day, after the Bank of England had spent heavily buying sterling without stopping its fall. Soros's Quantum Fund was widely reported to have bet against the pound, and published accounts put its profit at around a billion dollars, though the figures differ from source to source. Estimates of the cost to the British Treasury ran above three billion pounds.

It would be too neat to say that one idea produced that trade. A single success proves little about a method, and Soros has had losses too. What the story does show is the shape of the stance. A trader who believes he is right has no reason to define his exit. A trader who assumes he will be wrong some of the time has to decide, before the trade, what evidence would change his mind and what he will do when it arrives.

[[lessons]]
$$**Decide before you commit what would prove you wrong, and what you will do when it does.** A café owner who extends the opening hours, a manager who restructures a team or a friend who lends a large sum all start with confidence, and confidence gives them no reason to name a stopping point. Writing one sentence, such as "if this has not changed by the end of the quarter, I will reverse it," moves the decision from the moment of pressure to a calmer moment. It lets you change course early and without having to defend the original choice.$$

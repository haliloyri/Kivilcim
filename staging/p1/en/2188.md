[[story]]
Scott Young, the author of Ultralearning, was unhappy with a self-portrait he had drawn. Frustration with that picture was a main reason he started a project he documented on his own site: learning to draw portraits in 30 days. Before the challenge he spent about 20 hours studying Drawing on the Right Side of the Brain, and he made a second self-portrait on day one. The third came at the end.

The schedule was simple. He planned five hours a day, Monday to Friday, which added up to about 100 hours over the month. He drew with pencil and paper only and ruled out projection and grid methods, two common ways to copy a face onto paper. To find his mistakes he used the image editor GIMP to lay a reference photo over his drawing, so the errors showed up as visible gaps.

The part worth noting is how often he changed the drill. At first he did line drawings, four to a page, each taking 15 to 20 minutes. Then he went to the other extreme: 20 to a page, 20 seconds each, about 100 drawings in all. Finally he settled on four to a page at three to four minutes each. Each version asked for a different kind of attention, slow in one and very quick in the next.

Halfway through, he also changed his method for building a face. He switched to a triangulation and blocking approach, working from the outside in, which he took from a portrait drawing course by Vitruvian Studios. Instead of starting with an eye and hoping the rest would fit, he placed the large shapes first and checked them against the photo.

Young presents the result as three pictures side by side: the frustrating one from before, the one from day one after roughly 20 hours of general study, and the one from day 30 after about 100 hours of focused practice. This is his own report, and the pictures are his own judgment too, but the structure of the method is easy to see. It had a clear test, quick feedback and drills that changed when they stopped teaching him something.

The overlay deserves the most attention. Drawing is a skill where it is very easy to believe a face looks right. An overlay removes that room for belief and turns it into a gap you can see.

[[lessons]]
$$**Pick a check that shows your mistake at a glance, then shrink the drill until you can repeat it many times.** Practice only improves you when errors come back to you quickly and clearly. Young's overlay did that for drawing, and his 20-second sketches let him repeat the lesson about a hundred times in a short stretch. In your own work the same idea might be a recorded call you replay, a draft that a colleague marks up the same day, or a test that fails within seconds. Without a fast signal, hours of effort can simply repeat the same slip.$$

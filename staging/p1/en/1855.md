Twenty volunteers live on a hospital ward for a month and eat whatever is put in front of them, as much as they like. Both menus are built to match on paper, yet the volunteers do not eat the same way on both. The scales pick it up within days.

[[story]]
One question runs through Chris van Tulleken's *Ultra-Processed People*: does ultra-processed food make people eat more, regardless of the sugar and fat inside it? One of the best-known tests of that question is a 2019 study led by Kevin Hall at the US National Institutes of Health, published in *Cell Metabolism* in May. Twenty adults moved into a research ward, where everything they ate was provided and watched.

For two weeks the volunteers ate a menu of ultra-processed foods. For another two weeks they ate a menu of unprocessed or minimally processed foods. Everyone tried both, so each person was their own comparison, and differences in age, build or lifestyle could not blur the result. The meals were matched for calories, sugar, fat, fibre and sodium, and the volunteers could eat as much or as little as they wanted.

The breakfasts show how different the two menus looked. One day brought honey-nut cereal and a muffin, another a Greek yogurt parfait. On paper the two plates were almost twins.

The eating was another matter. On the ultra-processed menu the volunteers took in about 508 more calories a day, and they ate faster. After two weeks they had gained an average of 0.9 kilograms, roughly two pounds. On the unprocessed menu they lost the same amount.

That is why the study mattered. People had long argued that if the numbers on the plate are equal, the food must be equal. Here the numbers were held equal, and appetite still moved.

The study does not say why. Speed of eating, texture and how little chewing the food needs are all candidates, but this experiment was not built to separate them. What it shows is narrower and harder to dismiss: with the nutrients matched, something about the food itself still changed how much people ate.

The limits are plain too. The volunteers lived on a ward for two weeks, their average BMI was about 27, and there were only twenty of them. Real life adds shops, budgets, stress and habit. Still, a ward where nobody could snack elsewhere is a rare chance to see what the food alone does.

[[lessons]]
$$**When two foods have similar labels, notice how fast you eat each one and how much you want afterwards.** The label records what a food contains, but it cannot record how easily a food slides into a second portion. Someone who grabs a packaged lunch at their desk and a homemade one on another day might find they are hungry again an hour sooner after one of them. Keeping a simple note for a week gives you a measure of your own that no printed number can supply. Over time it also tells you which foods to keep in the house and which to buy only now and then.$$

On a laboratory bench sits a slab of germanium no bigger than a fingernail, pressed from above by a small plastic wedge wrapped in gold foil. Wires run from the wedge to the rest of the circuit. A weak electrical signal goes in on one side, and a stronger copy of it comes out on the other.

[[story]]
The device on that bench was the first transistor, and it worked for the first time on 16 December 1947 at Bell Labs. Jon Gertner's The Idea Factory follows the people behind it, among them William Shockley, who led the semiconductor group, and the two men who got the first result, John Bardeen and Walter Brattain. Bardeen was a theorist and Brattain an experimenter, and the pairing was typical of how the Labs worked.

According to published histories, the trick was to press two gold contacts, very close together, onto a piece of high-purity germanium. A small signal through one contact controlled a larger current through the other. In other words, the thing amplified. Accounts put the gain at up to about a hundred times, which was enough to show that a solid crystal could do what a vacuum tube did, without a glowing filament or a glass bulb.

Seven days later, on 23 December, Bardeen and Brattain showed the device to Bell Labs officials. The date matters because it shows how short the loop was between a first working result and a demonstration for the people who could decide what happened next. Nobody waited for a polished product. They put a rough, fragile arrangement of foil, wedge and germanium on a table and let it speak for itself.

Shockley spent the following weeks improving on it. In late January 1948 he conceived the junction transistor, a different design that proved easier to make reliably in large numbers. The Labs announced the invention publicly on 30 June 1948. In 1956 Bardeen, Brattain and Shockley shared the Nobel Prize in Physics for it.

The sequence is the point. A crude version came first, worked once, and was shown to decision-makers within a week. A better design followed within weeks, and the public announcement came after both. Each stage did a different job: the first proved the idea, the second made it practical, and the third told the world.

The first transistor was not elegant. Gold foil on a wedge is not how anyone would want to build a million of anything. Its value was that it answered one question with a yes, and that yes was shown to the right people while it was still warm.

[[lessons]]
$$**Show the rough version that works to the people who decide, and improve it afterwards.** A working crude prototype persuades faster than a polished plan, because it turns a claim into something people can watch with their own eyes. At work, that might be a one-page mock-up that someone can click through, a spreadsheet that already calculates the answer, or a single customer using the thing for a week and telling you what broke. Once the rough version has said yes, the better design has a reason to exist, and you have people in the room who want it built and who have already seen it work.$$

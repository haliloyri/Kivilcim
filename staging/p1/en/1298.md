An offer lands in your inbox. The timing works, the people are pleasant, and nothing about it is wrong. Most of us would say yes on reflex. Greg McKeown's test skips the question of whether it is good. It asks only one thing: is your first reaction an unmistakable "Hell yeah!"?

[[story]]
The test appears in *Essentialism: The Disciplined Pursuit of Less*, and it rests on a point McKeown treats as unavoidable. Every choice is a trade-off. Saying yes to one thing always means not doing something else, whether we admit it or not.

Most of us try to dodge that fact. When two options compete, we ask, "How can I do both?" McKeown suggests a different question: "Which problem do I want?" It sounds bleak at first. But it is simply honest about what a calendar can hold. Both options come with a cost, and the useful move is to choose which cost to carry.

To make that choice easier, he borrows a rule from the entrepreneur Derek Sivers. If you are not saying "Hell yeah!" about something, say no.

The appeal of the rule is its bluntness. It treats hesitation as information. If your honest reaction to an invitation is "sure, why not," that lukewarm answer already tells you something about how much you want it.

McKeown then sharpens the idea into a selection rule of his own. If the answer isn't a clear yes, it's a clear no. He also describes it as a 90 percent rule. Take the single criterion that matters most to you, and score the option against it from 0 to 100. If it lands below about 90, treat the score as zero.

That last step is the surprising one. A 75 is a respectable score. So is an 85. In ordinary life we would call those options pretty good and sign up. Under McKeown's rule, they count exactly the same as a flat refusal.

##The danger isn't the bad offers; it's the mostly fine ones we accept automatically.##

The reasoning goes back to the trade-off. Time and energy are limited. Every mostly-fine opportunity you accept without thinking takes a share of both. When a rare, excellent option finally shows up, the room it needs has already been handed out, one reasonable yes at a time.

So the rule is not about being difficult or saying no for its own sake. It is about keeping space open for the few things that would earn an immediate, wholehearted yes. A good opportunity is hardest to refuse precisely because nothing is wrong with it. McKeown's scoring gives you a reason to refuse it anyway.

[[lessons]]
$$**Every yes hides a no.** McKeown's starting point is that trade-offs cannot be avoided, only ignored. When you agree to something, you are also turning down whatever that time would have gone to, even if you can't see it yet. Asking "Which problem do I want?" puts the hidden no on the table.$$

$$**A higher bar protects the best options.** The 90 percent rule sounds harsh because it turns an 85 into a zero. But the point is not the options you reject; it's the space you keep. A clear-yes-or-clear-no filter stops good enough from quietly filling the room that excellent would need.$$

$$**Hesitation is data.** Sivers's "Hell yeah or no" works because it trusts your first honest reaction. A shrug, a "should be fine," a need to talk yourself into it: under this rule, each of those already counts as the answer.$$

[[reflect]]
- Which recent yes did you give because the offer was fine, not because you wanted it?
- What did that yes quietly take time away from?
- When two options compete in your life right now, which problem would you rather have?

&&The next time an invitation or request arrives, what score would it honestly get against your main priority before you answer, and how many land short of a clear yes?&&

A patient says an iron rod in the doctor's hand is passing a "fluid" into her body, and she trembles and reports being cured. The king wants to know whether any of it is real. The commission he appoints does one thing: it blindfolds the patient.

[[story]]
Carl Sagan describes this inquiry in *The Demon-Haunted World*. In 1784 France, Franz Mesmer claimed that an invisible fluid he called "animal magnetism" surrounded all living things, and that he could steer it to cure disease. Hundreds of people in Paris flocked to his sessions. Some convulsed, some fainted, and many said they felt better.

King Louis XVI set up a commission. It began work in August 1784 with nine members, chaired by Benjamin Franklin. Bailly was the secretary, and the group included the chemist Lavoisier and the physician Guillotin. The commission did not deal with Mesmer himself but with his disciple, Charles d'Eslon.

The members first watched the sessions, then changed one thing. Patients were blindfolded, so none of them could see when the "magnetising" took place. Sometimes a patient was told the treatment was being applied while nothing was done; sometimes it was applied while nothing was said. The result was clear: patients reacted when they believed the fluid was being directed at them, not when it actually was.

The commission's verdict was blunt. There was no evidence that any magnetic fluid existed. The effects came from imagination and expectation. Mesmer lost public support. The commission also produced a second, secret report, which dealt with the moral side of the sessions.

The strength of this experiment is not cleverness. Franklin and his colleagues never denied that patients felt better; they simply tested why. A blindfold removes the question "what do I see because of what I already know?" It is an early example of the blind method that clinical trials rely on today.

And notice what the test did not need. It needed no theory of magnetism, no new instrument and no expert in Mesmer's craft. It needed a strip of cloth and a willingness to be surprised by the result. Few evaluations of anything in daily life are this cheap, yet few are done this way.

[[lessons]]
$$**If you want to test whether something works, stop the person judging it from knowing what was applied.** When you assess a campaign, a new meeting format or a colleague's suggestion, what you expect shapes what you see. Because of that expectation, you count whatever goes well as proof and whatever goes badly as chance. Have someone who does not know which is which make the comparison, or write down what you will measure before you look at any results. Then the measurement speaks, not your hope.$$

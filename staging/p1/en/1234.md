Many people have heard the story. A traveler has a drink with a stranger, blacks out, and wakes up in a hotel bathtub full of ice, with a note saying to call 911 because one of his kidneys has been removed. It never happened. Yet people remember it for years. Chip and Dan Heath wanted to know why.

[[story]]
*Made to Stick* opens with an experiment you can run on yourself. First, the Heaths tell the kidney-heist legend, with its bathtub, ice, note and missing kidney. Then they offer a paragraph of a very different kind: dry, abstract language about maximizing value, aligning goals and delivering results.

The question is simple. An hour later, which one can you remember?

Almost everyone remembers the kidney story. Almost no one remembers the paragraph. That's strange when you think about it. The kidney story is false. The paragraph might be describing something true and important. Yet one sticks and the other slides away.

##The Heaths spent years studying why some ideas stick in people's minds while others vanish, and they found clear patterns.##

Sticky ideas, they argue, tend to share six qualities. The initial letters spell a word: **SUCCESs**.

They are **Simple**: stripped down to a core message. They are **Unexpected**: they surprise us and hold our attention. They are **Concrete**: full of images and details we can see and feel. They are **Credible**: they carry their own evidence. They are **Emotional**: they make us care. And they are told as **Stories**: they put people in situations we can imagine.

The kidney legend hits almost all of these. The corporate paragraph hits almost none. It's abstract, predictable and bloodless. Nothing in it can be pictured.

The Heaths identify one big obstacle to making ideas stick, especially for experts. They call it the **Curse of Knowledge**. Once you know something well, it's very hard to imagine what it was like not to know it. So experts naturally talk in abstractions, summaries and jargon, because to them those words are full of meaning. To everyone else, they're empty.

That's why a doctor, engineer or manager can explain something perfectly accurately and still be forgotten by the time the listener reaches the door. The problem isn't the idea. It's the packaging.

The fix, according to the Heaths, is to fight abstraction. Replace general claims with specific examples. Replace statistics with people. Replace "improve customer satisfaction" with the story of one customer whose day you actually changed.

[[lessons]]
$$**Concrete beats abstract.** The kidney legend is vivid; you can picture the bathtub and the ice. The corporate paragraph gives the mind nothing to hold. The Heaths show that people remember what they can see. Turning abstract claims into specific images and examples is one of the most reliable ways to be remembered.$$

$$**Sticky ideas have a shape.** Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional, Stories: the SUCCESs principles work as a checklist. Few messages need all six, but most forgettable ones have almost none. Checking your idea against the list quickly shows why it isn't landing, and what to add.$$

$$**Beware the Curse of Knowledge.** Experts speak in abstractions because they can no longer imagine not knowing. That makes clear, correct ideas surprisingly hard to communicate. Deliberately picturing a newcomer, and speaking to them with concrete examples, is how you break the curse.$$

[[reflect]]
- Which of your important messages at work is too abstract for anyone to remember?
- What concrete example, image or short story could replace it tomorrow?
- Where might the Curse of Knowledge be making your explanations harder to follow?

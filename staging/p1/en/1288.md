As a young boy, David Goggins worked late nights at his father's roller-skating rink in Buffalo, New York, and went to school the next morning exhausted. At home, his father was violent toward his mother and the children. What does anyone do with a start like that? Goggins' answer, in *Can't Hurt Me*, is not the one you might expect.

[[story]]
The early pages of *Can't Hurt Me*, David Goggins' 2018 memoir, do not soften anything. His father was violent toward his mother and toward the boys. The family business was a roller-skating rink, and David, still a young child, was made to work there late into the night. He often arrived at school worn out.

Eventually his mother fled, taking David and his brother with her. David ended up with her in a small town in Indiana, with very little money.

Leaving did not make life easy. In Indiana he faced open racism. He also fell far behind at school. For years, he has written, he got by by copying other students' work instead of actually learning to read well. It worked in the sense that it kept him moving forward on paper. It did not work in the sense that the gap kept growing underneath.

Look at that list for a moment: a violent father, nights of work as a small boy, poverty, racism, a school system he was faking his way through. None of it was his choice. None of it was his fault. Goggins does not pretend otherwise. He treats the harm as real and never calls it deserved or minor.

But he makes a second claim that sits right beside the first. ##The harm was real, and living as its victim was the part that kept him stuck.## Both things are true at once in his telling. What happened to him could not be changed. The story he kept telling himself about it could.

That is why the first chapter ends not with a moral but with an assignment. Goggins asks the reader to write down the "bad cards" they were dealt, and to think about how those cards could become fuel rather than an excuse. It is a strange request at first. Most of us either avoid listing our hard cards or keep them close as the explanation for everything. He asks for something else: put them on paper, look straight at them, then decide what to use them for.

His own turn did not come overnight. It came later and more gradually, and the book tells that part separately. What followed was a life almost nobody would have predicted for the exhausted boy from the rink. He became a Navy SEAL. He completed Army Ranger School. He ran ultramarathons. He set a Guinness record for pull-ups.

The point of the opening chapter is not that hardship builds champions. The point is narrower and more useful: you can admit fully what was done to you and still refuse to let it be the whole story.

[[lessons]]
$$**Name the cards before you use them.** Goggins' first challenge is simply to write down the bad cards you were dealt. That sounds small, but it breaks two habits at once: hiding the hard parts, and hiding behind them. A card on paper can be looked at, weighed, and put to work. A card you never name just runs in the background.$$

$$**Real harm and a new story can coexist.** Goggins never says his father's violence, the poverty or the racism were minor. He says they happened, and that living as their victim kept him stuck. You do not have to shrink what was done to you in order to stop it from writing every chapter.$$

$$**A hidden gap keeps growing.** Copying classmates' work got him through years of school, but it only postponed the moment he had to face how far behind he was. Covering a weakness buys time; the bill still comes.$$

[[reflect]]
- Which card from your past do you most often use to explain why something can't work for you?
- Is there a skill you've been getting by without, the way Goggins copied his way through school?
- When you describe a hard stretch of your life, do you tell it as something that happened to you, something you used, or both?

&&If you wrote down one card you were dealt, what is one way it has made you stronger, or still could?&&

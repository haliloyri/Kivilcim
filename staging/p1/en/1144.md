Before one of the hardest swims of SEAL training, the instructors gave the trainees a short lesson in marine biology. They calmly listed the kinds of sharks living in the water, including great whites. Then they sent the trainees in anyway, at night.

[[story]]
In *Make Your Bed*, William H. McRaven describes a series of long swims that every SEAL trainee has to complete to pass basic training. One of them is a night swim off San Clemente Island, in the Pacific off the coast of California.

The waters there are known for sharks. The instructors made sure the trainees knew it. Before the swim, they went through the species living in those waters, great whites included. Then they offered a reassurance of sorts: no student had ever been eaten, at least not recently.

Then came the instructions. McRaven remembers them clearly.

> If a shark begins to circle your position, stand your ground. Do not swim away. Do not act afraid.

And if the shark, hungry for a midnight snack, darted toward you? Then you were to summon every ounce of strength you had and punch it in the snout. The shark, they promised, would turn and swim away.

##It was, McRaven admits, not especially comforting advice.##

But it was honest advice, and it wasn't really about marine biology. Everyone going into that water was afraid. The instructors weren't asking them not to be. They were asking them to control what fear made them want to do. Swimming frantically away from a predator signals weakness and makes you look like prey. Holding your position, calmly, signals something else.

McRaven turns this into one of the lessons of his book. There are a lot of sharks in the world, he writes. If you hope to complete the swim, you're going to have to deal with them.

The sharks in ordinary life take different forms. A boss who tries to intimidate you. A competitor who circles your work waiting for a mistake. Critics who seem to smell fear. A problem that won't go away no matter how long you avoid it. In each case, the instinct is the same as in the water: get away, as fast as possible.

McRaven's advice is the same too. Don't back down. Don't show more fear than you have to. Stand your ground, and if it comes to it, push back hard.

He doesn't claim this makes the fear disappear. It makes you the one deciding what happens next.

[[lessons]]
$$**Courage means going in afraid.** No trainee entered that dark water without fear, and the instructors didn't pretend otherwise. They listed the sharks out loud. The courage wasn't in feeling calm. It was in swimming anyway, and in choosing not to let fear decide how you moved once you were out there.$$

$$**Running often makes it worse.** "Do not swim away. Do not act afraid." Fleeing signals weakness and invites the attack you're trying to avoid. The same is true with bullies, intimidating bosses and aggressive critics: steady, visible calm often makes them lose interest faster than retreat does.$$

$$**Every worthwhile swim has sharks.** McRaven's line is plain: if you want to finish, you'll have to deal with them. Sharks aren't a sign you chose the wrong goal. They're part of any goal worth reaching, and expecting them makes them less paralyzing when they show up.$$

- Which "shark" is circling one of your goals right now?
- When did backing away from a problem or a person make things worse?
- When you feel threatened, what does fear usually make you do: freeze, flee, or over-explain yourself?

[[open]]
A researcher takes a tumor from a chicken, filters the extract until no cell can pass through, and gives the clear liquid to other chickens. Tumors grow in the birds. Nobody in his field seems ready to accept what that means, and he will wait for decades.

[[story]]
Peyton Rous worked at the Rockefeller Institute, and this story is a real episode from the history of cancer, the subject of Siddhartha Mukherjee's *The Emperor of All Maladies*. It is about an experiment that was right, a field that was not ready for it, and how long the wait turned out to be.

In 1911 Rous published his results in the Journal of Experimental Medicine. He had taken a sarcoma from a chicken and prepared a cell-free filtrate from it. When he gave that filtrate to other fowl, they developed tumors. Something in the liquid, too small to be filtered out with the cells, had passed the cancer on. We now call it the Rous sarcoma virus.

At first the finding was dismissed. Part of the difficulty is easy to see: a cancer that could be carried by something small enough to slip through a filter would have sat awkwardly beside what people then understood about the disease.

Recognition came much later. In 1966 Rous shared the Nobel Prize with Charles Huggins, and he was 87 years old. Fifty-five years separate the experiment from the prize.

The design of the experiment deserves a second look. A cell-free filtrate rules out the simplest explanation, which is that living tumor cells were simply transplanted from one bird to the next. If the liquid still produced tumors, the cause had to be something other than the cells themselves.

Think about what the gap means. The experiment itself did not change in all those years. The chickens, the filtrate and the tumors were exactly what they had been in 1911. What changed was the surrounding knowledge, which at last had room for a result that could not be fitted in before.

A fair reading of the story avoids two easy lessons. One is that the establishment is always wrong, which the record does not show. The other is that genius always waits. The narrower and more useful point is that a correct result can be unwelcome for reasons that have nothing to do with the evidence, and that the person holding it has to survive the gap.

[[lessons]]
$$**If your result is sound and the world is not ready for it, keep the record clean and keep working until the world catches up.** You may have an idea in your own field that your team, your manager or your customers dismiss for now, whether it is a process, a product or a way of reading the data. Write down exactly what you did and what you saw so that anyone can check it later, and keep returning to it when the surrounding knowledge shifts. Rous had no way of shortening the wait, but a documented experiment was still sitting there when people finally came looking.$$

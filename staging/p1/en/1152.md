In the camps, you might expect the physically strongest prisoners to be the ones who endured. Viktor Frankl, a psychiatrist who was himself a prisoner, noticed that it wasn't always so. Something less visible seemed to matter just as much, and sometimes more.

[[story]]
Viktor Frankl was a psychiatrist and neurologist in Vienna. During the Second World War, he was deported and held in several Nazi concentration camps, including Auschwitz and Dachau. His wife, his parents and his brother all died in the camps. At Auschwitz, even the manuscript of the book he had been working on was taken from him.

In *Man's Search for Meaning*, he describes that experience not only as a survivor, but as a doctor watching what happened to the people around him.

One of the things he noticed was surprising. Some prisoners who were physically frail, but who had a rich inner life, seemed to endure the camps better than some who were much more robust. Strength of body mattered. But it didn't settle everything.

What seemed to matter was whether a person still had something to live for.

Frankl describes two men in the camp who had both spoken of ending their lives. Their reason was the one many prisoners felt: they had nothing more to expect from life. Frankl tried to turn the question around. The point wasn't what they could expect from life, but what life still expected from them.

For the first man, it was his child, whom he adored and who was waiting for him in a foreign country. For the second, a scientist, it was a series of books he had not yet finished, work that no one else could complete.

##Each of them found something waiting for him, and that changed what he could bear.##

Frankl himself held on to something similar. He longed to reconstruct the manuscript that had been taken from him at Auschwitz, and he later wrote that this wish helped keep him going.

He summed it up with a line from Nietzsche:

> He who has a why to live for can bear with almost any how.

Frankl was careful not to romanticize suffering. Meaning did not make the camps less horrific. What it did was give a person a reason to endure them. And he saw one freedom that no one could take away. Everything could be stripped from a prisoner, he wrote, except the last of the human freedoms: to choose one's attitude in any given set of circumstances.

These observations became the foundation of logotherapy, his approach to psychotherapy built around a single idea: our deepest drive is the search for meaning.

[[lessons]]
$$**A why makes the how bearable.** The two men who had spoken of suicide didn't find their circumstances any easier. What changed was that each remembered something waiting for him: a beloved child, unfinished books. Nietzsche's line captures it. With a strong enough reason, people can carry astonishing weight.$$

$$**The last freedom can't be taken.** Frankl lost his family, his work and his freedom. Yet he insisted one thing remained: the choice of attitude toward whatever happens. That isn't a small consolation. In situations you truly can't change, it may be the only real power you have left.$$

$$**Inner life is a form of strength.** Frankl noticed that some frail prisoners with rich inner lives endured better than some physically robust ones. What you carry inside, like relationships, purpose and memories, isn't a luxury. In the hardest moments, it can be what holds you up.$$

[[reflect]]
- What is your "why" right now: a person, a task, something that only you can finish?
- In a situation you can't change, what attitude are you currently choosing?
- When things get hard, what do you usually ask: what can I expect from life, or what does life expect from me?

&&Who or what is waiting for you, and how could remembering it change the way you carry today?&&

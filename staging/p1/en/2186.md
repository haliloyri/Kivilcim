[[open]]
An exam paper printed from a public website sits on the desk. There are no notes, a three-hour clock, and an official answer key that stays closed until the last minute. The person taking the test is also the one who will mark it, and nobody else will ever check the arithmetic.

[[story]]
Scott Young, who later wrote Ultralearning, set himself an odd target: the entire four-year computer science curriculum of MIT, in twelve months. Young was never enrolled at MIT. He worked from the free course materials MIT publishes online, and he began on 1 October 2011 after a trial run with a physics course that summer. He finished on 26 September 2012, just under a year later.

The workload was heavy. By his own account he started at around 60 hours a week and settled at about 35 later on. The part that makes the story worth telling, though, is how he handled the exams. He took them under student-like conditions: no peeking at the questions beforehand, no notes, the standard time limit, usually three hours. Then he graded his own paper against MIT's official solutions, and a course counted as done if he scored a pass on the final, which he describes as typically at least 50 percent.

Self-grading invites generosity, and Young said so himself. He gave part marks when a student had the right concept but slipped in a long calculation. On his own site he later noted that a stricter recalculation would have dropped five finals below the passing line, while the other courses were unaffected or still passed. He also listed the rest of the weak spots. Some courses had no final, so he used practice midterms. Some official solutions were restricted to MIT members, so he could not post them. Programming and lab work was only partly completed, and some labs resisted objective judging.

The logic is simple. An answer key does not care how you feel, the exam clock does not negotiate, and whatever you wrote before opening the key can no longer change. Young's method put his own judgment behind those three fixed things.

Failure had its own rule. If he failed a class, he would take a different exam rather than argue with the first one. He called it a fail-first approach.

The result was a public record that could be checked and argued with. He did not earn a degree and said so plainly. What he wanted was proof that he could do the material, and he had built a method that gave him an answer harder to fool than his own feelings about it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Measure your learning against an outside standard, and publish the places where it is weakest.** Feeling prepared proves little and feels the same whether it is earned or not, because the mind grades its own work kindly. A past exam, an official answer key or a colleague's review gives you a number you did not choose. Write down where that number is generous to you, as Young did with his part marks, and the weak spots become your next study list instead of a hidden risk. Anyone who reads your honest record will also trust the strong parts more, since you showed where you could have cut corners.$$

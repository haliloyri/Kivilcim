First thing in the morning, a blank page. Julia Cameron's instructions for it are short. Three pages. By hand. And they don't have to be good. In fact, they're not supposed to be. That's an odd prescription for people who feel creatively stuck, and it's the core tool of *The Artist's Way*.

[[story]]
Julia Cameron developed the practice while teaching creativity workshops to people who felt blocked. In the book, she presents it as the basic tool of her twelve-week program. She calls it "morning pages."

The rules are simple. Every morning, first thing, you write three pages in longhand. Not typed. By hand.

What goes on the pages is stream of consciousness: whatever is in your head, in whatever order it comes. Cameron is clear that the pages can be petty, whiny, repetitive or boring. She says they are not art. There is no wrong way to do them.

That's the surprising part. The tool for creative block is writing that is explicitly not meant to be good.

##The pages aren't there to produce anything; they're there to clear the way.##

Cameron describes morning pages as a way to clear the mind of the worries and clutter that sit between you and your creative work. Instead of carrying them around all day, you put them on paper.

Her view reverses a common picture of being stuck. The usual assumption is that a blocked person has run dry and has nothing to say. Cameron's picture is closer to the opposite: the mind is crowded, and the crowd gets in the way.

The speed matters too. Writing quickly, without stopping to edit, is meant to get past what she calls the inner Censor. That's the critical voice that judges creative work, the one that tells you a sentence is bad before you've finished writing it. When the standard is zero and the pace is fast, the Censor has less to grab onto.

Cameron adds two more instructions that protect the practice. Don't show the pages to anyone. And don't reread them, at least in the early weeks.

Both rules take away an audience. If no one else will read the pages, there's no one to perform for. If you won't read them yourself for a while, there's no future judge either. What's left is writing that exists only to get things out of your head.

It's worth noting how Cameron frames the results. The benefits she describes come from her own interpretation and from what her students reported, not from a controlled study. She offers morning pages as a practice to try, and her claim is about what the practice is for: clearing space so the work you actually want to do has room to start.

Three pages, by hand, every morning. None of it meant to be good.

[[lessons]]
$$**Block can be clutter, not emptiness.** Cameron treats morning pages as a way to clear worries and noise from the mind. That reframes being stuck. If the problem is too much in your head rather than too little, then the first step isn't finding an idea. It's making room for one.$$

$$**A zero standard gets past the critic.** Cameron's insistence that the pages are not art is the whole trick. Written fast and unedited, the pages give the inner Censor nothing to judge. When quality isn't the goal, the critic's usual objections stop applying, and the writing can keep moving.$$

$$**Privacy removes the performance.** Not showing the pages to anyone, and not rereading them early on, means no one is watching, including you. Without an audience, there's no reason to polish, and the pages can stay what Cameron intends: a place to empty out.$$

[[reflect]]
- What's on your mind first thing in the morning, before you've done anything?
- Where does the need to make something good stop you from starting at all?
- What would you write if you knew no one, including you, would ever read it?
&&Tomorrow morning, before anything else, could you write three longhand pages of whatever comes up, then notice whether anything in the day feels a little clearer?&&

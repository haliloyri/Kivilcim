An engineer sits down to choose a chip for a new product. On the data sheet, the rival's chip looks better than the one you sell. The deciding question is one the data sheet cannot answer: who will help when my design stops working?

[[story]]
The target was a number: 2,000. In 1980 Intel set itself the goal of winning 2,000 design wins for its 8086 microprocessor, meaning 2,000 customer products designed around that chip. The campaign was called Operation Crush. Andy Grove was an Intel executive in those years, and *High Output Management* grew out of the way he ran the company, so the episode belongs to the same world as the book.

The reason for the campaign was a slide. According to the Computer History Museum's record of a panel of its participants, Intel began to see its image and market share slipping in late 1979, with field reports of the problem reaching top management. Rivals such as Motorola, with its 68000 chip, were competing for the same customers. The company's answer was to mobilize everyone at once, with a Crush team of nine executives drawn from across the business.

What they chose to sell is the interesting part. They did not argue that the 8086 was the faster or more elegant chip. They sold a complete solution: the chip, plus the development tools a customer needed to build with it, plus continuing product support, plus Intel's record on quality, reliability and service. An engineer who picked Intel was picking a supplier who would still be there on the day the design failed.

The count at the end was well beyond the goal. Participants later recalled about 2,300 design wins, and Intel's own history page puts the figure at almost 2,500. Whichever number you prefer, the target of 2,000 had been passed.

One of those wins mattered more than the rest. Earl Whetstone, an Intel field sales engineer, used the same approach to pitch the 8086 family to Don Estridge, who was building IBM's first personal computer under a one-year deadline. IBM chose the 8088, a variant of the 8086, and the IBM PC launched on 12 August 1981. Intel credits the support and the promise of future generations of chips, as well as performance, for winning the contract.

Look at the shape of it. A single, countable goal gave nine executives and a whole company something to push toward, and the sales pitch gave customers a reason to choose that had nothing to do with a benchmark. The number told everyone whether it was working. The pitch told customers what they were buying.

[[lessons]]
$$**Sell what the customer can do with your product, together with the help that keeps it working.** When a competitor looks better on paper, the data sheet is the wrong place to fight, because the customer is really buying confidence that the result will come out right. Package your tools, your support and your reliability into one offer, and put a number on it that everyone in the company can count. A goal you can count tells you honestly whether the pitch is landing, and it lets a large group of people pull in the same direction without needing a long meeting to agree on what winning means.$$

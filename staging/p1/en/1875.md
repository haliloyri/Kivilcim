Over the Atlantic, in the dark, the autopilot switches itself off. Two young co-pilots are at the controls, the captain is resting, and the airspeed numbers on the display no longer agree with what the aircraft is doing. Nobody in the cockpit yet knows which of those facts matters.

[[story]]
Air France 447 left Rio de Janeiro for Paris on 1 June 2009, an Airbus A330 with 228 people aboard. At 02:10:05 UTC the autopilot disconnected, because iced pitot tubes had stopped giving reliable speed readings. That was the whole of the initial fault. A sensor had frozen.

Two co-pilots were flying, Bonin, 32, and Robert, 37. The captain, Marc Dubois, 58, had left the cockpit at 02:01:46 to rest. So when the plane suddenly had to be flown by hand, the two younger men were alone with it.

The indicated airspeed fell from 274 knots to 52. The aircraft climbed to about 38,000 feet and stalled, meaning the wings had lost the airflow they needed to keep it up. Dubois returned to the cockpit at 02:11:40. One minute and 35 seconds had passed since the autopilot dropped out, and he walked into a situation that had been developing without him.

The plane fell into the ocean and all 228 people died. The flight recorders were found in 2011 at a depth of 3,980 metres, and their data had been downloaded by 15 May 2011. The final report of France's investigation bureau, the BEA, was published on 5 July 2012. Its conclusion was that the crew failed to diagnose the stall.

The logic of the accident sits in that sentence. A frozen speed sensor is a nuisance, but a stall is a different problem with a different response, and the pilots had to tell the two apart while the numbers in front of them contradicted each other. "What broke?" was the wrong question to be stuck on. The question that mattered was "what is the aircraft doing right now?", and answering it required a picture in the head, not just a reading on a screen.

There is also the matter of the clock. The two pilots had seconds, and the captain arrived to a situation that no one could summarise for him cleanly. A good handover needs a clear account, and there was no clear account to hand over.

The numbers one pilot read were the same numbers the other read. More eyes on the display could not by themselves produce the missing picture. A sentence said aloud, which the others could confirm or reject, might have. The report records only that the diagnosis was not made; which sentence would have helped is our own inference.

[[lessons]]
$$**Under pressure, describe the situation in one sentence before you act on it.** When a customer complaint, a failing server and a ringing phone all arrive together, the pull is to chase whichever symptom is loudest, and that can leave the real problem untouched. If you say aloud "here is what I think is happening", the people next to you can correct you in seconds, and you notice for yourself how much you are guessing. The same sentence also gives anyone who joins late something solid to start from. It costs a few seconds and is the cheapest protection you have against solving the wrong problem.$$

Imagine spending months on your best work. It's polished, it ships on schedule, and you're proud of every piece of it. Then launch day comes and almost nobody cares. Nobody is angry. There's just quiet. It's a painful way to fail, because you did everything a good team is supposed to do. Eric Ries went through exactly this, and what he learned from it became the backbone of *The Lean Startup*.

[[story]]
The company was IMVU. Ries and his co-founders had a plan they believed in: an instant-messaging add-on with 3D avatars. The key part of the design was that it wouldn't ask anyone to switch anything. It would plug into the IM networks people already used. Customers could keep their friends and their chat apps and get avatars on top. On paper, it looked like the smart way to grow.

So they built it, and they built it seriously. For months the team worked hard, solved real technical problems and got the product out the door. By the usual measures of a well-run project, they were doing fine. They were talented people working with discipline toward a clear goal.

Then they launched, and hardly anyone showed up.

The team had to find out why, so they did the obvious thing that no amount of planning had done for them. They talked to real users. What they heard was worse than "needs polish." The product had missed at the level of its core strategic assumptions. Customers didn't want an add-on to their existing networks. The idea everything else was built on, plugging into what people already had, was the part they didn't want.

That meant a great deal of the careful work had to be thrown away. It wasn't sloppy work. It was well-made, and it answered a question customers weren't asking.

##Ries concluded that being on time, on budget and technically excellent means little if you're building something nobody wants.##

This is what makes the story useful rather than just sad. The IMVU team didn't fail through laziness or incompetence. They failed while executing well, because all that effort was pointed at something they had never checked. Every productive week had carried them further along a road that led nowhere customers wanted to go.

From this experience and others like it, Ries developed what he called the Lean Startup approach. Instead of disappearing for months and hoping the big reveal lands, you ship a **minimum viable product**, the smallest thing that can test your assumptions with real people. You measure what customers actually do, not what they say or what you hope. Then you learn from it and go around again. He called this the Build-Measure-Learn feedback loop, and the point of the loop is to get through it quickly, before too much has been invested in a guess.

At some point the loop forces a decision, and the book frames it as a plain question:

> Pivot or persevere?

Persevering means the evidence says you're onto something, so you keep going. Pivoting means making a structured change of strategy based on what you've validated. Ries insists that this isn't failure. It's what learning looks like when you act on it. One of the book's examples is Groupon, which didn't begin as Groupon. It started as The Point, a platform for collective action, and changed course into the company people later knew.

[[lessons]]
$$**Effort in the wrong direction moves you away from the goal.** The IMVU team didn't lack drive. They worked for months and shipped a well-built add-on. But every hour spent perfecting a product that plugged into existing IM networks was an hour spent deepening a bet customers would reject. Hard work multiplies whatever direction you're pointed in, and that includes the wrong one.$$

$$**Test the idea before you build out the whole thing.** The team only learned their core assumption was wrong after launch, when they finally talked to users. The Build-Measure-Learn loop moves that conversation to the start. A minimum viable product exists to put your riskiest belief in front of real people while changing course is still cheap, not months later when much of the work ends up in the bin.$$

$$**Count what you've learned, not what you've produced.** The code IMVU threw away was polished output, and it still wasn't progress, because it didn't bring them closer to something customers wanted. Ries's answer is to measure progress by validated learning. That shift is also what makes a pivot respectable. When Groupon moved on from The Point, it wasn't admitting defeat. It was acting on evidence.$$

[[reflect]]
- Name the assumption your current project depends on most. Has a real user ever confirmed it, or has only your team agreed on it?
- Look back at last week. Could you report how busy you were more easily than what you actually found out?
- Think of work you've polished that no one outside has seen. What would it cost to show it now, compared with finding out late the way IMVU did?
- If the evidence told you to pivot tomorrow, would you see it as a failure or as information?

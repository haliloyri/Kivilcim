[[open]]
Two United States senators stand at a microphone on a summer morning and read out a line from a Pentagon web page. The page invites people to trade contracts on events in the Middle East. Within hours, reporters are repeating one word from it, and the word is "betting".

[[story]]
In 2003 the Pentagon's research agency, DARPA, was close to opening an experimental online market called the Policy Analysis Market. It was part of a research programme on using markets to forecast events. Registered traders were to buy and sell contracts tied to political, economic and military developments in the Middle East, and the market prices were meant to work as forecasts. The market was due to open on 1 October 2003.

The logic came straight from the idea behind *The Wisdom of Crowds*. Intelligence agencies have many analysts, each holding a fragment of what is known, and the fragments rarely add up inside any one office. A market gives people a reason to put in what they know, and an easy way to combine thousands of fragments into a single price. If the price moved, that would itself be information.

Then the plan became public. Senators Ron Wyden and Byron Dorgan attacked it publicly in late July 2003. Critics said it amounted to a market on terrorism and assassinations, and they found it grotesque to let anyone profit from such events. Supporters of the idea, among them the economist Robin Hanson, who worked on this line of research, argued that this description was unfair to the project's real aim.

The reaction was fast. The Pentagon scrapped the Policy Analysis Market on 29 July 2003, within a day or so of the controversy becoming loud. Reports from the time say that John Poindexter, who headed the DARPA office involved, announced his resignation soon after, although accounts differ on how directly the market was the cause.

What happened next is worth noticing. The mechanism was never really tested, so nobody learned whether it would have forecast anything well. The debate was about how it looked and what it was called. People heard "bet on terror" and the conversation ended. The strength of the idea, that a price can carry the knowledge of many people, never got a hearing.

You can still disagree with the project. A reasonable person might find even a well-meant market on political violence distasteful. The lesson is not that the critics were wrong or right. It is that a sound mechanism can be killed by how it is described to the people who have to approve it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you propose a new method, describe its purpose in words your most sceptical listener will accept.** A mechanism that works on paper can still die if the first sentence about it sounds like something else. If you want a team to estimate with a vote, a bet or a market, start with the question you want answered and why it matters, and only then explain the tool. Find out which word will make people stop listening and replace it. Doing this takes ten minutes of preparation and may be the difference between your idea being tried and being thrown out.$$

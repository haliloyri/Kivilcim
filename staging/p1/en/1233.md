A group of executives walks into their boardroom and stops. The big conference table is buried under a mountain of work gloves, hundreds of them, each with a price tag attached. No spreadsheet had managed to change their minds. This pile was about to.

[[story]]
In *Switch*, Chip and Dan Heath tell the story of Jon Stegner, who worked at a large manufacturing company. Stegner was convinced the company was wasting a great deal of money through the way it bought supplies. Each factory purchased its own materials, with little coordination. He believed consolidating purchasing could save a huge amount.

The problem was convincing anyone. Stegner knew what would happen if he made his case with charts and numbers. The executives would nod, agree it was interesting, and nothing would change. Purchasing reform didn't feel urgent to anyone.

So he tried something different. He asked a summer intern to investigate a single item: the work gloves used in the company's factories. Find every kind of glove we buy, he said, and what we pay for each.

The intern came back with a remarkable result. The company was buying 424 different kinds of gloves. And often, the same glove was being bought by different factories at very different prices.

##Stegner collected a sample of every one of those gloves, tagged each with its price, and piled them all on the boardroom table.##

Then he invited the division presidents to come and look.

The executives walked around the table, picking up gloves, reading the price tags, finding identical pairs bought at wildly different prices. They were stunned. The waste wasn't an abstract number anymore. It was sitting right in front of them, in a pile they could touch. The glove display became a traveling exhibit that went around the company's plants, and purchasing began to change.

The Heaths use this story to explain how to **motivate the Elephant**, the emotional side of the mind that has to be engaged for change to happen. We usually assume change follows the sequence *analyze, think, change*: present the facts, let people reason, and they'll act. But in many real situations, change follows a different path: *see, feel, change*. People see something that makes them feel the problem, and that feeling moves them.

The Heaths also offer a related insight. When people fail to change, we often call it laziness or resistance. But they note that self-control is a limited resource that gets used up. What looks like laziness is often exhaustion. People who have been making careful choices all day may simply have no energy left to make one more.

[[lessons]]
$$**Feelings move people more than analysis.** Stegner's data alone wouldn't have changed anything. The pile of gloves made executives feel the waste. The Heaths call this see-feel-change. When you need people to act, ask not only what will convince them, but what will make them feel the need.$$

$$**Make the problem visible.** Four hundred and twenty-four gloves on a table turned an abstract inefficiency into something concrete and undeniable. Abstract problems are easy to ignore; physical, visible ones are not. Finding the equivalent of that pile for your own cause can be more persuasive than any report.$$

$$**Resistance may be exhaustion.** Before judging people as lazy or resistant, the Heaths suggest considering that they may simply be depleted. Self-control wears out. Making change easier and less tiring, instead of demanding more effort, often works better than pushing harder.$$

[[reflect]]
- What change have you tried to argue for with data alone, without much success?
- How could you make that problem visible, concrete and impossible to ignore?
- Where might you, or someone else, be judged lazy when the real issue is exhaustion?

&&What is your "pile of gloves," the thing that would make people feel the problem?&&

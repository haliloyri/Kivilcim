Kellogg has prepared a special batch for its visitor: Cheez-Its, frozen waffles and cereal, all made without salt. The visitor is a journalist. The company wants him to taste exactly what it is up against, and the first bite is not going to be kind.

[[story]]
Michael Moss got his taste of the problem inside Kellogg. The company let him into its research and development department and made unsalted versions of its products for him to try. The point was to show him why cutting salt is so hard.

The Cheez-Its, Moss says, stuck to the roof of his mouth, and he could barely swallow. Frozen waffles tasted like straw. The cereal tasted of metal.

Moss explains why. Salt does more than taste salty. It masks the off flavors that processing creates, such as the "warmed-over flavor" of reheated meat. It also costs about ten cents a pound, and Moss calls it a miracle ingredient for these companies. Take it out, and the flavors it was hiding step forward.

There is an earlier chapter in the same story. Robert I-San Lin, a scientist Moss calls wonderful and brilliant, joined Frito-Lay in the late 1970s, just as the FDA began hearings on whether salt was inherently safe. According to Moss, Lin pushed the company to cut back on salt in its own products, for economic reasons. Lin later left the company. Years afterward, at Lin's dining room table, Moss listened to his regrets at not having been able to do more in the late seventies and early eighties.

Put the two scenes side by side. In Kellogg's lab, salt looks like a flavor. In Lin's career it looks like a cost, a regulatory question and a decision that somebody might have made. A simple switch appears in neither.

There is also an imbalance in the work itself. Adding salt costs little and fixes several problems at once. Removing it fixes nothing at once and exposes the problems one by one, in the mouth of whoever is eating the waffle. Under those conditions it is easy to see why leaving the recipe alone looks like the safer bite.

The tasting teaches an order of operations. A person who proposes taking salt out first has to ask what the salt has been quietly doing for the product, because the answer is sitting in that waffle that tastes like straw. Lin pushed from the side of cost and regulation, and the Kellogg trial shows the side of taste. A plan that handled the first two would meet the third in its first bite.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you remove something, find out which job it has been quietly doing.** In Kellogg's trial, taking out the salt left waffles tasting like straw because the salt had been covering for the product. At work, a form field, a weekly meeting or an approval step may be covering for something nobody wrote down, so ask who relies on it and what would break first, since the quiet jobs are the ones nobody defends until they stop being done. Then try the removal on a small batch before you announce it to everyone, so that whatever it was covering shows up where it is cheap to fix.$$

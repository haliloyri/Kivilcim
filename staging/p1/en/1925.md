A company's biggest shareholder wants out and is getting ready to sell. At the door stands a buyer that the management does not want. The managers have a hard option: bring a partner of their own choosing onto the stage, and pay for it with something from their own table.

[[story]]
Salomon Brothers' headache in 1987 was one of its own shareholders. Minorco, a Bermuda-based company controlled by Harry Oppenheimer, held 14 percent of the firm, was its largest shareholder, and wanted to sell. Its 21.3 million shares were worth about $800 million.

One man was interested in those shares: Ronald Perelman. According to common accounts, Michael Milken of Drexel Burnham Lambert stood behind him. Salomon's management was wary, because they suspected Perelman wanted more than the shares themselves. Whoever bought that stake could one day walk through the firm's door and take part in its decisions, and management did not want that.

The answer lay with Warren Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway. Berkshire bought $700 million of Salomon's convertible preferred stock. Preferred stock is a class of shares that ranks ahead of ordinary shares, and in exchange for the investment Berkshire received two seats on Salomon's board.

The logic of the move is simple. Minorco's wish to sell was not something management could control. What management could do was offer an alternative to the question of who would end up holding the shares. Instead of waiting for an unwelcome buyer, it chose to open the door to someone it trusted. Berkshire walked in and sat down at the table with two seats.

It is worth seeing the price. Two board seats meant letting an outside voice into the firm. To ward off one threat, Salomon agreed to share its place at the decision table. It weighed holding control entirely against being protected from a buyer it did not want, and it picked the second.

Convertible means the stock can later be turned into ordinary shares. Berkshire's money was therefore tied to Salomon's near future and its distant one. The positions of the three sides were clear: the seller wanted out, the unwelcome buyer wanted in, and the chosen partner asked for a lasting place. Four years later, in August 1991, the same Buffett would become Salomon's interim chairman, but that is the story of another day.

The question that comes out of the story is this: when danger arrives, do you have an ally whose door you can open? Salomon had an answer, and the answer came with a price.

[[lessons]]
$$**Get to know the ally you trust before the one you do not want comes knocking.** When a threat shows up, your options narrow fast, so a good partner is best met in calmer times. At work that means building a network of trusted advisers, partners or supporters before a difficult client or competitor appears. An ally also costs something, so think early about what you are willing to share, and keep the relationship alive after the alliance is made, or else the pressure of the moment will set the price for you, and your bargaining power is usually at its lowest exactly then.$$

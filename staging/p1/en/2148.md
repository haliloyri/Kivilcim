You receive a booklet with four short stories from your early childhood. Three of them really happened to you, and your own relatives supplied the details. The fourth tells you that you were lost in a shopping mall as a small child. It never happened.

[[story]]
Psychologists Elizabeth Loftus and Jacqueline Pickrell set out to test whether a false childhood memory could be planted in an adult. They published the result in 1995 in the journal *Psychiatric Annals*, in a paper titled "The Formation of False Memories". The method was simple enough to describe in a few lines.

The study had 24 participants. For each one, a relative supplied three real events from the participant's childhood. The researchers added a fourth event they had invented: the participant had been lost in a shopping mall or another public place. The participants were not told that one of the four was false, and they were interviewed about all four events. The invented story sat among three real ones, so it arrived with the same authority as they did.

About a quarter of them, six of the 24, came to report remembering the invented event, at least in part. They usually described it less clearly than the real events, and with fewer words. When the researchers eventually revealed that one story was false, five people did not pick the mall story. They named one of the true events instead.

Loftus called the result an existence proof: it showed that a false memory of an entire event could be created, not how often it happens in life.

The study has been questioned from several sides. Some critics have argued that its method was ethically questionable, and others that it says little about memories of trauma. In 2023, a larger replication led by Gillian Murphy involved 123 participants. The researchers judged that 35 percent had formed a false memory, but only 14 percent said they actually remembered the event. A 2025 reanalysis by Bernice Andrews and Chris Brewin argued that only five of those participants could reasonably be said to have a false memory, and that half of the people classed as false-memory cases had in fact been lost at some point before.

Whichever count is right, the result that is hard to dismiss is the small one, because it needed so little: only a believable story, placed next to true ones. A few people, given a believable story by someone they trusted, started to treat it as part of their own past.

[[lessons]]
$$**How clear a memory feels tells you little about whether it is accurate, so check the memories that matter against a record.** Two siblings may recall the same family holiday very differently, and each can be completely sincere. Vividness and confidence tend to come from how often a memory has been retold and how believable it was when first heard, not from a perfect recording. When a decision, a dispute or a promise depends on what happened, look for a photograph, a message or a note before you rest your case on how sure you feel, and let that record, not your confidence, settle the question.$$

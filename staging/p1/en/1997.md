[[open]]
A man comes home from work and complains about his boss. His wife offers a sensible idea: take the boss to lunch and get to know him. The man, who wanted something else entirely, gets angrier, and the evening goes badly for both of them.

[[story]]
The author of *Supercommunicators* (2024) puts himself first in line. Charles Duhigg had an MBA from Harvard Business School and a career as a journalist, which made him sure he would be good at communication. Then, at The New York Times, he was promoted to manager and asked to run a complex project. By his own telling he was good at strategy and terrible at the communication part.

The feedback kept coming back in the same shape: colleagues felt he was not listening to them. One told him their suggestions were being ignored. Duhigg answered with what he knew how to do, which was to offer practical fixes. The colleague was not asking for fixes. They wanted empathy and respect. He was a professional communicator, he thought, and it surprised him.

The same pattern ran at home. Over a marriage of about twenty years, he says, it went on for something like fifteen or sixteen of them. He would come home and complain about his boss or his coworkers. His wife, sensibly, suggested he take the boss to lunch to get to know him. What Duhigg wanted was for her to be outraged on his behalf. Instead of listening to her he got angrier, and she, being yelled at for no apparent reason, got upset too.

Vacations produced the same scene. When there was a mix-up over a hotel room or a passenger reclined a seat on a plane, he looked for outrage while his wife offered practical suggestions. With his children he had a habit of half-listening.

He did not find the explanation until he began talking to psychologists, neurologists and other researchers. What they showed him is the book's framework: many conversations contain three conversations at once, a practical one about what is really going on, an emotional one about how people feel, and a social one about who they are. Trouble starts when two people are in different ones. His colleague was in the emotional conversation and he was in the practical one. His wife was practical, and he was emotional.

The detail that stays is how ordinary the failure was. Nobody was being unkind. Everyone was trying to help, and each person's help was aimed at a conversation the other was not having.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask which kind of help is wanted, and say which kind you want.** Suppose a sibling calls to talk about a rough week and you start with a plan to fix it, or you vent to a friend who starts drafting solutions. In both cases each person is being useful in a way the other did not ask for. A simple sentence at the start, such as "do you want ideas, or do you want company?", costs nothing and often saves an evening. You can also offer the same sentence about yourself, so that the people close to you know which kind of listening you need.$$

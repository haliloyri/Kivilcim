[[open]]
Under one of the most famous talks on TED's website sits a short note. It says some of the talk's findings are part of an ongoing debate about robustness and reproducibility, and it links to corrections and to the speaker's own response. Few talks carry a label like that.

[[story]]
In June 2012 the social psychologist Amy Cuddy gave a talk on "power posing". According to TED's summary, her idea was that standing in a posture of confidence, even when you do not feel confident, can boost feelings of confidence and may affect your chances of success. The talk travelled far.

Its backbone was a 2010 paper in *Psychological Science* by Dana Carney, Amy Cuddy and Andy Yap. It rested on 42 participants, and the authors reported that brief postures changed testosterone and cortisol levels and willingness to take a risk.

In 2015 Ranehill and colleagues ran the experiment again with 200 participants. The result split in two. People did report feeling more powerful, but no difference showed up in hormones or in risk tolerance.

In 2016 Carney posted her own position on a Berkeley website. She wrote that she did not believe power pose effects are real, and that the evidence had been mounting against them since early 2015. Then she criticised the original study from the inside. The sample was tiny. Data had been collected in chunks and checked along the way. On the risk task she had reported the smaller of two p-values, .052 and .05. An outlier was excluded from the testosterone analysis, and the self-report measure was built from the power questions that "worked". The experimenters knew the hypothesis. And because winning raises testosterone, any hormone difference might have come from winning the gamble, not from posture.

Simmons and Simonsohn, reviewing the published studies the same year, concluded that once selective reporting was accounted for the evidence did not point to an effect. Cuddy and co-authors replied in 2018 that studies of postural feedback show strong evidential value. The argument is not fully closed.

None of these points is fraud in itself. They are small decisions that pile up almost unnoticed in a lab with a small budget, and together they change the result. Carney herself calls them researcher degrees of freedom.

Carney also wrote that she no longer teaches power poses, no longer speaks about them in the media and discourages others from studying them. So the starting point of a famous talk was withdrawn by one of its original authors. What the case shows is how far apart popularity and verification can travel.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you use a widely shared finding, ask whether it has been replicated and what exactly was measured.** A result presented in a meeting as "research shows" may rest on one small study. How a posture makes you feel and whether it changes your hormones are separate claims, and the second turned out to have weaker support. Checking the source, the sample size and whether a larger repeat exists takes a few minutes, and it keeps you from building a team decision on weak ground. You learn how much confidence the idea deserves without having to throw away something you like.$$

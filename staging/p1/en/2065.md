[[open]]
A form for extra retirement saving lies on a desk, and filling it in takes five minutes. Plenty of people push it aside with a "not this month". Offer the same saving as a slice of next year's raise, though, and the answer can change.

[[story]]
Richard Thaler and Shlomo Benartzi started from an awkward fact about retirement plans. Many employees save less than standard economic models say they should, and the two economists blamed bounded rationality and weak self-control rather than ignorance. Someone can agree that saving more is wise and still hesitate when it means handing over a slice of this month's pay.

Their answer was a program called Save More Tomorrow, which Thaler and Cass Sunstein also describe in *Nudge*. The employee agrees in advance that part of every future raise will go into retirement saving. Nothing is deducted today.

When the raise arrives, the saving rate steps up. The pay slip never shows a smaller figure than before, because the extra deduction comes out of money that had not yet reached the account. After the first yes, no later increase needs a new decision.

The trick lies in the order of events: the choice is made now and the cost is paid later. People tend to find it easier to give up money they have not yet received than money already in their wallet, and a single signature became a routine that ran on its own through four annual raises.

The first real test ran at one company, and the results appeared in the Journal of Political Economy in 2004. Of the employees who were offered the plan, 78 percent joined. Of those who joined, 80 percent were still in it after the fourth raise.

The saving rate of the participants climbed from 3.5 percent to 13.6 percent over 40 months. That ten-point gap was built from small steps, each tied to a raise and none requiring a fresh decision from the employee.

The numbers need careful reading, and the first thing to know is their scope. The 13.6 percent is the average for people who joined, not for everyone at the company. A review by the US Department of Labor rates the causal evidence in this study as low, because those who signed up may have been keener savers to begin with. Even so, once the date of the cost moved from today to a future raise, most of the people who were offered the plan took it, and that suggests the timing of a request can matter as much as its size.

[[lessons]]
$$**Attach a hard decision to a specific moment in the future.** A baker who keeps promising to set money aside each month and never does could instead decide that part of every future price rise goes into an oven-replacement fund. The money is taken from earnings that have not arrived yet, so it does not feel like a sacrifice, and keeping the promise gets easier. You can do the same with a choice you keep postponing by tying it to an event that is already certain, such as a raise, the last payment on a loan or the start of a new job, and writing the decision down today.$$

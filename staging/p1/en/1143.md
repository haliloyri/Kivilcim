A four-star admiral who spent his career leading special operations stands in front of a stadium of graduates. They're waiting for grand advice about changing the world. His first piece of it is about bedsheets. He isn't joking.

[[story]]
In May 2014, Admiral William H. McRaven gave the commencement speech at the University of Texas at Austin. McRaven had been a Navy SEAL for decades and had risen to lead U.S. special operations. The speech was built around ten lessons he'd learned from SEAL training. It was shared so widely afterwards that he turned it into a short book, *Make Your Bed*.

The first lesson is the one everyone remembers:

> If you want to change the world, start off by making your bed.

McRaven explains where it came from. In SEAL basic training, the instructors, most of them Vietnam veterans, would show up in the barracks every morning, and the first thing they inspected was your bed. It had to be done perfectly. The corners square. The covers pulled tight. The pillow centered just under the headboard. The extra blanket folded neatly at the foot of the rack.

He admits it seemed a little ridiculous at the time. These were young men training to become some of the toughest warriors in the world, and they were being graded on their sheets.

But over time, he came to see the wisdom in it.

##If you make your bed every morning, you have accomplished the first task of the day.##

It gives you a small sense of pride. And that small win encourages you to do another task, and another, and another. By the end of the day, one task completed has turned into many. Making your bed also reinforces something bigger: that the little things in life matter. McRaven puts it bluntly. If you can't do the little things right, you'll never do the big things right.

Then he adds a second reason, one that's easy to miss. Some days will go badly. Some will be miserable. But if you made your bed that morning, you come home to a bed that is made, and you're the one who made it. It's a small thing. On a hard day, it's a reminder that you still did something right, and a bit of encouragement that tomorrow will be better.

The advice is almost too simple to take seriously, which is exactly why McRaven opens with it. Big changes rarely start with big decisions. They start with small disciplines, repeated until they become who you are.

[[lessons]]
$$**One finished task starts a chain.** McRaven's point is momentum. Making your bed means you've completed something before the day has even really begun. That tiny sense of pride makes the next task easier to start. By evening, the first small win has quietly pulled several more along behind it.$$

$$**Little things train you for big ones.** The SEAL instructors checked the corners of the bed for a reason. "If you can't do the little things right, you will never do the big things right." Precision in something small builds the habits you'll need when the stakes are high and there's no one inspecting.$$

$$**Order can be a comfort on bad days.** The most human part of the lesson is the miserable day. Coming home to a bed you made yourself won't fix what went wrong. But it's proof that you did one thing right, and that small proof can be enough to believe tomorrow will go better.$$

A red telephone hangs on the wall beside a hotel swimming pool in Los Angeles. A guest lifts the receiver, and the voice on the other end answers with a greeting no hotel front desk would ever use. A few minutes later, someone in white gloves crosses the pool deck carrying a silver platter.

[[story]]
Chip and Dan Heath use the Magic Castle Hotel in *The Power of Moments* as an example of a defining moment, and on paper this hotel should not impress anyone. It began as a two-story apartment complex built in the 1950s. Later it was converted into a hotel and painted canary yellow. Chip Heath has joked that its pool "might qualify as Olympic size, if the Olympics were being held in your backyard."

Yet when Stanford's business school wrote about the hotel in early 2018, Tripadvisor ranked it fourth among the 355 hotels it listed in Los Angeles. Rooms cost $199 a night. Only the Beverly Hills Hotel, the Hotel Bel-Air and the Peninsula Beverly Hills stood above it.

The reason is the red phone by the pool. A guest picks it up and hears, "Hello, Popsicle Hotline." The guest names a favorite flavor of ice pop. A few minutes later an employee in white gloves arrives with it on a silver platter, and nobody is charged anything.

Look at how little the hotel actually gives away. A popsicle is one of the cheapest things a hotel could hand a guest. What the staff add is a ritual: a phone that exists for one purpose, a greeting that treats the request as normal, white gloves, a silver platter. The cheap object is delivered as if it were a banquet, and that mismatch is what people remember and repeat to friends.

Heath draws a pointed conclusion about what the hotel does not do. In his words, "Magic Castle isn't worried about changing the awful yellow color of the building or upgrading the bathrooms." Its owners left the flaws alone and put their effort into a small number of moments. His advice is: "You don't have to excel at everything. You only have to excel at a few things that are going to be memorable."

The Heaths file the Popsicle Hotline under what they call elevation, a moment that lifts people above the ordinary run of a day. It works because it is a surprise a guest can start on their own, a small request answered with more care than anyone expected.

[[lessons]]
$$**Spend your care on the few moments people will remember, and let the rest stay ordinary.** Most of a hotel stay, a school year or a job is average, and trying to polish everything makes all of it forgettable. In your own work, choose one touchpoint, such as the first hello, the handover or the goodbye, and give it a small surprise that costs more attention than money. A customer will forget the bathroom tiles but retell the popsicle on a silver platter. Once that one moment is chosen, you also know what can safely stay plain.$$

Picture two numbers side by side. One is roughly what you earn in a year: about $8,000. The other is the figure you've decided to earn next year: $100,000. The second one is sitting where you'll see it every single day, and every time you look at it, it seems a little ridiculous. Can staring at a number actually change anything? Jack Canfield bet that it could.

[[story]]
Early in his career, Jack Canfield, the man who would later co-write *The Power of Focus*, was earning roughly $8,000 a year. It wasn't a disaster, but it wasn't much of a life plan either.

Then he did something that, from the outside, looks almost silly. He set a goal of earning $100,000 in a single year. This wasn't a stretch of a few thousand dollars. It was more than twelve times anything he had ever made. There was no promotion lined up, no business plan that promised it. There was just the number.

He didn't leave the number as a thought in his head. He made a mock $100,000 bill and put it where he couldn't avoid it, somewhere his eyes would land every day. Morning after morning, there it was, reminding him what he'd said he wanted.

The obvious question is the one you're probably asking now: so what? A fake bill doesn't pay rent. Looking at a goal isn't the same as reaching it.

What happened next is the part Canfield keeps coming back to when he tells this story. Over the following months, things he had walked past before started to catch his attention. Ideas he would have dismissed now seemed worth a second look. Opportunities that had probably been around him all along started to stand out. None of them were new. What had changed was that he now noticed them, because he had a reason to.

##That year he earned about $92,000. He missed the target, and he made more than ten times what he had earned before.##

Canfield explains this with a piece of brain machinery called the reticular activating system, or RAS. Your senses take in far more than you could ever consciously process, so the RAS acts as a filter, deciding which incoming information gets flagged as important and which gets quietly ignored. Mostly it does this without asking you. But you can influence it by telling it what matters.

His favorite way to make this concrete is one almost everyone has lived through. Say you start thinking about buying a particular car, a certain model, maybe a certain color. Suddenly you see that car everywhere: in parking lots, at traffic lights, in your neighbor's driveway. Those cars didn't just appear on the roads. They were there the whole time. Your filter simply started flagging them because you'd decided they were relevant.

That, in Canfield's telling, is what the mock bill did. By putting a $100,000 goal in front of himself every day, he was pointing his filter at anything that might help him get there. The opportunities didn't arrive out of nowhere. He finally started to see them.

So the answer to the question, whether looking at a number every day can change anything real, turned out to be a qualified yes. The number didn't do the work. It changed what he paid attention to, and he did the work from there. By his own account, a man earning about $8,000 finished the year at around $92,000.

[[lessons]]
$$**Your attention follows your goals.** The opportunities that helped Canfield weren't new. They'd been sitting in plain sight, like all those cars of the model you're considering. Once he kept a clear goal in front of him, his reticular activating system started flagging whatever was relevant to it. If you want to notice different things, you have to give your filter something different to look for.$$

$$**A missed big target can still be a win.** Canfield fell about $8,000 short of $100,000, which happens to be roughly what he used to earn in an entire year. If he had set a "sensible" goal of, say, $12,000, he might have hit it and called it a success. Aiming at a number that looked absurd stretched his sense of what was possible, and even falling short left him more than ten times ahead of where he'd started.$$

$$**Make the goal something you can see.** Canfield didn't just resolve to earn more. He made a mock $100,000 bill and kept it in view every day. A vague wish is easy to set aside the moment life gets busy. A physical, specific object you can't avoid keeps telling your mind that this goal is real and current, and that is what keeps the filter pointed the right way.$$

[[reflect]]
- Think of one goal that exists only in your head right now. Where would you actually see it if you wrote it down?
- Recall the last time you noticed something "everywhere" after deciding it mattered. What could you point that same filter at?
- Look at your current targets. Are any of them so safe that hitting them wouldn't change much?
- If you aimed ten times higher and fell a little short, where would that leave you compared to today?

&&If your most important goal were written where you'd see it every morning, what would you start noticing that you miss today?&&

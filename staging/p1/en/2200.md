A woman stands in her vegetable garden and counts. A small plane crosses her land, turns and crosses again. Before the day is over she has counted fourteen passes by that one aircraft. Within months she will be organising a lawsuit against the United States government.

[[story]]
Marjorie Spock lived in Brookville, on Long Island, and in spring 1957 planes sprayed her neighbourhood with DDT as part of a federal campaign against the gypsy moth. According to a published account of the case, she counted fourteen passes by a single plane in one day, and afterwards reported dead pea plants, withered berries and wood thrushes that had disappeared. The government programme itself was large. A Supreme Court justice later described a 1956 Department of Agriculture campaign to treat more than three million acres in ten states with a mixture of DDT and kerosene.

Spock did not just complain. She organised a group of residents, the Committee Against Mass Poisoning, and found more than a dozen plaintiffs, among them the ornithologist Robert Cushman Murphy. Their lawyer argued that the spraying was not authorised by statute and violated the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments. Defendants included Agriculture Secretary Ezra Benson.

The legal road was not kind to them. The district court refused a preliminary injunction, and the spraying went ahead while the case was pending. A trial followed in Brooklyn in February 1958, in which the court found the spray was not injurious to human health but declined to make findings about its effect on milk and crops. The government then argued that the case was moot, since the spraying was finished, and the Court of Appeals agreed and directed that it be dismissed.

The plaintiffs took it to the Supreme Court. On 28 March 1960 the Court refused to hear it. Justice William O. Douglas dissented. He wrote that he expressed no view on the merits, but that the questions were too important to go unreviewed, and he listed what the plaintiffs had reported: milk from one dairy contaminated months later, vegetables and fruit made inedible, fish and birds dead, and some children coughing with watery eyes.

So the plaintiffs lost. What did they have afterwards? A court record in which experts had disagreed sharply about DDT, a published dissent that said the issue deserved a hearing, and a network of people who had worked together. The same account says that Rachel Carson's literary agent telephoned the Spock household on Carson's behalf, because Carson wanted to learn about the case. Spock sent her evidence.

[[lessons]]
$$**Count a loss by what it leaves behind: the record, the allies and the question that is now on paper.** A formal defeat feels like a full stop, yet the people who lost in Brooklyn and Washington still had testimony, a dissent and a group that knew how to act together. In your own work, after a rejected proposal or a lost vote, ask what was written down, who now knows the subject and which question can no longer be ignored. Then pass those things on to the person who can use them next, so that the effort is not lost with the decision.$$

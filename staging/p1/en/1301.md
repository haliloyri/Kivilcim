How does a family get its water? Hans Rosling likes to start there. Someone walks barefoot with a bucket. Someone rides a bicycle to the well. Someone turns on a tap. Someone else chooses between hot and cold. That makes four answers, and the usual picture of the world only has room for two.

[[story]]
In *Factfulness*, written with Ola Rosling and Anna Rosling Rönnlund, Rosling names what he calls the most common misconception about the world. It is the belief that humanity is split into two groups: "developed" and "developing," rich and poor, with a gap between them. He calls the habit of thinking this way the gap instinct.

To replace it, he divides humanity into four income levels. The numbers are rounded, the way he rounds them in the book.

Level 1 is below about $2 a day. Roughly 1 billion people live there. Level 2 runs from about $2 to $8 a day, and holds about 3 billion. Level 3 is about $8 to $32 a day, home to roughly 2 billion. Level 4, above $32 a day, has about 1 billion people.

Add the middle two together and the gap instinct starts to fall apart.

##About 5 billion people, most of humanity, live on Levels 2 and 3: right where the supposed gap should be.##

The two-box picture has no place for them. It only shows the ends.

Rosling makes the levels concrete with everyday life, and water and transport do most of the work. At Level 1, people walk barefoot to fetch water. At Level 2, a family may be able to afford shoes and a bicycle. At Level 3, they may have running water and a motorbike. At Level 4, there is hot and cold water at home, and cars.

Put side by side, these are not two worlds with a canyon between them. They are four steps, and each one looks like a real improvement over the one below.

That is also why the gap feels so convincing. If you compare the top of Level 4 with the bottom of Level 1, the difference is enormous. The comparison is true, but it leaves out most people.

So Rosling's advice is practical. Look for the majority, which is usually in the middle. And be wary of comparisons of averages or extremes, because they can make a gap look much bigger than it is. When someone says "the poor" and "the rich," ask where the 5 billion in between went.

[[lessons]]
$$**Two boxes hide the middle.** Rosling's four levels put about 5 billion people on Levels 2 and 3. A rich-versus-poor frame has nowhere to put them, so they disappear from view. Before accepting any split into two groups, ask where the majority actually sits and what their daily life looks like.$$

$$**Daily details beat labels.** "Developing" is abstract. Walking barefoot for water, owning a bicycle, turning on a tap: those are pictures anyone can hold. Rosling uses water and transport so the levels become places you can imagine, and the steps between them become visible.$$

$$**Extremes stretch the gap.** Comparing the richest with the poorest, or one average with another, can make a divide look wider than it is. Rosling's warning is to notice when a comparison skips the middle, because that is where most of the story usually lives.$$

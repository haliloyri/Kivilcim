It is freezing, and in the dark ahead small lights begin to appear along the enemy's parapet. They are candles on makeshift trees. A rifleman in the opposite trench looks across at them. Then he hears singing start up on the other side.

[[story]]
Rutger Bregman closes *Humankind* with the Christmas truce of 1914, and the record of it is better than most legends. Five months into the war, on 7 December, Pope Benedict XV appealed for an official Christmas truce. Both sides refused. Soldiers on the Western Front went ahead on their own.

They had been testing each other for weeks. On 11 December the war diary of the 2nd Essex Regiment noted a meeting with men of a Saxon corps and recorded that "Germans said they were fed up." Two weeks later, on Christmas Eve, there was frost and in places snow. Rifleman Graham Williams of the 5th London Rifle Brigade saw lights appear along the German parapet and later described how the singing went: "First the Germans would sing one of their carols, and then we would sing one of ours." Private Albert Moren of the 2nd Queen's Regiment heard "Silent Night" drift across near La Chapelle-d'Armentières.

Men climbed out and met halfway. They swapped food, drink, buttons, tobacco, chocolate and photographs, and on Christmas Day both sides held burial services for their dead. Some units reported kick-abouts with a ball, though historians still dispute how many matches there were. Estimates put roughly 100,000 British and German troops in these ceasefires, though not every unit knew of them.

The generals were not pleased. General Sir Horace Smith-Dorrien, commanding the British II Corps, wrote: "I have issued the strictest orders that on no account is intercourse to be allowed between the opposing troops."

The end came with another gesture that both sides could read. On Boxing Day, Captain Charles Stockwell of the 2nd Royal Welch Fusiliers wrote, "At 8:30, I fired 3 shots into the air and put up a flag." A German officer answered with a sheet reading "Thank You". They bowed and saluted, a German fired two shots into the air, and the war went on. Through January 1915 British newspapers printed soldiers' letters about it, and in December 1915 Allied commanders ordered raids and shelling to prevent a repeat.

The truce did not stop the war and it was not universal. What the accounts show is how it began and ended: with songs and shots that each side could understand and answer in turn.

[[lessons]]
$$**Start with a small gesture the other side can answer in kind.** When two groups are stuck in a standoff, a team that blames another department or a neighbour you have not spoken to for months, a speech about peace is easy to refuse. A song, a coffee or a short hello can be returned without anyone losing face, and each reply makes the next step smaller. If you want to break a standoff, pick a gesture that takes the other person ten seconds and leaves them a clear way to respond, then wait for what comes back before you try anything bigger.$$

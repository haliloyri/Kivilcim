Every morning a message arrives by Telex at the offices of big investors. It is short commentary on the markets, written by one man in the next room of his own flat. There is no logo to impress anyone and no staff to speak of, only a habit of writing every day.

[[story]]
Bridgewater says that Ray Dalio grew up in a middle-class family on Long Island, where his father was a jazz musician. At twelve he bought his first stock, Northeast Airlines, with money he earned caddying. He barely got into CW Post College, yet he majored in finance and graduated at the top of his class in 1971, and in 1973 he finished an MBA at Harvard Business School.

After two short stints at Wall Street firms, Dalio founded Bridgewater in 1975. The firm began in the two-bedroom apartment where he lived in New York City. The first thing it sold was neither a fund nor a famous name. It advised institutional investors and actively managed their exposures, mainly in commodities and futures, which was the corner of the market Dalio already knew.

To be noticed he wrote. Bridgewater's own account says he produced daily market commentary, the *Bridgewater Daily Observations*, which clients received by Telex. A one-person firm could not compete on size, so it competed on a small, regular, useful habit.

The growth was slow. In 1981 the office moved to a barn in Wilton, Connecticut. In 1985, ten years after the founding, the firm won its first institutional account for direct management, a 5 million dollar mandate from the World Bank.

Think about what a daily note does for a reader. It is cheap to read, it builds a record of judgments that can be checked later, and it shows the writer thinking in public. For a firm nobody had heard of, that was a way to earn attention without paying for it, and the same page, repeated for years, became its calling card.

Look at what is missing from this timeline. There is no single breakthrough. There is a clear starting point, a narrow area of knowledge, one thing delivered every day, and ten years of that. Each step was small enough to be taken with the resources at hand.

It is easy to read a firm's present size backwards into its beginning and imagine a grand plan. The record points the other way: a flat, a Telex machine, a subject he understood, and the patience to repeat the work until someone trusted it with a larger share of their money. The trust came from the daily output, not from the address.

[[lessons]]
$$**Start with the thing you already know and deliver it every day, however small your setting.** A narrow, repeatable contribution is something a client, a boss or a team can check, and each time it proves useful the next request becomes possible. If you are building something of your own, whether a service, a side project or a new role, choose the smallest version you can start this week and set a rhythm you can keep for years. Size will follow from the trust that regular work earns, so use the first ten years to earn it.$$

[[open]]
Behind a hospital in Knoxville, Tennessee, a razor-wire fence encloses two and a half acres of woodland. Inside, people lie in the open air, each one donated to science, while researchers walk between them with notebooks and write down what weather, insects and passing weeks do to a human body. Nothing here is hidden. Watching is the whole point.

[[story]]
Mary Roach's *Stiff* spends time at that fence. She visits a facility at the University of Tennessee in Knoxville, which she describes as the only one in the world devoted to the study of human decay. The man behind it was William Bass, who had arrived at the university in 1971 as head of the anthropology department and become the state's forensic anthropologist. Police called him whenever remains turned up that nobody could date.

One of those calls came in the late 1970s, about a grave that someone had broken open in an old family cemetery near a mansion outside Franklin. Bass examined the body in the anthropology offices under Neyland Stadium. The flesh was still pinkish and the corpse was largely intact, so he judged that the person had been dead no longer than a year. According to the university's own account, he was off by 112 years.

The body belonged to Colonel William Shy, a Confederate officer killed at the Battle of Nashville in 1864. A trained eye had misread a body that had lain in an iron coffin for more than a century. Bass could see the gap behind the mistake: no facility anywhere measured how human bodies actually change after death, so every estimate of time since death rested on rules of thumb. The case is often told as the push behind what he did next.

In the fall of 1980, Bass and his students began building a fenced research plot near the university medical center. The first body arrived in the spring of 1981, a 73-year-old man donated by his daughter. Bass collected him himself from a funeral home in Crossville and labelled him 1-81, the first subject of 1981.

Today the plot takes in more than a hundred donated bodies a year. About 60 percent are arranged by families, and the rest come from medical examiners or from people who registered while they were alive. Researchers record the stages of decomposition, which they call fresh, bloat, decay and dry, along with how fast they pass and which insects arrive when. They also set up the situations investigators really face, such as a body in a car trunk or in water. Every row in the plot is an answer to a question Bass could not answer with the pink flesh in front of him.

[[lessons]]
$$**When your estimates keep missing, start recording what actually happens.** A baker who has always guessed how much bread to make on a rainy Tuesday can keep a plain tally of the loaves left at closing for a few months. The sheet corrects her instincts faster than another year of experience would, and it shows patterns she never noticed, such as the days when her guess runs reliably high. A team that misjudges how long tasks take, or a parent who cannot tell why evenings go badly, can use the same dull record kept the same way every time, and it will beat confident memory.$$

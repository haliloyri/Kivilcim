Among Coca-Cola executives, the people who bought the drink were not called customers. They were not called consumers, or loyal fans either. The company had a plainer word for the ones it cared about most, and that word described a quantity of cans, not a person.

[[story]]
Michael Moss tells this through Jeffrey Dunn, who ran Coke's business in North and Latin America and was, in Moss's description, the top warrior of the company and a likely heir to its top job around 2000. Inside Dunn's company there was a vocabulary, and the vocabulary had a favorite phrase: "heavy users."

The phrase came with arithmetic. Coke worked on a rule that 20 percent of its customers drink 80 percent of its Coke. Moss says some of these heavy users drank as many as 1,000 cans of soda a year. Run the numbers and the pull is plain. If one fifth of the buyers account for four fifths of the volume, an average heavy user drinks sixteen times as much as an average one of everybody else.

That is why winning more of those people mattered so much more than winning more of the rest.

Coke also looked at age. Moss says the company went after teenagers, because the choices people make early, especially in their teens, turn into brand loyalties. A drinker won at fifteen was a heavy user in the making.

Dunn stood in the middle of this machinery, and Moss says he began to have a change of heart. In Moss's telling, Dunn came to feel that the kids in front of him needed a lot of things, and another Coke was not among them. According to Moss, he pushed the company toward healthier drinks and less marketing in schools, and then he left. Moss adds that Dunn now sells carrots, marketed the way junk food is marketed.

The arithmetic also sets a direction. When most of the volume sits with a fifth of the buyers, the easiest way to grow is to sell more to the same people. Nowhere is that written down as a goal. It is simply what the numbers reward, and a rule like that quietly shapes every plan built on top of it.

Look at what a word does in this account. "Customer" suggests a person with a life. "Heavy user" suggests a number of cans. The label measures people by volume, and once an entire company speaks that way, a question like "is this good for them?" has nowhere to land, because the vocabulary has no place for it.

What Dunn did was take the same children out of the same figures and ask a different question about them. The numbers did not change. The question did, and with it the answer to what the company should do next.

[[lessons]]
$$**Listen to the nouns your team uses for the people it serves, because the noun decides which question gets asked.** A company that says "heavy users" measures people in cans, while one that says "customers" can ask about their lives, and a label spreads because it is short and handy, dropping one more question each time it travels. In your own work, notice whether you describe the people you serve as volume, tickets, leads or headcount. Describe one of them in a sentence without any of those words, and see which new question appears, one you could take to your next meeting.$$

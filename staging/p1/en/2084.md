A young mathematician in Princeton works for a think tank that helps the National Security Agency break Soviet codes. His boss is a general who has just published an essay defending a war. The mathematician disagrees with him, and he decides to say so to the same newspaper.

[[story]]
Gregory Zuckerman's *The Man Who Solved the Market* is the biography of Jim Simons, the mathematician who later built the Medallion fund at Renaissance Technologies. This story is about a job he lost long before that. In 1964 Simons joined the Institute for Defense Analyses in Princeton, a nonprofit whose Communications Research Division hired mathematicians to help the National Security Agency break Soviet codes and ciphers. The terms were generous.

Simons has said he took the post partly to repay money he had borrowed. The arrangement let him spend half his time on the institute's work and half on his own mathematics. For a young researcher that was a rare bargain.

The institute's president was General Maxwell Taylor. Taylor wrote in the New York Times Magazine that the war in Vietnam was one worth winning. Simons, who opposed the war, answered with a letter to the editor of the Times, and by one published account that was in 1967. He did not hide whose side he was on.

Then a stringer from Newsweek interviewed him about his opposition. Simons laid out a plan for the interviewer. While the war lasted, he would give all of his time to his own mathematics, and once it ended he would give the institute an equal stretch of time with nothing else on his desk. He told his superiors about the interview, and they were unhappy. He was fired.

The plan was specific about the trade. The institute had been getting half of his time, and he offered it a whole share later in return for none now. That was a deal an employer could weigh, and the answer came as a dismissal.

He left in 1968 and became chairman of the mathematics department at Stony Brook University in New York. Looking back in a 2019 interview, he said he had been confident of finding a good job somewhere because of the mathematics he had already done. His summary was dry: getting fired once can be a good experience, but you don't want to make a habit of it.

A decade later, in 1978, he left academia to start the firm that became Renaissance Technologies. The code-breaking job, the letter and the dismissal all sit before that point in the story. What they show is a man who set his terms before he spoke, and who knew what his own work was worth.

[[lessons]]
$$**Build work that is worth more than your job before you take a public stand.** Simons could say what he thought of the war because the mathematics he had already published made another post easy to find. A nurse with a strong reputation on her ward, or a developer whose side projects people respect, can disagree with a manager more cheaply than someone whose only asset is the current job. Ask yourself what you could point to if your employer disappeared tomorrow, and keep adding to that list during ordinary weeks. Then, when the day comes to disagree, you will be choosing your words rather than fearing the consequences.$$

A young founder sits down with a professor at Wharton and asks him to put money into an online eyeglass company. The professor listens, weighs one detail, and says no. He feels careful and sensible. Years later he will describe that no as the worst financial decision he ever made.

[[story]]
Adam Grant teaches management at the Wharton School, and in 2009 one of the founders of a start-up called Warby Parker asked him to become an early investor. The company planned to sell glasses over the internet, a product most people expect to try on in a shop before buying. The four founders, Neil Blumenthal, Andrew Hunt, David Gilboa and Jeffrey Raider, were MBA students at Wharton at the time.

Grant said no. By his own account, the reason was that the founders were not working on the company full time. He held a belief that sounds like common sense: to be an entrepreneur you have to be a risk-taker, and you have to be all in. Anyone with a safety net, he thought, lacked real faith in the idea.

He has since called that decision the worst financial decision of his life. The company launched in February 2010 with $2,500 of seed money from a university start-up program. Its name came from two characters in a journal by Jack Kerouac. Its answer to the obvious objection, how can anyone buy glasses without trying them on, was a home try-on program: customers chose five frames online, tried them at home for free, and sent back the ones they did not want.

After the miss, Grant went looking for what he had misjudged, and he describes his conclusion in public interviews. Successful entrepreneurs, he says, are more likely than failed ones to play it safe and to keep a backup plan. In his reading, the time the founders spent on other things did not signal weak commitment. It gave them room to be original. Had they felt forced to leave everything and launch at once, they would have been under pressure to release a product right away. Instead they worked out the home try-on program, which is what people needed to make the leap and order glasses online.

Look at what his first judgment measured. It measured a visible sign, how much of their week the founders gave the company, and it treated that sign as the thing itself. The thing itself was how good the idea was and whether the people behind it could fix its weakest point. A person can be all in and still wrong, and a person can keep a job and still be right. Grant's no came from a rule he had never tested.

[[lessons]]
$$**Find an idea's weakest point and who is fixing it before you judge how much its owner has risked.** When someone pitches you, it is tempting to read their willingness to risk everything as proof of belief. A founder who keeps a safety net may simply be buying time to repair the one flaw that would sink the project. Next time you hear a proposal, ask what the biggest objection is and what the person is doing about it. That single question tells you far more about the idea and about the people behind it than counting their hours.$$

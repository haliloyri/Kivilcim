A signed letter goes to the governing body of men's tennis. Most of it describes an innocent mistake involving a can of soda. The man who signed it knows the account is false, and the fact that it works is the problem he will carry.

[[story]]
For twelve years, Andre Agassi's account of one 1997 test stayed shut. Then excerpts from his memoir *Open* ran in The Times of London in October 2009, and he told it himself: he had used crystal meth, failed a drug test, and lied to the ATP, the body that runs the men's tour, to escape the consequences.

The lie went into a letter. In it he said he had drunk, without knowing, from a soda that his assistant, whom he calls Slim, had spiked with the drug. The central falsehood, by his own telling, was the word "unwittingly": he claimed he had taken the substance by accident. He signed the letter, and he says he felt ashamed.

The ATP reviewed the case, accepted his explanation, and threw out the positive result. The account was never tested against anything, because there was nothing to test it against. A spiked drink is hard to check after the fact, and the man making the claim held an Olympic gold medal from 1996 and a Wimbledon title from 1992.

Look at what the story does when you read it as a mechanism. The lie solved one problem completely and quietly. It removed the immediate danger, and with it any pressure to say anything else. A false story that works well enough is also the easiest kind to keep, because nobody ever comes back to ask for the truth.

A signed letter is also a record. What he said stopped being a remark and became a document, and he later retracted it in the same medium, in print, under his own name.

Agassi was not saved by it in any broader sense. The same year, according to the accounts of the excerpts, was one of his worst on court, and his ranking fell to 141. Whatever the letter protected, it did not repair the rest of that season.

The ATP's spokesman declined to comment when the excerpt appeared, and the International Tennis Federation referred questions to the ATP. Nobody on that side offered to confirm or correct the details at the time. What remains on record is the one account the author himself put in print, naming the lie as a lie, in a book with his name on the cover.

[[lessons]]
$$**Correct a false statement while it is still small, because success is what makes it permanent.** A lie that is accepted removes the pressure to revisit it, so it stays on file for years with nobody asking. A shop owner who once told a supplier that a late payment was a bank error, or a student who told a teacher a file was corrupted, will find that the story needs no maintenance and also cannot be retired without a confession. Choosing a short, plain correction soon after costs an awkward minute. Waiting means carrying the same account for as long as you stay in that relationship.$$

A boy has been to four schools before he finishes sixth grade, and the one thing that follows him to each is a diagnosis: dyslexia. Reading, which most of his classmates do without a thought, costs him real effort every day. Nobody in that classroom would have guessed where the road leads.

[[story]]
Gary Cohn grew up in the Cleveland area, and the name was dyslexia. According to published profiles, he was diagnosed as a child, and by the end of sixth grade he had attended four different schools. Reading, which most classmates did without thinking, was slow and public work for him. Years later he would be president and chief operating officer of Goldman Sachs, one of the most powerful banks in the world.

Gladwell uses him in *David and Goliath* as an example of what he calls a desirable difficulty. His argument concerns what a disability can teach. A person who has failed in front of others again and again, he suggests, learns that failing is survivable. Fear of looking foolish, which stops many bright people from trying things, becomes much weaker for someone who has been through it daily since childhood.

Cohn's early career fits that reading, though the details come from published profiles rather than from one tidy source. After college, he took a job selling aluminum siding for a steel company's home products division. He did not want to sell siding. He wanted to be a trader, which is a long way from a siding sales desk. He joined Goldman Sachs in 1990, became a partner in 1994, became president and chief operating officer in 2006, and left in 2017 to work in the White House.

Be careful with the logic here. Dyslexia is not a hidden gift, and many children with it are held back by schools that fail to help them. Gladwell's point is narrower: for some people, the long experience of being the one who cannot do what others do builds a tolerance for risk, for asking for help and for looking foolish. Those habits can matter more in a trading room than neat reading ever could, because a trader who hides a mistake costs the firm more than one who reports it at once.

That is the interesting part of the story, and it is also the part most easily misread. Nobody can say how much of Cohn's career came from dyslexia and how much from ambition, luck or ability, and no one can measure that share. The same history that makes a classroom painful can leave a person unafraid in a room where others are afraid of making a mistake.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask what a long struggle has quietly taught you, and use that skill on purpose.** A weakness you have lived with for years usually leaves behind a habit, such as tolerating embarrassment, asking for help early or finding another route, that people who never struggled have not built. At work, that might mean being the first to say "I don't understand" in a meeting, or to try a role everyone expects you to fail at. Name the habit, check whether it actually helps in the situation in front of you, and then give it a specific job to do this week.$$

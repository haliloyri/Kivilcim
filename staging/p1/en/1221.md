Watch the podium at an Olympic event. The gold medalist beams. The bronze medalist often looks thrilled. And the silver medalist, second best in the entire world, frequently looks the least happy of the three. The reason says a lot about why comparison can quietly steal our gratitude.

[[story]]
In *The Gratitude Diaries*, Janice Kaplan spends a year trying to live more gratefully. Again and again, she runs into the same obstacle: comparison.

When she looked at her own life on its own terms, she found plenty to appreciate. But the moment she started comparing, the picture changed. A friend's bigger house. A colleague's more impressive job. Someone else's seemingly perfect family. Suddenly she wasn't noticing what she had. She was noticing what she didn't. The problem with this kind of comparison is that it has no finish line. There is always someone with more.

Gratitude, Kaplan found, works partly by shifting attention: away from what others have, and back to what is actually in your own life.

Psychologists have studied how powerful that choice of comparison can be. In a well-known study, researchers Victoria Medvec, Scott Madey and Thomas Gilovich analyzed footage of Olympic medalists and rated how happy they looked. The results were surprising. On average, bronze medalists appeared happier than silver medalists.

##The silver medalist had done better, but felt worse. What mattered was what each athlete compared themselves with.##

The researchers' explanation was simple. Silver medalists tended to compare themselves with the gold they had narrowly missed. Their thoughts ran toward *if only*. Bronze medalists tended to compare themselves with the athletes who finished fourth and went home with nothing. For them, the thought was closer to *at least I made it*.

Same kind of achievement, different comparison, very different feeling.

That's the heart of what Kaplan describes. Comparison isn't just a background habit. It decides how you experience your own life. Compare upward, with those who have more, and even a good life can feel lacking. Compare with where you started, with what could have been worse, or simply with what you have, and the same life can feel full.

Kaplan doesn't suggest pretending not to notice other people. That's unrealistic. But she found that gratitude, practiced deliberately, gave her a way to choose her comparisons more wisely, and to spend less time on the podium of her mind wishing she'd won gold.

[[lessons]]
$$**Comparison shapes feelings more than results do.** Silver medalists did better than bronze medalists, yet often felt worse. The difference was what they compared themselves with. Our satisfaction depends heavily on the reference point we choose, which means changing the comparison can change the feeling even when nothing else changes.$$

$$**Looking up erodes gratitude.** Kaplan found that comparing herself with people who had more instantly shifted her attention to what was missing. Because there is always someone with more, this kind of comparison never ends. Noticing when you've started it is the first step toward stopping it.$$

$$**Choose your comparison on purpose.** Gratitude gives you another option: compare with where you started, with what could have gone worse, or with nothing at all and simply notice what you have. It's not denial. It's deciding which true story about your life you'll pay attention to.$$

At a camp gate, a 39-year-old psychiatrist hands over his coat. Hidden in the lining is a draft of his first book, the work of years. The coat does not come back, and neither do the pages. He will have to find the book again somewhere else.

[[story]]
Viktor Frankl was a Viennese psychiatrist when, in 1942, about nine months after his wedding, he and his family were deported to the Theresienstadt ghetto. His father died there on 13 February 1943, aged 81, of starvation and pneumonia. His mother and his brother Walter were later killed at Auschwitz, and his wife Tilly died at Bergen-Belsen. Frankl himself spent three years in four camps.

He reached Auschwitz in October 1944, at the age of 39. By his own account, the clothes he wore were taken from him there, and in the lining of his coat was a draft of his first book, The Doctor and the Soul. Sources disagree about who had stitched the pages in and whether the coat was burned or simply thrown away. What they agree on is that the manuscript was gone.

Frankl began to rebuild it from memory, on slips of paper. Later he was moved on to Kaufering III, a subcamp of Dachau. The draft had been paper, but the thinking in it was still in his head. He later credited that decision, among other things, with helping him stay alive. A man who had lost his family and his clothes and his pages sat down to write the pages again.

When the war ended he returned to Vienna, where he headed neurology at the General Polyclinic, and wrote Man's Search for Meaning in nine days. It came out in German in 1946 under the title A Psychologist Experiences the Concentration Camp, and the English translation followed in 1959. It has since sold millions of copies in dozens of languages. The Art of Happiness draws on him in its chapter on finding meaning in pain and suffering, where Howard Cutler passes on Frankl's observation that what carried the survivors was a sense of purpose. Youth and physical strength were not what decided it.

Look at what kind of task the manuscript was. A lost draft has a next sentence, and a next sentence lies in the future. It could be worked on with a scrap of paper and a memory, it belonged to no guard, and it pointed at a finished book that someone would one day read. Rebuilding it did not depend on the day going well.

[[lessons]]
$$**Keep one piece of work alive that points beyond the hard stretch.** When circumstances take over, it helps to hold a task that is yours alone and that has a next step you can take today. A woman recovering from a long illness might keep a notebook of recipes for the restaurant she hopes to open, adding a line whenever she has the strength. A project like that cannot cure anything, but it gives the days a direction and reminds you that you are still someone with something to finish. Choose it while you are well enough to choose, and keep it small enough to continue on a bad day.$$

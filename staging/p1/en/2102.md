On a small island a visiting writer meets an older man and exchanges a few words with him. The man is unassuming, even humble, and nobody on the island treats him as someone of note. The writer has shipped on a whaler himself, and he will later put down what he thought of the man.

[[story]]
The man was George Pollard, who had been captain of the Essex when the whale sank her, and the writer was Herman Melville. What became of Pollard afterwards is covered in the Nantucket Historical Association's edition of the survivors' accounts. Pollard got home in 1821. Within a couple of months the owners of the whaleship Two Brothers gave him her command, and he sailed again for the Pacific.

In February 1823, in waters north of the Hawaiian Islands, the Two Brothers was lost. The crew took to the boats and were picked up a few days later by the whaleship Martha, and all of them got to Honolulu. On the voyage home, Pollard told the story of the Essex to two English missionaries, who wrote it down. According to their record, he said something that has survived: "And now I am utterly ruined. No owner will ever trust me with a whaler again, for all will say I am an unlucky man."

He was right. No owner did. After some months without work in Nantucket he took a job on the night watch, patrolling the town while it slept, and he never went to sea again.

##Pollard never went to sea again.##

Pollard died on 1 February 1870 at the age of eighty-one. The obituary was written by an old friend, William H. Macy, who said Pollard had been a young man when he left the sea and had lived among the islanders for more than forty years. He called him a good and worthy man, and said that was the record he left behind for those who remained.

Melville saw more. In his own copy of Owen Chase's narrative he wrote that he had seen Pollard on Nantucket and exchanged some words with him: "To the Islanders he was a nobody—to me the most impressive man, tho' wholly unassuming, even humble—that I ever encountered."

In his poem *Clarel*, Melville gave Pollard a few lines of his own. Men called him a Jonah, he wrote, and none would give him a place in a third venture, until dire need made him a night patrolman on the quay. The owners had settled on a word for Pollard. The man who wrote *Moby-Dick* looked at the same life and settled on a different one.

[[lessons]]
$$**Look at how a person carries a failure before you label them unlucky.** A manager may hear that a colleague's last two projects collapsed and quietly stop giving her anything important, when both failures were decided by factors outside her control. A label like "unlucky" is easy to hand out and hard to remove, and it tends to settle on the person rather than on the circumstances. Next time a name comes up in a staffing decision, ask what the person did in the days after things went wrong, and weigh that alongside the result, since it tells you something the result alone cannot.$$

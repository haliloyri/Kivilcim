Two young founders sit across a desk from the chief executive of a popular web portal. They offer him their search engine for a sum that, for a technology company, is almost small change. He thinks it over and shakes his head. Later a go-between persuades the founders to ask for even less.

[[story]]
Today Google is one of the best-known companies in the world. In early 1999 it was a search engine run by two founders, Larry Page and Sergey Brin, and they wanted to sell it. According to published accounts, they offered it to George Bell, the chief executive of the web portal Excite, for one million dollars. Bell rejected the offer.

The story did not stop there. Vinod Khosla, a venture capitalist connected with Excite, persuaded the founders to lower the price to seven hundred and fifty thousand dollars. Bell still declined. So one of the most valuable companies of the following decades was offered, twice, at a price that its potential buyer refused, and the refusal survived a discount of a quarter.

The facts are short, so it helps to be exact about what they allow. We know the rough sums, who was in the room, and the answer. Nobody has a record of what Bell thought, so any claim about his motives is a guess. What we can examine is the structure of the exchange, and one detail stands out: the second offer was cheaper and got the same answer.

If a buyer says no because the price is too high, a lower price should change the answer at some point. It did not. That suggests the objection was never the number. A cut of twenty-five percent would have mattered to a buyer who thought the company was worth, say, half a million dollars. It mattered not at all to a buyer who did not see the product as something his own business needed. In that case the founders were arguing about price while the real disagreement was about what the product was.

Excite was a portal, a front door to news, mail and links, and a search engine was one feature among many. A buyer who judges a new thing by where it fits in his current business sees a feature. The founders, who judged it by where search might go, saw a company. Neither view was foolish in advance. But a discount cannot move someone from the first view to the second, and lowering the price is a way of continuing a conversation that has already ended. The useful move would have been a question: what would make this worth buying at any price?

[[lessons]]
$$**When a buyer says no, find out whether the objection is price before you cut it.** A discount works only when the price is the problem, and when it is not, a lower number tells the other side you doubt your own value. In your own life this applies to a salary request, a freelance quote or a proposal to your manager: ask what would have to be true for the answer to be yes, and listen for whether they name money or something else. If the reason is not price, spend your effort on the real objection or take the idea to someone who sees the need.$$

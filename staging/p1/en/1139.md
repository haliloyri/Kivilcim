Picture a courtroom. An expert witness is about to testify, and the opposing lawyer's job is to make the jury stop believing them. It turns out there are only four ways to do it. Stephen M. R. Covey argues the same four apply to everyone you work with, including you.

[[story]]
In *The Speed of Trust*, Covey refuses to treat trust as a vague feeling. He wants to know what it's made of. His answer is what he calls the **four cores of credibility**.

He explains them with a scene most of us know from courtroom dramas. When a lawyer wants to discredit an expert witness, they have a limited set of moves. They can attack the witness's honesty: *has this person ever lied?* They can attack their motives: *are they being paid to say this?* They can attack their qualifications: *do they actually know the field?* Or they can attack their track record: *have they been wrong before?*

Those four lines of attack map onto the four cores.

**Integrity** is the first. It means your words and actions match, and that you're honest even when it costs you. **Intent** is the second: your motives and your agenda. Do people believe you genuinely care about their interests, or that you're quietly working for yourself? **Capabilities** are the third: the skills, knowledge and talent that let you actually do the job. And **results** are the fourth: your track record, whether you deliver what you said you would.

Covey groups them in two pairs. Integrity and intent are about **character**. Capabilities and results are about **competence**. Trust, he argues, needs both.

##Remove any one of the four, and the other three can't hold trust up on their own.##

You can see this in ordinary working life. There's the colleague who is honest and kind but can't do the job; people like them, but they don't hand them anything important. There's the brilliant one whose motives seem to shift with whoever is useful to them; people use their talent, but they don't trust them. And there's the capable, well-meaning person who keeps missing deadlines; each time they ask for another chance, they get a little less.

Covey also uses the image of a tree. Integrity is the roots, mostly hidden but holding everything up. Intent is the trunk. Capabilities are the branches. Results are the fruit, the part other people actually see and taste.

The real value of this breakdown is practical. "I don't trust him" is a dead end. "I trust him, but he hasn't done this kind of work before" is a problem you can actually solve.

[[lessons]]
$$**Good character isn't enough, and neither is talent.** The honest colleague who can't deliver and the gifted one with a hidden agenda both end up untrusted. Covey's split between character and competence explains why. You need integrity and intent, and you also need the capabilities and results to back them up.$$

$$**Naming the missing core makes trust fixable.** The lawyer attacking a witness always goes after something specific. You can do the same, more kindly. Asking "which core is missing?" turns a vague bad feeling into something you can talk about and work on, like experience, clarity about motives or a record of delivery.$$

$$**Results are what people see.** In Covey's tree, results are the fruit. The roots and trunk may be strong, but others judge largely by what you deliver. A good heart and real skill build trust slowly if promises keep slipping.$$

[[reflect]]
- Think of someone you don't fully trust. Which of the four cores is actually missing?
- If a skeptical colleague had to question your credibility, which core would they go after first?
- Which of your recent promises is the fruit other people will judge you by?

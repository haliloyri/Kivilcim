A high-school student in Albuquerque is giving flu to a pretend town of ten thousand people on her computer. The town has households, neighbourhoods, school classes and workplaces. When she watches how the sickness moves, the fastest carriers turn out to be the teenagers sitting shoulder to shoulder in class.

[[story]]
The teenager was Laura Glass, and she took third place at the 2006 science fair in Albuquerque, according to the Albuquerque Journal. Her father, Robert Glass, was a scientist at Sandia National Laboratories, and the Journal reports that he ran her model on the lab's computers. Michael Lewis's *The Premonition* (2021) follows the doctors and scientists who tried to prepare the United States for a pandemic, and this study belongs to that background.

The same year, in November, the journal *Emerging Infectious Diseases* published a paper called "Targeted Social Distancing Designs for Pandemic Influenza". Its authors were Robert Glass, Laura Glass, Walter Beyeler and Jason Min. Next to Laura's name the affiliation read: an Albuquerque public high school.

The model built a stylised community of 10,000 people, matched to the 2000 US Census and modelled on a small American town. People touched one another in households, neighbourhoods, school classes, work groups, gatherings of older adults and random encounters. Every scenario ran more than a hundred times, each starting with ten randomly chosen infected adults.

Children and teenagers made up 29 percent of the town and accounted for 59 percent of the contacts through which flu could pass. That gap pointed at schools. A class packs dozens of households into one room each morning, so it works like a junction, and shutting the junction stops a large share of the traffic.

For a flu as infectious as the 1957 Asian flu, closing schools and keeping children and teenagers at home cut the attack rate by more than 90 percent. Starting the day after ten symptomatic cases gave a 93 percent cut, waiting for eighty cases gave 73 percent, and even half-hearted compliance of 50 percent still gave 68 percent.

The authors also marked the limits. With a more infectious virus, or one less concentrated among the young, adults and workplaces had to be targeted too. In the most infectious scenario the best combination of measures only brought the attack rate down to somewhere between 20 and 40 percent. Each of these figures came from the simulated town, not from a real epidemic, and the authors presented them that way. The paper offered a ranking of where to cut first, not a miracle.

[[lessons]]
$$**Look for the few connections that carry most of the load before you spread effort everywhere.** An office that tries to save money in every department at once often ends up fixing nothing, while a handful of processes or relationships usually carry most of the delays and costs. Drawing the map of who and what touches what shows you where one change would matter most. That lets you spend your limited energy on that junction first, accept that the rest will wait its turn, and plan it calmly instead of in a panic. It also gives you a clear answer when someone asks why you started where you did.$$

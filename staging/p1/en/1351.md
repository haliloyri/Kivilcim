An entrepreneur and an artist, both struggling, are taken aside by an eccentric billionaire who claims he can help. His prescription is not a strategy, a contact or a loan. It is an alarm clock. Get up at five, he tells them. The odd part is not the early hour itself. It is what he wants them to do with it, down to the minute.

[[story]]
That is the setup of Robin Sharma's *The 5AM Club*, published in 2018. The book is written as a fable. The mentor's lessons to his two unlikely students carry Sharma's own argument about how to start a day.

The heart of that argument is a single hour, from 5 to 6 a.m. Sharma calls it the **Victory Hour**. His case for it is not that early rising is a virtue in itself. It is about what the world is like at that time. It is quiet. Few people need anything from you. Messages are not arriving, meetings have not started, and no one is asking for a quick favour. There are simply fewer demands and fewer distractions than at any other point in the day.

Most of us try to find time for ourselves later, in the gaps between other people's requests. Sharma's point is that those gaps rarely survive. An hour taken before anyone else is awake is different. Nobody has claimed it yet.

##The early hour works not because it is early, but because nobody else has claimed it.##

He does not leave that hour open, either. He divides it with what he calls the 20/20/20 formula.

The first 20 minutes are **Move**: intense exercise. The next 20 are **Reflect**: time for journaling, planning or simply thinking about the day ahead. The last 20 are **Grow**: learning, such as reading.

So the hour has a shape. Body first, then a quiet stretch of reflection, then something that feeds the mind. Each part has its own job, which makes the hour easier to use than a vague promise of "time for myself."

The obvious objection is that some people are just not morning people. Sharma disagrees. He argues that rising early is a habit that can be installed, not a fixed trait you are born with. He is also frank that the change is hard at first. He describes an adjustment period in which getting up early feels difficult before it gradually becomes automatic.

It is worth keeping one thing in view. Some of Sharma's explanations for why the early hour works lean on his own interpretation of brain and hormone effects. The book does not present measured results that prove them. The stronger part of his case is the simpler one: one quiet hour, before anyone else needs you, can be protected in a way the rest of the day cannot.

[[lessons]]
$$**Claim time before it is claimed.** The Victory Hour works for a practical reason: at 5 a.m. there are few demands and few distractions. Time you try to carve out later has to be won back from other people's requests. Time taken before those requests begin only has to be kept. You do not have to adopt the exact hour to use the principle.$$

$$**Give the hour a shape.** Sharma does not say "use the morning well." He splits it into three 20-minute blocks: Move, Reflect, Grow. A block with one clear purpose is easier to start and harder to waste than open-ended me-time. It also shows at a glance which part of your life the hour is feeding.$$

$$**Expect the start to be the hard part.** Sharma treats early rising as a habit you install, not a trait you either have or lack. He describes a period when it feels difficult before it becomes automatic. Knowing that difficulty is expected makes it easier to read an awkward first week as normal, rather than as proof that it is not for you.$$

In one year the world's banks lose more than half a trillion dollars, and a research firm in Connecticut ends that year ahead. Years earlier, long before anyone smelled smoke, they had built a gauge for exactly this kind of fire.

[[story]]
According to Institutional Investor, Bridgewater had built what it called a "depression gauge" about eight years before the financial crisis. It was a set of measures drawn from the firm's study of past debt crises and deleveraging, meant to show when a credit-fuelled boom was turning dangerous. The point of the gauge was that it existed before the storm. Nobody had to decide in the middle of panic what a warning sign looked like.

Ray Dalio says it was very apparent to him in late 2006 that the economy was in a bubble. In 2007, as the subprime market began to unravel, Bridgewater's newsletter, the Daily Observations, warned its readers about "crazy lending and leveraging practices". In April 2008 the firm wrote that the role of banks was about to change for the worse once again.

Then came 2008. The global banking industry lost more than 500 billion dollars that year. According to Institutional Investor, Bridgewater's flagship Pure Alpha fund, which invests across more than a hundred markets, returned 8.7 percent after fees. Dalio described the portfolio as a set of bets that did not depend on each other, leaning towards safe assets and away from risky ones. He added that the only reason Bridgewater ever loses money is being wrong.

That last remark is the key to the story. After the failure of 1982 he had learned to treat his own opinions as hypotheses. A hypothesis needs a test, and the test has to be written down while you are calm, because nobody measures honestly in the middle of a crisis. The gauge turned a vague feeling of unease into something that could be checked against numbers.

It matters, too, what the gauge did not do. It did not predict the date or the details. It told the firm which kind of world it was probably in, and the firm built its positions to survive being wrong about the rest. A warning that arrives early is only useful if the plan can absorb it. Seeing the risk early is half of the work, and the other half is making sure your plan does not depend on your being right.

[[lessons]]
$$**Build your measuring stick while things are calm, because nobody measures honestly in a panic.** Decide in advance which two or three signals would tell you that a project, a budget or a relationship is going wrong, and write them down with the number at which you will act. When those signals move, you no longer have to argue with your own hopes, since the rule was set by a calmer version of you. Share the list with someone who will hold you to it. Then check that your plan still holds if you turn out to be wrong about the rest.$$

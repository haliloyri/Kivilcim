Three teenagers flip a coin over a school science project. Whoever loses will be the experiment, and the other two will have to keep him awake. It lands, and a boy who has only just moved to town is the one who will stay up.

[[story]]
Randy Gardner was seventeen and new in San Diego. His family had moved there with the military in December, and he called himself a science nerd. He needed an idea for the science fair, and he chose to beat the record of a Honolulu radio disc jockey, who had gone about 260 hours without sleep.

With his friends Bruce McAllister and Joe Marciano he planned the attempt. The coin toss made him the subject, and the other two took on the job of measuring his reaction times and keeping him on his feet. It began on 28 December 1963, in the school winter break.

By the third day he was feeling nauseous and his memory was slipping. He described it later as something like early Alzheimer's. The mental side, he said, was what went downhill, and he grew more irritable with every day that passed.

Then the grown-ups arrived. Newspapers took an interest, and the attention, Gardner said, helped to keep him awake. William Dement, a Stanford sleep researcher, travelled down to San Diego and helped to watch him, and a Navy medic, Lieutenant Commander John Ross, joined in. Dement played basketball and pinball with the sleepless teenager to keep him moving. Gardner remembered beating him most of the time, and the account of the episode says he really did win.

On 8 January 1964, after eleven days and 264 hours, he set a new Guinness record. He admitted afterwards that on the last day he snapped at reporters and behaved like a brat.

Then came the check. At a naval hospital doctors recorded his brain waves and called him perfectly healthy. He slept about fourteen hours and woke groggy, no more than anyone would. He and his two friends took first place at the San Diego science fair. Another person broke his record later in 1964, and Guinness eventually stopped keeping the category, citing the harm it could do.

What deserves attention is how the experiment was built. Gardner's own sense of how he was doing was the least reliable instrument on the table, and the people around him did not rely on it. Someone else timed his reactions. Someone else sat across the pinball machine. The friends who started the project and the scientist who joined it all measured him from the outside, which is the only way to learn how a tired mind really performs.

[[lessons]]
$$**Check how you are doing against something outside your own head.** Someone who has slept badly all week can sit in a meeting sure they are sharp while the people around them watch the same mistakes repeat. A tired or stressed mind has little way of noticing its own slips, so the person best placed to see them is usually someone else, or a simple record. Ask a colleague or friend to tell you what they see, or check your work against something fixed, before a decision that matters. It costs you a little pride and saves you from acting on a judgement you had no way to test.$$

A surgeon removes a woman's breast, the muscles of her chest wall and the lymph nodes under her arm. For decades this is simply how breast cancer is treated, and nobody asks for proof. The answer, when it finally comes, comes from a trial and not from the operating room.

[[story]]
William Halsted worked at Johns Hopkins, and in 1894 he published a series of breast cancer operations he had performed between June 1889 and January 1894. This story is a real episode from the history of cancer, the subject of Siddhartha Mukherjee's *The Emperor of All Maladies*. It follows one operation for most of a century, and the question of whether it was ever tested.

The operation removed the breast, the pectoral muscles beneath it and the lymph nodes in the armpit. The reasoning behind it was easy to grasp: if the disease spreads outward from the breast, take out everything it might reach. Early in the twentieth century this radical mastectomy became the standard operation, and it stayed standard for decades.

Then, in 1971, Bernard Fisher's National Surgical Adjuvant Breast and Bowel Project began a trial that enrolled more than a thousand women. Women were assigned by chance to different treatments, so that the groups could be compared fairly, and the radical operation was set against less extensive surgery. The first report appeared in 1977, and further papers in the New England Journal of Medicine followed in 1985.

The finding was that more surgery made no difference to survival. Women who kept more of their bodies lived as long as women who had lost them.

The scale of the two efforts is worth setting side by side. Halsted's series covered operations spread over less than five years, and from it a practice spread through surgery for most of a century. The trial that questioned it enrolled more than a thousand women and was built to answer exactly one question.

Look at how long the operation had been protected from that question. It had a logic that sounded right, a famous surgeon behind it, and years of use that looked like experience. None of those things is a measurement. Not one. A procedure can be repeated thousands of times and still never be compared with the alternative, and repetition makes it feel more certain with every operation.

What changed things was not a better theory but a different kind of question. Fisher did not ask whether the radical operation seemed sensible. He asked what happened to women who had it compared with women who had less, and he let chance, not surgeons' preference, decide who received what.

[[lessons]]
$$**When a practice has been standard for decades, ask who ever measured it against the alternative.** In your own work this might be a weekly report nobody reads, a hiring ritual or a procedure that everyone follows only because it has always existed. Pick one such habit and give it a fair test. Decide in advance what a good result would look like, and compare doing it with doing less of it for a while. If the lighter version gives the same result, you have saved effort and spared people a cost they were bearing for nothing.$$

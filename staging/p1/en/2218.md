[[story]]
Schools were squeezed from two directions. The number of children who were overweight and unfit kept rising, and schools faced steady pressure to produce students who met academic standards. Castelli, Hillman, Buck and Erwin wanted to know how the two worries were connected.

They studied 259 public school students in third and fifth grades. Instead of lab equipment, they used field tests of physical fitness, the kind that can be run in a school gym, and set the results against each child's academic achievement. The study appeared in 2007 in the Journal of Sport and Exercise Psychology.

Two measures of the body stood out. Aerobic capacity, the ability to keep going at a steady effort, was positively associated with achievement. Body mass index went the other way: it was inversely related. The associations held up in total academic achievement, in mathematics and in reading, so the pattern did not belong to one subject. That led the authors to say that aspects of physical fitness may be globally related to academic performance in preadolescents.

That word, globally, is a strong one, and it deserves a careful reading. The study set fitness beside achievement for the children it measured. It could show that fitter children tended to score higher, but not that fitness produced the scores. A child with a calm home, regular sleep or plenty of resources might both run faster and read better. The authors' own phrasing, "may be globally related," is the language of an association.

What the study could do was change the shape of a school decision. A principal weighing PE time against study time now had evidence that, among these 259 children, the fitter ones were not the ones falling behind. The authors discussed the findings in terms of maximizing school performance and the implications for educational policy.

The same research group went on to test the causal question directly, with experiments such as Hillman's 2009 treadmill study, which measured children after rest and after a walk. The 2007 paper was the earlier step. It asked a question before anyone could answer it fully.

[[lessons]]
$$**Test a pattern with a small trial before you act on it.** The fitness and achievement link was strong enough to question a common school decision, yet a single snapshot could not say which way the arrow pointed. If you notice that your best salespeople all start their days early, you have a promising pattern, and the useful step is to ask what else those people have in common before changing everyone's schedule. Holding the pattern loosely lets you act on it as a small experiment instead of a rule. You can then keep what holds up and drop what does not.$$

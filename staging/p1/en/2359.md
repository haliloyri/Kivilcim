Health officials in Washington state go through hospital records and find more children with the same serious kidney illness than the season should produce. Doctors and officials start asking what else the children have in common, and the answer leads them to a hamburger chain.

[[story]]
In January 1993 the Washington State Department of Health investigated an unusually high number of cases of hemolytic uremic syndrome, a kidney complication, among children in the Seattle area. The cause turned out to be a bacterium, *E. coli* O157:H7, found in hamburger patties sold by the Jack in the Box restaurant chain. Eric Schlosser's *Fast Food Nation* (2001), a study of the American fast food industry, belongs to the same history of what hamburger meat can carry.

The outbreak did not stay in one state. Idaho, California and Nevada later reported cases among people who had eaten at the chain. In the end 73 restaurants were linked to it. More than 700 people fell ill in four states, 602 of them in Washington, and 171 were hospitalized. Four children died.

One of the survivors was Brianne Kiner, aged nine, who was taken to Seattle's Children's hospital days after eating a hamburger at a restaurant in Redmond. She developed the kidney syndrome, survived a coma and lived with lasting effects. The attorney Bill Marler, who represented her, obtained a $15.6 million settlement.

The question of cooking was plain. The required temperature for hamburger was 155 degrees Fahrenheit, a heat that kills this bacterium. According to Marler's account, the chain's parent company, Foodmaker, had been warned by local health departments and by its own employees that burgers were undercooked, and it decided that cooking them to 155 degrees made them too tough.

Investigators named five slaughterhouses in the United States and one in Canada as possible sources of the contaminated meat, and the exact origin was never pinned down. That detail matters. The beef may have arrived carrying the bacterium, but the final step before the customer, a few minutes on a grill at the right heat, was a rule the restaurant itself controlled.

The two sides of that decision were not equally visible. A tough patty shows up at once, on the plate and in a customer's complaint, while an undercooked one shows nothing until days later, in a hospital ward. A choice weighed on what can be seen will lean toward the grill setting that looks better.

The story has no clever turn. It is a chain of ordinary steps, from slaughterhouse to grill, and the cooking was one of them. The step that was written down as a requirement and then weighed against how the burger tasted is the step the account puts at the centre.

[[lessons]]
$$**Treat a safety limit as a floor you check every shift, because comfort and taste will keep pushing against it.** A small caterer whose staff say the chicken comes out pink faces the same choice: trust the thermometer, or trust the plate that looks fine. Complaints about quality are real and worth hearing, but they belong in a conversation about how to reach the limit, such as a different cut, a longer time or a different pan, rather than in a quiet decision to stop meeting it. Write the number where everyone can see it and make the person who wants an exception say so out loud.$$

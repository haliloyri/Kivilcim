In the first phase of Navy SEAL training, a candidate caught alone, even on the way to the dining hall, hears two words: "Get sandy." He runs into cold water, then rolls on the shore until sand covers him. The rule behind it has nothing to do with strength.

[[story]]
In *Team of Teams*, Stanley McChrystal describes BUD/S, the Navy's training for SEAL candidates, as a place where trust is built on purpose. The course lasts about six months, and in his account it tests drive and teamwork more than physical fitness. Nearly every candidate can handle the physical challenges.

According to the Navy's own numbers, as McChrystal cites them, around 90 of the 160-some students in an entering class drop out before the end, most in the first few weeks. What remains is the question of who learns to work as part of an intimate team.

On day one the candidates are split into "boat teams" of five to eight and stay with that team for the next six months. Every candidate also has a swim buddy, and nobody goes anywhere alone. In an early exercise, pairs simulate setting off an explosive miles out at sea at night. One holds a watch, the other reads direction, and they have to work together without talking.

Charles Ruiz, the officer in charge of the first phase, told McChrystal that his main job is "taking the idea of individual performance out of the lexicon on day one." The structure follows that aim. Everything is done in pairs and crews, so there is little room for a solo star.

The logic is simple. After months with the same small group and the same partner, a person starts to know what the others will do next without being told. At night, at sea, with a watch in one hand and no time to consult a rulebook, that familiarity is what the exercise runs on. It was built over months and nobody had to announce it. The same exercise run by strangers who met that afternoon would produce arguments about who holds the watch.

McChrystal concludes that the course produces "super teams, not super soldiers." The SEALs, he writes, build this unity "methodically and deliberately." For the authors, the detail marks the gap between a small team and a large organization: in a team of four, five or six, everyone knows everyone, and a big organization has to design the same bond on purpose. That question, how to make hundreds of people hang together like one boat team, runs through the whole book.

[[lessons]]
$$**When you build a new team, reward leaning on each other in the first weeks.** A leaderboard that praises the top seller on a new sales team pushes people to hide what they know from one another. Giving the work to pairs and recording the result for the pair makes asking for help ordinary. Whatever habit you reward in the early weeks is the one the team falls back on on a stressful day, so decide at the start what you want to see and say it out loud, so nobody has to guess, before the team settles into a pattern of its own.$$

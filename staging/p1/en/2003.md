On summit day, one team rule is clear to everybody: anyone who has not reached the top by two in the afternoon turns around. Two o'clock comes and goes. Climbers are still working their way up the slope, the top is close, and the rule gets harder to remember with every step.

[[story]]
Nobody disputes the basic outline. In May 1996 two commercial expeditions, one led by Rob Hall and one by Scott Fischer, were trying to reach the summit of Everest in the same days. According to published accounts, a turnaround time of 2 p.m. had been set. On 10 May it was not enforced, and many climbers stood on the summit after two.

The hour mattered for a plain reason. Even in good weather, anyone arriving at the top after 2 p.m. could not get down to Camp IV before dark, which fell at about 6:45 in the evening. The weather did not stay good. A blizzard arrived in the afternoon, and over 10 and 11 May eight people died. This account is not here to dwell on those deaths; it is here to look at one decision point.

Oliver Burkeman draws on the work of D. Christopher Kayes, a researcher of organizational behavior, in his discussion of goals in *The Antidote*. Kayes argued that stubborn attachment to a goal, in complex and fast-changing conditions, can narrow people's attention. A single, vivid target such as a summit can make it harder to look at the situation and say, "it is time to turn back." Nobody can say how far that applies to any particular climber, and Kayes was not claiming to read minds. He was pointing at a pattern.

The logic of the pattern runs like this. As you near a goal, the feeling of "almost there" starts to stand in for the actual clock. Daylight, wind and the strength left in your legs do not care how close the goal looks. A goal tells you where to go. It says nothing about how much you have left. That information has to come from a criterion fixed beforehand.

The 2 p.m. rule was exactly that kind of criterion. What was missing was someone with the weight to apply it when the hour arrived. Guy Cotter, a guide quoted in published accounts of the disaster, observed that once a client sees the summit close and is set on reaching it, turning that person around high on the mountain is very hard. A rule on paper still needs someone to own it.

You will meet the same pattern far from any mountain. Someone who has chased a product or a career plan for years often keeps going after the signs have shifted, because stopping feels like declaring the effort wasted. The remedy is to choose the stopping criterion while the goal is still shining and the fatigue is still far away, calmly, and to share it with someone outside your own head.

[[lessons]]
$$**When you start something demanding, write down in advance when you will stop, and hand that rule to someone besides yourself.** As you approach a goal, tiredness, effort already spent and rising expectation all make quitting look impossible, so the decision has to be made far away from the goal's pull. Writing "if this result has not arrived by that date, I stop" and showing it to a colleague leaves a voice in the room that will remind you when the moment comes. The goal then stops dragging you along, and stopping becomes keeping a promise you made earlier instead of a verdict you pass on yourself that day.$$

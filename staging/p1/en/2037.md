The coast of southern Ireland is in sight, grey and low, and the captain of a very large ship has to know exactly where he is. A submarine warning has been sitting in his wireless log since morning. He has a choice between turning away from the land and steering straight at it to measure it.

[[story]]
Captain William Turner commanded the Lusitania on her last crossing, the voyage Erik Larson follows in *Dead Wake*. On 7 May 1915 she was close to the end of it, nearing the Irish coast on the way to Liverpool, in waters where German submarines were known to be active. A wireless message that morning had said a submarine was active in the southern part of the Irish Channel, last heard of twenty miles south of the Coningbeg light vessel.

Turner did two things that later mattered. According to published accounts of the inquiry, he kept the ship at about 18 knots, although she could manage more. Then, with land in view, he turned toward the Old Head of Kinsale to take what sailors call a four-point bearing. It gave him an accurate fix on his position, but it meant holding a straight course for a stretch of time, not weaving. Shortly after that the torpedo struck. The inquiry placed the ship ten to fifteen miles off the Old Head, and she sank in less than twenty minutes.

Turner stayed on the bridge until the ship went down, and he survived. Within weeks he had to explain every choice in a hearing chaired by Lord Mersey, with the navigation evidence heard behind closed doors. The charge, put to him by lawyers for the Admiralty, was that he had not followed its advice about speed, course and distance from the shore.

Mersey's report is careful on this point. It accepted that Turner had not followed the advice in some respects, but said it seriously doubted that following it would have saved the ship. The advice, the report said, was "not intended to deprive him of the right to exercise his skilled judgment." It concluded that "blame ought not to be imputed to the Captain." The whole blame for the loss of life, it said, rested with those who planned the attack.

What makes this episode worth thinking about is the shape of Turner's decision. Each of his choices could be defended as sensible on the day: hold a known speed, get a reliable position, follow his own reading of the warnings. Nobody could test them in advance, and afterwards the straight line on the chart looked like the whole story. A choice made in good faith with the information at hand is not the same as a choice that turns out well. A fair review, like the one Mersey attempted, has to keep those two apart.

[[lessons]]
$$**Judge a decision by what the person knew when they made it, and say so before you look at how it ended.** After a bad outcome, every earlier choice starts to look like a mistake waiting to happen, and hindsight makes the warning signs seem louder than they were. In a post-mortem on a failed project or a missed deadline, write down first what was known on the day and what the options were, then look at the result. The Lusitania inquiry had to do exactly this, and its finding was about judgment, not outcome. This habit protects people from unfair blame and also shows you which of your own rules really need changing.$$

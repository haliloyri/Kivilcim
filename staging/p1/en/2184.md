[[open]]
A shed behind a Paris school was once used for dissecting bodies. Its roof does not keep out the rain, the air inside is stale, and a woman stands over a row of dishes of dissolved ore. She tips one solution, lets it crystallise, and begins again.

[[story]]
In 1898 Marie Curie and her husband Pierre began work on pitchblende, a heavy black ore that gave off more radiation than its uranium alone could explain. The first work on it started on 14 April 1898. By 26 December of that year they had announced a new element, which they named radium.

Announcing an element and holding it in your hand are different things. To prove radium was real, they had to separate it, and radium turns up in ore in tiny amounts. Their workplace was a converted shed beside the school, a former dissecting room of the medical school, which neither of them could simply swap for something better. By one description it was poorly ventilated and not even waterproof.

The method was fractionation, repeated again and again. Radium salts behave much like barium salts but are slightly different in how they dissolve, so each round of dissolving and crystallising enriched the radium a little. The quantities of ore were measured in tonnes, though accounts disagree on how many. Nobody could speed it up, because the chemistry set the pace. Skipping a round would have meant a weaker result, so patience was part of the method. One round gave a small gain, and the next gave another.

It took years. A history of science magazine puts it as four years of seemingly endless fractionations. In 1902, Marie separated about a tenth of a gram of radium chloride. That is a pinch of salt from an ore pile.

The recognition came in stages. In 1903 the Nobel Prize in Physics was shared by Marie, Pierre and Henri Becquerel for their research on radiation. In 1911 Marie received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the discovery of radium and polonium, and for isolating radium and studying its compounds.

Look at what the four years consisted of, because the shape of the work matters more than its setting. There was no single brilliant afternoon. There was one repeatable step, done thousands of times in poor conditions, and a clear measure of progress at the end of each round: how much more radium was in the dish. The obvious obstacle, the shed, never changed. The calendar showed no dramatic days, only days that looked like the one before. The work simply got finished anyway.

[[lessons]]
$$**When a big result depends on slow purification, find the one repeatable step and count your rounds, not your hours.** The Curies could not hurry crystallisation, but they could always say whether this round had left more radium than the last. You can borrow that kind of measure for a task that feels endless, such as cleaning a dataset, editing a long manuscript or learning a language. Define one small cycle, run it daily and note what each pass removes or improves. A task that is measured in rounds shows its progress long before its end, which makes the wait easier to bear.$$

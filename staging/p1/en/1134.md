How many decisions have you made today? What to eat, what to wear, which message to answer first, how close to stand to the person you talked to. Probably hundreds. Now the harder question: how many of those did you actually think through? David Brooks suspects the honest answer is humbling.

[[story]]
*The Social Animal* is an unusual kind of book. Instead of lecturing, David Brooks invents two characters, Harold and Erica, and follows them from birth into adulthood. Through their childhoods, their schooling, their careers and their marriage, he weaves in research from psychology and neuroscience. The story is fiction. The science is the point.

And the science keeps returning to the same idea. Most of what happens in our minds happens below the surface.

We like to think of ourselves as rational decision makers. We gather information, weigh options and choose. Brooks argues that this picture gets the order wrong. The unconscious mind does most of the work of noticing, judging and deciding. It spots patterns, reads other people's faces and moods, and produces feelings of attraction, unease or certainty long before we can explain them.

Conscious reason, in his account, is slower and more limited. It can only handle a few things at a time. But it is loud. It talks constantly, and it tends to believe it's in charge.

##Often, reason isn't the one deciding. It's the one explaining what was already decided.##

What makes Brooks's view distinctive is that he doesn't see this as a tragedy. Older ideas about the unconscious, especially Freud's, painted it as a dark basement full of primitive drives. Brooks describes something very different: an unconscious that is intelligent, emotional and deeply social. It's where much of our wisdom actually lives. A skilled professional's gut feeling, a parent's sense that something is wrong with a child, the quick read you get of a stranger: these are the unconscious at work, and often it's right.

But there's a consequence. If much of your decision-making happens where you can't see it, then the things that shape that hidden mind matter enormously. For Harold and Erica, it's their families, their friendships, the places they grew up and the habits they formed. Those influences don't just affect what they think. They shape what they automatically *want*, what feels natural and what feels wrong.

The same is true for all of us. Our surroundings, our relationships and our routines train the unconscious. The unconscious then makes most of our choices. And conscious reason arrives afterwards to tell a tidy story about why.

[[lessons]]
$$**Reason often explains more than it decides.** Brooks's picture of the mind is humbling. Many choices form beneath awareness, and conscious reason then supplies the reasons. That doesn't make thinking useless, but it's worth being suspicious when your explanation for a choice arrives suspiciously neatly after the choice itself.$$

$$**The unconscious is smart, not just primitive.** Brooks rejects the idea of a dark basement full of urges. The quick reads and gut feelings that guide Harold and Erica are often wise, built from thousands of past experiences. The goal isn't to silence that part of the mind. It's to understand it and train it well.$$

$$**Change what feeds the hidden mind.** If family, friends, places and habits shape Harold's and Erica's automatic choices, the same is true for you. You can't directly control most of what your unconscious decides. You can control a lot of what it's exposed to every day.$$

[[reflect]]
- Pick one decision you made recently. What reason did you give for it, and what might have really driven it?
- Which people and places most shape your automatic choices, for better or worse?
- When did a gut feeling turn out to be wiser than your careful reasoning?

&&If you can't directly choose most of your choices, what can you choose about your surroundings?&&

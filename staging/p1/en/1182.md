A man works for a delivery company his whole career. He never earns a big salary. He never runs a company, invents anything or wins the lottery. By the time he's ninety, he's worth around $70 million. Tony Robbins tells his story because the secret is something almost anyone could copy.

[[story]]
In *Money: Master the Game*, Tony Robbins introduces Theodore Johnson, an early employee of UPS.

Johnson was not a highly paid executive. According to Robbins, he never made more than about $14,000 a year. That was a modest income even in his time. Nothing about his paycheck suggested he'd ever be wealthy.

But Johnson had one rule, and he never broke it. From every paycheck he received, and from every bonus, he set aside 20 percent. Not when it was convenient. Not when there was something left over at the end of the month. Every time, first.

He put much of that money into UPS stock, which the company offered to employees.

##By the time he was ninety years old, his savings had grown to around $70 million.##

He then gave a large part of it away, including major gifts to support education.

Robbins uses the story to illustrate the force at the center of his book: **compounding**. When you invest money, it earns a return. Then that return starts earning returns of its own. In the early years, the growth looks unimpressive. A few percent here, a few percent there. But over decades, the gains start to feed on themselves, and the curve bends sharply upward. Most of Johnson's fortune was built in the later years, from growth on top of growth on top of his original savings.

The lesson Robbins draws is clear. It's not how much you earn that decides whether you become wealthy. It's how much of what you earn you consistently keep and invest, and how long you let it grow. Johnson's income was ordinary. His discipline and his time horizon were extraordinary.

Robbins is also candid about the role of luck. Johnson's result depended heavily on UPS stock performing extremely well over many decades, and he concentrated much of his savings in one company. Not everyone will pick an investment that grows like that, and putting everything into one company carries real risk. But even with more ordinary returns, the same combination of a fixed savings rate and a long time horizon can turn a modest income into real security.

The most copyable part of the story isn't the stock. It's the rule: a fixed percentage, taken first, every time.

[[lessons]]
$$**What you keep matters more than what you earn.** Johnson never had a big salary. What he had was a fixed rule: 20 percent of every paycheck and bonus. Many people with far higher incomes save nothing. Robbins's point is that wealth is built from the gap between earning and spending, kept consistently over time.$$

$$**Compounding is slow, then sudden.** Returns earning returns barely show at first. Over decades, the growth accelerates dramatically. That's why starting early and staying invested matter so much. Most of the $70 million came late, from growth that could only happen because the money had time.$$

$$**A rigid rule beats good intentions.** "Every paycheck, every bonus" removed the monthly decision. Johnson didn't have to feel disciplined each time; the rule decided for him. Automatic, non-negotiable saving works far better than planning to save whatever is left over.$$

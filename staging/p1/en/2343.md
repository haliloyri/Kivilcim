[[open]]
A geobiologist who spends her days measuring leaves and seeds sat down to write about something no instrument in her lab could measure. She had kept it out of print for years. In a memoir about her life in science, she decided it belonged on the page next to the plants.

[[story]]
Hope Jahren's *Lab Girl* is the first place she wrote about her episodes of mania and her bipolar disorder, as she told *Time* when the book came out. A reader's guide to the memoir points to an interlude of a few pages in which she describes her struggles with mental illness. She did not hide it in a footnote or a thank-you page.

The way she talks about the illness is plain. In the *Time* interview she describes it as a physiological illness, one that has treatments and medication. Then she adds the other half of the sentence: the experiences were still very real to her. She holds both statements at once, and neither cancels the other. A condition can have a body-chemistry explanation and a treatment, and still be lived from the inside as real events.

A published review of the book in *Science News* adds one more detail from her life, that she had to go off her medications during pregnancy. That is a hard decision, and it is easy to imagine how a writer might have skipped it. The review does not call it a triumph or a tragedy. It simply sits among the facts of her working life, next to the grant deadlines and the lab moves.

Her choice of voice matters here too. In a Science Friday interview she said she deliberately avoided scientific terminology in the memoir, and that she accepted the loss of precision as a trade for reaching readers outside science. The same instinct runs through how she writes about illness. She does not wrap it in clinical language, and she does not turn it into a scene meant to shock.

Consider what the two halves do for a listener. "Physiological, with treatments" tells them this is a medical matter with a known path, so nobody needs to hunt for someone to blame. "Still very real to her" tells them not to wave away what she went through as a mere symptom. Together the two statements ask a reader for neither pity nor distance.

What the account offers a reader is a template for speaking about something difficult without apology. She says what it is, says that it can be treated, and says that it was real. Those three plain claims are enough to let a listener understand her without having to rescue her.

[[lessons]]
$$**Describe a hard thing with the plain facts that are true of it.** When something painful is on your mind, it is tempting to pad it with apologies or to shrink it into a joke, and both leave the listener unsure what to do. A person telling a team about a long-term health condition can say what it is, what helps, and what a bad day looks like, and then go back to the task. Plain statements give other people something concrete to respond to, and they spare you from managing their reaction on top of everything else.$$

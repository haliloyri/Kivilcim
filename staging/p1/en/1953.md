[[open]]
Two triangles and a circle slide across a screen, in and out of an open rectangle. There are no faces, no voices, no words, only shapes changing position. Viewers sit through the whole film once, and then they are asked to put down in writing what they saw.

[[story]]
At Smith College in the 1940s, the psychologists Fritz Heider and Marianne Simmel made a short silent film of exactly that: two triangles and a circle moving around a rectangle. They then ran a simple test on what people would see. The result is a neat laboratory version of a point made in *Difficult Conversations* by Douglas Stone, Bruce Patton and Sheila Heen, that we read other people's intentions straight off what we can observe.

The instruction was as plain as the film. Viewers were told to "write down what happened in the picture." Nothing in the instruction mentioned people, motives or a story.

According to the Open University's account of the paper, 33 of the 34 participants described the shapes "in terms of animated beings." Nineteen of them went further and wrote a connected story. One account, which the authors treated as a common interpretation, ran like this: a fight between two men, a girl who does not want to be with the first man, and a chase around the room.

Look at what was actually on the screen. A shape changed position, sped up, slowed down, went into the rectangle or came out of it. That was all the viewers could measure. The men, the girl, the fight and the reluctance came from the people holding the pencils. They took what moved and what stayed still, and filled in who wanted what. The authors did not examine how it felt to watch; what they recorded is what ended up on the page, and it was a cast of characters.

What makes the result useful is how little effort it took. Nobody asked the viewers to speculate, and nobody had told them the shapes were alive. The inference arrived so fast, and in such complete form, that for nearly everyone it passed as plain observation of what the film contained.

The same thing happens at the office and at home. A reply arrives an hour late, a door closes a little hard, a friend's face goes still, and we read a motive into it. The movement is real, and so is our reaction to it. The motive is the part we supplied, and it is the part most likely to start the fight.

[[lessons]]
$$**Separate what you saw from the motive you supplied before you respond.** A coworker who stays silent after your suggestion may be thinking, tired, or annoyed, and the silence alone does not tell you which. When you say "you ignored my idea," you are speaking from your story about the silence and not from the silence itself. Say the movement first, "you did not answer when I raised the plan," and ask what was happening on the other side. You will learn more than the story would have told you, and the other person will not have to fight a motive they never held.$$

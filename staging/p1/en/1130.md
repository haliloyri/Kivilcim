A ten-year-old boy is handed a puzzle he can't solve. He pulls his chair closer, rubs his hands together and shouts that he loves a challenge. The adult watching him is the one who's confused. She had come to study failure, and he doesn't seem to have noticed he's failing.

[[story]]
Carol Dweck tells this story near the start of *Mindset*, and she tells it against herself.

As a young researcher, she was obsessed with how people cope with failure. So she set up a simple study. She brought children in one at a time and gave them a series of puzzles. The first ones were manageable. Then they got harder, and harder, until the children could no longer solve them. Her plan was to watch what happened at that point: who would get discouraged, who would keep going, who would fall apart.

She expected to see coping. She thought she already knew the options.

Then a ten-year-old boy reached the hard puzzles. He pulled up his chair, rubbed his hands together, smacked his lips and cried out:

> I love a challenge!

Another child, sweating over a puzzle he couldn't crack, looked up at her with a pleased expression and said:

> You know, I was hoping this would be informative!

Dweck admits her first reaction was to wonder what was wrong with them. In her mind, failure was something you endured. You got through it. Nobody *enjoyed* it. And yet here were children who weren't enduring anything.

##They didn't think they were failing. They thought they were learning.##

That was the moment she realized she had it backwards. She wasn't going to teach these children how to cope with failure. They were going to teach her something about it. She had assumed that a hard problem carries only one message: *you aren't good enough*. These children were reading a completely different message: *here is something you don't know yet*.

That difference became the core of her life's work. In what she later called a fixed mindset, a hard puzzle is dangerous because it might reveal that you're not as smart as you want to be. So you protect yourself, avoid the hard ones, and feel sick when you get stuck. In a growth mindset, the same puzzle is simply where the learning happens. Getting stuck isn't a verdict. It's the interesting part.

The puzzles didn't change from one child to the next. The difficulty was identical. What changed was what each child believed the difficulty was telling them.

[[lessons]]
$$**The problem is the same; the meaning isn't.** Every child got the same impossible puzzles. Some shrank from them and some leaned in. The difference wasn't in the task. It was in whether getting stuck seemed to say something about who they were. When a problem feels threatening, it's worth asking what exactly you think it proves.$$

$$**Call it information and failure loses its sting.** "I was hoping this would be informative" is a remarkable thing to say while you're losing. That child had turned a setback into data. He could stay with the puzzle longer precisely because not solving it didn't cost him anything personal.$$

$$**Let surprising people correct you.** Dweck came in as the expert on failure and left as the student of two children. Her willingness to wonder whether she was the one with the wrong idea is what turned an odd moment in a lab into a theory that changed her field.$$

[[reflect]]
- Recall the last task that turned out harder than you expected. What did the difficulty seem to say about you?
- Where do you avoid hard problems mainly so you can keep looking capable?
- Who in your life reacts to setbacks the way those children did, and what could you learn from watching them?

A lone man walks the better part of twenty miles along a Texas road after dark, heading for an army fort. Behind him, on the prairie, lie wagons that were carrying corn that same morning. Earlier that day a general had ridden down this very road and nobody had touched him.

[[story]]
Thomas Brazeale's walk is one thread in the violent years S. C. Gwynne describes in *Empire of the Summer Moon*. On May 18, 1871, General William Tecumseh Sherman's inspection party passed the Salt Creek Prairie stretch of road without harm. According to published accounts, later that same day a convoy of ten mule-drawn wagons, hauling army corn and fodder for the government contractor Henry Warren, was ambushed in the same area.

The attackers were a Kiowa war party, and their leaders are named as Satanta, Satank, Mamanti and Big Tree. Seven teamsters died and five escaped, and Brazeale reached Fort Richardson on foot, some twenty miles away. The Kiowa are reported to have lost three warriors and to have taken about forty mules.

By the time Brazeale and the other survivors reached the fort after dark, Sherman was there. He ordered the chiefs arrested at Fort Sill. Satanta, Satank and Big Tree were taken. Satank was killed trying to escape on the way to Fort Richardson. Satanta and Big Tree were convicted of murder on July 5 and 6 in Jack County, and, per the accounts, both were paroled two years later and returned to their people.

The published accounts count the day's losses on both sides. The Texan record centres on the seven dead teamsters and the trial that followed, which some accounts rank among the first in which Native leaders faced a US court. The same accounts note the three Kiowa warriors killed and the mules taken. Both versions share the road and the day, though they weigh the outcome differently.

What stands out is how differently the same stretch of road treated two groups on the same day. Sherman's party rode through unharmed. The convoy did not. His safe passage showed only that this party, at this hour, got through.

One crossing like that says almost nothing about safety. Had we not known about Sherman's ride, we would call the road dangerous; once we know, it is tempting to call it safe. Both are mistakes. The real information came from the seven dead and from one man's twenty-mile walk, and when the news reached the fort after dark, it produced Sherman's order to arrest.

[[lessons]]
$$**Treat a narrow escape as luck until something shows it was safety.** A project finishes without harm, so the process is called sound. A system runs for a year without crashing, so the backups get skipped. A breach goes unnoticed, so security is judged adequate. Yet luck alone may explain the result, and luck is not a plan. Write down what you got through, and ask what worked this time and what happens when it fails next time, ideally before you cross the same road a second time. That is the moment the question is cheapest, because nobody has been hurt yet and nobody has to defend a past decision.$$

More than five thousand volunteers, a program of calorie targets and exercise, and years of check-ups. One group lost far more weight than the other, and its blood sugar readings improved as well. Then the researchers counted heart attacks, strokes and deaths, and the two groups came out almost level.

[[story]]
In 2001 a US trial called Look AHEAD began recruiting adults who were overweight or obese and had type 2 diabetes. By 2004 it had enrolled 5,145 of them at 16 centers. The question was direct: if people with diabetes lose weight and become more active, do they have fewer heart attacks and strokes?

The volunteers were assigned by chance to one of two groups. One group joined an intensive lifestyle program built around eating less and moving more. The other, the control group, received diabetes support and education, but no weight-loss program.

On the scale, the program worked. After one year the intervention group had lost 8.6% of its body weight and the control group 0.7%. Weight crept back over the years, as it usually does, yet the gap survived to the end of the study: 6.0% against 3.5%. The program also improved blood sugar control and some cardiovascular risk factors.

The main outcome was a composite of four events: death from cardiovascular causes, a nonfatal heart attack, a nonfatal stroke, or a hospital stay for angina. Angina was added after the first two years, because too few events had piled up in the control group. People were followed for a median of 9.6 years, out of a planned maximum of 13.5. In 2012 a futility analysis suggested that carrying on was unlikely to reveal a difference, and the trial was stopped early.

The count came to 403 events in the intervention group and 418 in the control group, or 1.83 and 1.92 per 100 person-years. The hazard ratio was 0.95, with a confidence interval from 0.83 to 1.09 and a p-value of 0.51. A gap that small sits comfortably inside what chance alone can produce.

Reading this result well means knowing its edges. The trial tested one particular program, in people who already had diabetes, with weight that partly came back over the years. It cannot say what a different approach would have done. What it does show is narrower and still striking: improvements in weight and in blood tests did not turn, on their own, into the hard outcome everyone hoped for.

That gap between a good number and a good result is the reason researchers insist on long trials. A measurement can be a sign of health without being the thing that protects it.

[[lessons]]
$$**A number you can improve may not be connected to the result you actually want.** Weight, a test score, a response time or a sales figure are stand-ins, and they can move while the outcome behind them stays put. In your own work, ask which hard result each metric is supposed to predict, and check whether anyone has ever seen the two move together. Do it early, because a metric that is easy to improve invites months of effort. Then you will know whether you are polishing a dial that is wired to something, or one that is wired to nothing at all.$$

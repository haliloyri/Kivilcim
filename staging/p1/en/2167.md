[[open]]
At a blackjack table, the first stretch of play is for tiny bets, a dollar here, ten there. The player is not trying to win yet. He is watching whether the dealing looks honest and whether the hands arrive the way his formula says they should, and he carries only a tenth of the money he was offered.

[[story]]
When Edward O. Thorp announced his blackjack results to the mathematical community, they were surprised, but most understood and approved. The casinos were mixed. Several said they loved system players and would send a cab to the airport for him. In January 1961 the Washington Post ran a mocking editorial about a mathematician coming to town who claimed he could beat blackjack, comparing him to a "surefire weed killer" advertised for $1, whose buyers got back a postcard reading: "Grab by the roots and pull like hell."

For Thorp, the test was not over on paper. As he later wrote, the last step was to verify the method in the real world. In 1961 two wealthy businessmen and gamblers, chosen from the many who sought him out, bankrolled a trip to Nevada. They proposed a bankroll of $100,000. Thorp cut it to $10,000 as a personal safety measure.

Then he climbed in stages. For the first eight hours he kept his bets between $1 and $10, to check that all was as expected (possible cheating?) and to get used to handling that amount of money. After that he played two hours at $2 to $20, two hours at $5 to $50 and three hours at $25 to $200. Only then did he move to full scale, $50 to $500, and stayed there for 20 hours.

Inside each range he sized his bets with a fraction of the Kelly system: a percentage of his bankroll proportional to his expectation in favorable situations, up to the top of the current range. At full scale he bet full Kelly, which generally came to a percentage of his bankroll a little below his percentage expectation.

In those 20 hours of full-scale betting he won $11,000. The midpoint of his own forecast had been a win of $10,000.

Thorp himself names the lasting idea in this ladder: limit your bet size to a level at which you are comfortable and at which you could tolerate a really bad outcome. The formula might have been right, but that did not rule out cheating, bad luck or a mistake of his own in the first week.

[[lessons]]
$$**Raise your stakes in steps, and cap them where a bad outcome is still bearable.** Someone starting a small business can test a new supplier with one modest order before committing the whole season's budget, learning about delivery times, quality and their own arithmetic while little is at risk. Each step collects evidence that the assumption holds before you move to the next. If your calculation turns out wrong, the price you pay is only the size of one step, rather than the size of everything you were willing to put in on day one.$$

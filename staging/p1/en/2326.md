[[open]]
In the prisons of a Siberian region, about one in four of the inmates who are on treatment for tuberculosis is dying. They are not untreated; they are on therapy, and it is not enough. Someone has to look at that number and decide that it is the problem.

[[story]]
Tracy Kidder's *Mountains Beyond Mountains* follows Paul Farmer from Haiti and Peru to Russia, where tuberculosis was killing people in places the world rarely looked. This story is about the prisons of Tomsk Oblast, in Siberia. According to published accounts, Partners In Health began work there in 1998 at the invitation of the Russian Ministry of Justice and the Open Society Institute, which ran a program against TB in the region's prisons.

The 1990s had been brutal for those prisons. A published review reports that TB incidence in the Tomsk prison sector climbed to a high of more than 7,000 cases per 100,000 people. Many patients were resistant to several drugs, including the second-line ones: Tomsk's own TB services reported that 49 percent were resistant to kanamycin, 10 percent to capreomycin and 54 percent to ethionamide.

Farmer later gave a stark figure. In Tomsk's prisons in 1998, he has said, 24 percent of the people on therapy for TB and MDR-TB were dying. It is a case-fatality rate, the share of those under treatment who did not survive, and it measures the one thing a patient cares about.

PIH began treating MDR-TB in the Tomsk prisons around 2000, funded by the Open Society Institute and Tom White, the Boston benefactor who had paid for the organization's first MDR-TB program in Haiti. In an interview with the Harvard Gazette, Farmer described the death rate among those on treatment falling from 24 percent to zero within two years, once the right drugs reached the right place.

The work later widened. In 2004 Tomsk received support from the Global Fund, which made it possible to treat all of the region's patients with MDR-TB, and that same year MDR-TB as a share of all TB cases in Tomsk began to decline.

The detail to hold on to is the choice of measure. A program could have counted pills delivered or patients enrolled and reported success. The number that mattered was how many of the people on treatment died, and once that figure was in front of the team, any step that did not move it could be questioned. Note also that the figures here come from Farmer's own recollection and an interview, so they show the direction and scale rather than a precise audit.

[[lessons]]
$$**Pick the one outcome that hurts most and let it decide what you change.** Activity numbers such as meetings held, tickets closed or doses delivered can all look healthy while the thing you care about gets worse. In Tomsk the measure was the share of treated patients who died, and it was hard to argue with. In your own work, find the figure that a customer, a patient or a family member would choose, put it where everyone can see it, and review every habit against it. The point is not to collect more data but to stop being comforted by the wrong data.$$

A Belgian village about four kilometres north of Bastogne has to be taken, and the attack has barely begun when it stops. The company commander is not moving forward, and the officer above him has to decide what to do about it. Whatever he chooses will be argued over for decades.

[[story]]
Stephen E. Ambrose's 1992 book *Band of Brothers* follows Easy Company of the 506th Parachute Infantry Regiment. In January 1945 the company was in the Ardennes, around Bastogne, and its next task was the village of Foy, roughly four kilometres north of the town. On 13 January, or the 14th according to one source, Easy jumped off from the woods known as Bois Jacques and moved toward the village.

The attack stalled. The company commander, Lieutenant Norman Dike, was not getting the unit forward. An attack that stops is only a group of men standing where the enemy can see them, and the village could fall only if someone started the movement again.

Dick Winters had Dike relieved and put Ronald Speirs in charge of the company. A link in the chain of command was swapped in the middle of an assault, without a long discussion. Speirs took over, and the company went on toward the village. By about half past three in the afternoon, Foy was secured.

Easy took casualties in the fight, but sources vary on the number, so no figure appears here. How Dike performed that day is also disputed. Some accounts judge him harshly and others more gently, and the sources do not settle the argument.

A small note sits at the edge of the story: even the date is not single. Most sources say 13 January and one says the 14th. A day like that was too scattered to fit one clean account, and the records reflect it. What is certain is that the village was taken and the company's command changed hands.

So the firmest thing the episode offers is the order of events. An argument about Dike could run for years, but the attack had to resume that same day. The question in front of Winters was narrower than anyone's character: whether this attack could move under this commander. Changing the commander did not amount to a final verdict on him. It said only that if the village was to be taken, someone had to fill that role at once.

The decision was neither a full accusation nor a full acquittal. They filled the role, took the village and left the judgment of the man for later. That later judgment is still going on.

[[lessons]]
$$**Secure the task first and judge the person afterwards.** When work stalls, arguing over who is at fault usually delays it further. If a restaurant kitchen jams at the height of the evening rush, someone has to be put on that station so service can continue, and the conversation about responsibility belongs after the last table has been served. Doing it in that order keeps the work moving and keeps the person from being buried under a verdict reached in a hurry. Being able to do the necessary thing now and the fair thing later protects both.$$

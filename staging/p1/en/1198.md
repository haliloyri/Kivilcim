Many people who have been through trauma can tell their story in detail. Some have told it many times, in many therapy rooms. And still, something doesn't shift. Bessel van der Kolk spent years asking why, and his answer pointed somewhere most therapy had overlooked: the body itself.

[[story]]
In *The Body Keeps the Score*, psychiatrist Bessel van der Kolk argues that trauma is not only a memory stored in the mind. It shapes how people experience their own bodies.

Many survivors, he writes, don't feel safe inside themselves. Their bodies may be tense and on alert all the time, braced for danger. Or they may feel numb and disconnected, as though they've learned to stop noticing what's happening inside. Both can be ways the body tried to cope with something overwhelming.

Van der Kolk uses the term **interoception** for our ability to sense what's going on inside our bodies: a racing heart, a tight stomach, tiredness, hunger, calm. He argues that trauma often disrupts this sense. People lose touch with their inner signals, or find those signals so frightening that they avoid them.

This, he suggests, helps explain why talking alone often isn't enough. Putting experiences into words is valuable. But if the body is still stuck in its old state of alarm, understanding the story may not be enough to make a person feel safe.

##Recovery, in his view, means learning that it's safe to feel what's happening inside, a little at a time.##

That led van der Kolk and his colleagues to explore approaches that work through the body. He describes breathing practices, movement, theater, and other methods that help people notice sensations, tolerate them and feel more in control. One approach his team studied closely was yoga.

They ran a study of a trauma-sensitive yoga program for women with chronic post-traumatic stress, many of whom had not improved with other treatments. Compared with a control group, the women who did yoga showed meaningful reductions in their symptoms. The practice was gentle and focused on choice: noticing sensations, moving at one's own pace, and learning that the body could be a place of safety rather than threat.

Van der Kolk doesn't present any one method as a cure. People differ, and many need several kinds of help, often including talking therapy and sometimes medication. His broader point is that healing trauma usually needs to include the body. Feeling calm, grounded and in control inside your own skin is not a side effect of recovery. It can be one of its foundations.

[[lessons]]
$$**Words help, but often aren't enough.** Many survivors can tell their story clearly and still feel no safer. Van der Kolk argues that because trauma lives in the body, healing often needs to reach it too. That doesn't make talking therapy less valuable. It means it may be one part of a larger process.$$

$$**Reconnecting with the body builds safety.** Interoception, sensing what's happening inside, is often disrupted by trauma. Gently relearning to notice breath, tension and calm, at a pace that feels manageable, can help the body stop treating every sensation as a threat.$$

$$**Gentle body practices can support healing.** The trauma-sensitive yoga study showed real improvement for women who hadn't been helped by other treatments. Van der Kolk presents body-based practices not as a replacement for professional care, but as a meaningful addition, especially where words alone have stalled.$$

[[reflect]]
- How connected do you feel, right now, to what's happening in your body?
- Which activities help you feel settled and safe in your body: walking, breathing, stretching, something else?
- When you're stressed, do you tend to feel tense and alert, or numb and far away?

&&What small, gentle practice could help you feel more at home in your own body?&&

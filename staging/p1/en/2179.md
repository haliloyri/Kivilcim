A car company reads out its quarter: the goal was 1,500 vehicles, and the number built is 260. On the same company's line, robots are causing trouble and one of the zones that makes battery modules is behind plan. The founder has to push his promise for cars per week to a later date.

[[story]]
Walter Isaacson's *Elon Musk* is a biography of the founder of Tesla and SpaceX. This story sits in 2017 and 2018, when Tesla tried to ramp up Model 3 production, a period that published accounts call "production hell." In the third quarter of 2017 Tesla's goal was 1,500 Model 3s, and only 260 were built. The company blamed production bottlenecks and said there were no fundamental problems.

In November 2017 Musk explained the delay. He pointed first at the new battery module, saying the team had to rewrite all of its software from scratch, according to Fortune, and he blamed a systems integration subcontractor for trouble in one of four battery production zones. He then pushed the target of 5,000 cars a week from December 2017 to March 2018. An analyst said Musk needed to stop over-promising and under-delivering. Bloomberg reported on 21 November 2017 that Tesla had been burning about 8,000 dollars a minute over the previous twelve months while it prepared the car. In the fourth quarter 2,425 cars were built and 1,542 Model 3s delivered, about 2,900 fewer than Wall Street had estimated.

By April 2018 the picture was shifting. Before a planned mid-April shutdown, Tesla had built more than 2,000 Model 3s a week for three weeks in a row. The company raised its target: 6,000 cars a week by the end of June.

On 1 July 2018 Musk announced that Tesla had reached 5,000 cars in a week. That was below the 6,000 target, but the original 5,000 had been passed about seven months after the date first promised. Model 3 deliveries rose from 1,764 in 2017 to 145,846 in 2018.

What stands out is the distance between promised dates and measured weekly output. The December 2017 target was still standing after a quarter of 260 cars, and it had to be moved. The April 2018 target came after three straight weeks above 2,000. One grew out of hope, the other out of a measured streak. A weekly number shows both a mistake and progress at the same speed, and a figure made public can be argued with, and a figure that can be argued with can be corrected.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you name a date, measure your real recent pace and base the promise on that.** Customers and managers trust an answer to "when will it be done" more when it rests on last week's count than on hope. Every postponement wears down faith in the next promise a little, and winning that faith back takes longer than making the first one. On the Model 3 line, the December 2017 target came before the pace and was postponed, while the April 2018 target followed three measured weeks. See the gap between pace and calendar in the same number each week and derive the date from it.$$

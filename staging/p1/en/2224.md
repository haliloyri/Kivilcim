A letter from a young heir to his old teacher begins with court gossip and a judge who turns up his nose. Halfway through it changes direction. The writer admits he has not done the assignment, and he explains why in a few lines that sound more like a patient describing symptoms.

[[story]]
Marcus Aurelius was twenty-five, already named as the future emperor, and he had not written the exercise his teacher had set him. Cornelius Fronto, the famous orator who had tutored him since he was a boy, was about to come to town. So Marcus sat down and confessed instead. The letter survives in Charles Haines's English translation of Fronto's correspondence.

He starts with a story about a man called Aufidius who gave himself airs as a judge and turned up his nose when Marcus smiled. Then he gets to the point. The books of Ariston, a Stoic philosopher, treat him well and make him feel ill at the same time. When they teach him a better way, he is grateful. When they show him how far his own character falls short of it, his teacher's pupil blushes and is angry with himself, because at twenty-five his soul has not yet drunk any noble teaching.

He lists what this was doing to him: penance, anger at himself, sadness, comparing himself with other people, going hungry. And every day he put off the writing until tomorrow.

What he decides next is oddly modest. He will let Ariston's books lie quiet for a while, he tells Fronto, read some of Cicero's shorter speeches, and then give himself to the task. He closes with a joke aimed at the very craft Fronto taught. He can only write for one side of a case, he says, because Ariston will never sleep so soundly that Marcus could argue both.

Haines dates the letter to the year after Marcus's twenty-fifth birthday began, between April 146 and April 147, and calls it the parting of the ways between rhetoric and philosophy. The turn is easy to follow in the Meditations, where Marcus later lists what he owed his teachers. From Rusticus he learned that his character needed mending. He learned not to write showy treatises or give speeches urging people to be good, to drop rhetoric, poetry and fine language, and to read carefully instead of settling for a quick impression. Rusticus also lent him a copy of Epictetus's notes out of his own library.

Fronto got his thanks as well. Marcus credits him with showing how much envy, deceit and hypocrisy a ruler's position attracts.

[[lessons]]
$$**Tell your teacher what is pulling you away from the path they set.** A young cook who has started to care more about bread than about the pastry her chef trained her for can say so at the next tasting, while still handing in the pastry she owes. Marcus did it in a letter that mixes a confession with a deadline and a joke, and it kept Fronto in his life long after rhetoric had lost him. Saying the shift aloud lets the old mentor remain part of the new direction instead of finding out from the outside.$$

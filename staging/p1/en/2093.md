[[open]]
A computer's fast memory is full, and a new piece of data arrives that must be stored right now. Something already inside has to go. If the machine could see which item it would ask for next, the choice would be trivial. It cannot, and that gap is the whole puzzle.

[[story]]
László Bélády was born in Budapest in 1928, according to published biographies, left Hungary after the 1956 revolution and worked at IBM's Thomas J. Watson Research Center from 1961 to 1981. In 1966 he published a paper in the *IBM Systems Journal* called "A Study of Replacement Algorithms for a Virtual-Storage Computer". Brian Christian and Tom Griffiths gather this kind of computer-science idea in *Algorithms to Live By*, a book about computer science and human decisions.

The problem he studied was concrete. A computer with virtual storage keeps only some of a program's pages in fast memory at once. When fast memory is full and a page is missing, one page must be thrown out to make room, and every poor choice costs a slow trip to fetch it back. Which page should go?

His answer, called MIN, was to evict the page whose next use lies farthest in the future. The logic is easy to feel on a desk. If you must clear a spot, you remove the paper you will not touch for the longest time, because it will cost you the fewest trips to the filing cabinet. Any other choice means walking back sooner than you needed to.

There is a catch, and it makes the rule both useless and priceless. To apply it, the machine must know the entire future sequence of requests, which no real program discloses. So nobody can run MIN in practice on a live system. Later work, credited to Mattson and colleagues in 1970 according to a standard textbook bibliography, supplied a proof that the rule is optimal.

Its value is as a yardstick. If you record what a program actually asked for, you can replay it afterwards and see how many misses a perfect, clairvoyant policy would have had. Then you can measure real, practical policies against that floor. According to published summaries, Bélády's paper compared several practical families this way, among them first-in-first-out and least-recently-used.

That last one rests on a bet about time: a page not used for a long while is probably not needed soon, so it is the safest to drop. It is a guess about the future built from the past, and it is the sort of rule that real systems adopted because the clairvoyant one was impossible.

The structure of the argument is what lasts. First, define the perfect answer even if it cannot be run. Second, build the best rule that uses only what you can know. Third, grade your rule against the perfect one, so you know how much is still being lost.

[[lessons]]
$$**Judge what you keep by when you will next need it, and use the past to estimate that moment.** Someone clearing a cupboard can ask of each item the question a memory manager asks of each page, namely how long it will be before it is reached for again. Things used every week stay in easy reach, and things untouched for a long time are the safest to move to the attic. You cannot see the future, but you can look at your own recent history to guess it, and then you can check afterwards how good the guesses were.$$

[[open]]
A finance professor writes down a hundred dollars and compounds it forward through thirty-one years of reported returns, one year at a time. Then he writes a paper about the number at the bottom of the page. A later group of researchers looks at the same fund and asks whether the starting arithmetic was right.

[[story]]
Gregory Zuckerman's *The Man Who Solved the Market* reports two figures for the Medallion fund over 1988 to 2018: an average return of 66.1 percent a year before fees and 39.1 percent after them. Medallion was Jim Simons's fund at Renaissance Technologies, and it stopped taking money from outside clients in 1993. Since 2005 only employees have been allowed to invest. Its fees reached 5 percent of assets and 44 percent of profits, according to Fortune.

The fund's results have drawn unusual scrutiny. Bradford Cornell, a finance professor emeritus at UCLA, took the reported yearly returns and calculated that one hundred dollars invested at the start of 1988 would have become $398,723,873 by the end of 2018. The same amount in a broad stock-market index with dividends reinvested would have reached $1,910. Cornell's paper concluded that no adequate explanation based on efficient markets exists, and that even calling Medallion the best trader ever would stretch that explanation to the limit.

One published detail concerns the success rate per trade. Robert Mercer, who later became co-chief executive, reportedly said that Medallion's trades were right 50.75 percent of the time. A tiny edge, repeated across a very large number of trades, can add up. Net returns were reportedly above 29 percent in every year from 2010 to 2018.

In 2024 another group of researchers challenged the headline. Their argument is that the 66 percent is reported as an average of yearly percentages, and that averaging yearly returns is not the same as measuring how fast money grows once each year is compounded onto the last. Using fund sizes and trading profits, they estimated a compounded return before fees of 31.8 percent. Using Simons's own wealth as a proxy, they got a growth rate of 25.6 percent over the thirty-three years through 2020. Their conclusion was that the compounded annual return before fees was probably under 35 percent.

Even the lower estimates describe a fund that grew for decades at a pace far above typical market returns. The disagreement is about arithmetic and definitions. A headline figure depends on what is being averaged, over what period, before or after what costs, and the reader has to ask which version is on the page.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask how a headline return was calculated before you let it set your expectations.** A fund that doubles in one year and halves the next shows a positive average, yet leaves its holders exactly where they began. A friend who says a stock "averages a handsome return every year" is describing something different from what your account balance will show. Next time a result sounds too good to be true, check three things on the page itself: whether it is gross or net of fees, whether it is an average or a compounded figure, and which years it covers.$$

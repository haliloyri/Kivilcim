A nurse spends her working days with people who are close to death. They tell her what they wish they had done differently, and she keeps listening. After enough of these conversations, the same few sentences keep coming back, and one of them is about work.

[[story]]
Bill Perkins builds *Die with Zero* on a simple idea: turn your money into experiences while you can still enjoy them. Among the evidence people tend to reach for is what the dying say about their lives, and the best-known version comes from Bronnie Ware, who worked in palliative care.

In 2009 she published a blog post called "Regrets of the Dying". By 2012 it had been read by eight million people. She then expanded it into a book, *The Top Five Regrets of the Dying*, which has since been translated into 27 languages. The post is a list, and it is short enough to remember.

The first item is the one she found most common: "I wish I'd had the courage to live a life true to myself, not the life others expected of me." The second is "I wish I hadn't worked so hard." Then come "I wish I'd had the courage to express my feelings," "I wish I had stayed in touch with my friends," and "I wish that I had let myself be happier."

Read the list slowly and look at what is missing. Nobody on it wishes for a larger house, a bigger pension or one more promotion. Every item is something that stayed possible for years and only became impossible at the very end: a phone call, an honest sentence, a refusal to take on another evening of work.

Ware's account is observation, not measurement, and she did not claim otherwise. Years later two psychologists, Shai Davidai and Thomas Gilovich, studied regret and found that people were more likely to voice regrets about their ideal selves, such as dreams not followed and potential not reached, than regrets about duties left undone. They called her observations anecdotal, and wrote that those observations were in line with their hypothesis.

That is a modest kind of confirmation, and it fits the list. The regrets that come at the end are rarely about a purchase. They concern the life that was lived for other people's expectations, the friends who drifted, the feelings that stayed unsaid. The dying simply meet that bill last, when nothing can be done about it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Treat the things you keep calling "someday" as dated tasks.** Every regret on Ware's list came from something that was possible for years and was postponed without a decision. A woman who keeps meaning to call an old friend from her first job, or a man who has never told his brother how much a favour once meant, is making the same quiet bet that there will always be time. Put one such thing on the calendar this month, with a day and a first step, and it stops being a vague wish and becomes a small appointment you can actually keep.$$

Think about the first time you saw something beautiful on a daily route: a view, a building, a tree in full bloom. Now think about the hundredth time. You probably can't remember it, because you weren't really seeing it anymore. Seth Godin built a whole book around that small, uncomfortable fact, and it starts with a family car and a field of cows.

[[story]]
Seth Godin and his family were driving through France. Along the road, field after field, there were cows grazing. They were the kind you'd find in a children's picture book, calm and pretty and perfectly placed against the green.

At first everyone in the car was charmed. They watched out the window. They pointed. They admired the cows the way you admire anything new and lovely when you're on holiday.

Then, within about twenty minutes, nobody was looking anymore. The cows were still there, just as picturesque as before. But the family had gone back to their conversations, their thoughts, whatever was happening inside the car.

##The cows hadn't changed at all. They had simply become familiar, and familiar had become boring.##

Godin's explanation is almost too simple. A new cow looks like the last cow. Once you've seen a few, the next one gives you nothing new to notice. It can be a perfectly good cow, healthy and handsome and exactly what a cow should be, and your eyes will still slide right past it.

Then he asks what would have made them look again. His answer gives the book its title.

> A purple cow.

A purple cow would have been worth a look. It would have been worth pulling over for, worth pointing out to everyone in the car, worth mentioning later to friends. It would have been, in Godin's word, *remarkable*. He means that literally: worth making a remark about.

From there he turns to business, and the cows become a way of describing almost everything you can buy. His argument is that "very good" products are now so common that they've become invisible. A very good product is the brown cow in the field. People don't dislike it. They just don't see it.

This leads to the claim that makes the book sting. Most organizations believe the responsible move is to play it safe: make something solid, avoid anything strange, don't take chances. Godin says that's backwards. Being safe and boring is the real risk, because safe and boring is exactly what gets ignored.

He also says where the remarkable part has to come from. You can't paint the cow purple afterward with advertising. It has to be designed into the product itself, into the thing you actually make. When it's there, it spreads on its own. The first enthusiastic users notice it, talk about it, and tell others. If it isn't there, no amount of louder promotion will make people turn their heads for more than a moment.

So the scene in the car is the whole argument in small. The cows got delight, then indifference, in about twenty minutes. Godin's point is that most of what we produce is on that same clock.

[[lessons]]
$$**Good fades faster than you think.** The family's attention lasted about twenty minutes. The cows didn't get worse; the viewers got used to them. Anything that is merely good follows the same curve, whether it's a product, a report, or a habit of yours. People admire it at first and stop seeing it soon after. Quality alone doesn't hold anyone's eyes for long.$$

$$**Remarkable is different from better.** A slightly healthier or better-proportioned cow wouldn't have made anyone look up. A purple one would have made them stop the car. Godin's test isn't whether something wins on paper. It's whether someone would bother to remark on it. That's a different question, and it often has a different answer.$$

$$**The safe choice carries the most risk.** Playing it safe felt responsible, but in Godin's argument it guarantees being passed by. And the fix can't be bolted on later with advertising. The difference has to be built into the work itself, so that the first people who meet it want to tell someone else.$$

[[reflect]]
- Think of something you produce regularly that people now accept without comment, the way the family stopped looking out the window.
- Try describing what you offer in one sentence. Is there a detail in it that someone would repeat to a friend?
- Pick a recent decision where you went with the familiar option. Did you choose it because it works, or because it felt responsible?
- Ask where you've been relying on promotion to make something noticeable instead of changing the thing itself.

[[open]]
A business that you started and that failed leaves a bill you can read: the money, the months, the awkward conversations. A business you never started leaves nothing on paper, so the loss seems not to exist. Psychologists asked which of the two people feel worse about, and the answer depends on what day you ask.

[[story]]
Regret has a clock, and Thomas Gilovich and Victoria Medvec set out to read it. Their work, published in 1994 in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* and reviewed again in 1995 in *Psychological Review* under the title "The experience of regret: What, when, and why", started from a puzzle. Earlier studies disagreed about whether people regret what they did or what they failed to do.

Their answer was that both were right, at different times. In the short run, actions produce more regret: the bad hire, the rash purchase, the embarrassing message sent. Over the long run, inactions are the ones people regret more, the chance they let pass, the question they never asked. The two kinds of regret do not sit at the same point on the timeline, so a survey taken at one moment can easily contradict a survey taken at another.

The authors also proposed that several mechanisms could account for this pattern, and they did not rest the whole finding on a single explanation. Gilovich has said the practical version many times, and in his own paraphrase it comes to this: over the years you will regret more of the things you did not do than the things you did.

Bill Perkins, in *Die with Zero*, argues that people should spend their money, time and energy on experiences before the window for them closes. This research gives that argument a psychological backbone. A decision to skip the trip, to save the money, to wait for a better year, feels safe on the day you make it. The cost of skipping is not on any statement that arrives next month. It is paid later, in a regret that has had years to grow.

Look at the asymmetry from the decision-maker's chair. A mistake gives immediate feedback: the sting arrives quickly and starts to fade soon after, and you can often point at what you would change. An omission gives almost none at first, which is why it is so easy to repeat. By the time the regret is loud, the chance has usually gone.

None of this tells you to say yes to everything. It says that a person weighing a risky step is comparing two different clocks: the short, sharp sting of a failure and the slow, quiet weight of a door never opened.

[[lessons]]
$$**Weigh a decision against how you will feel about it long after the first sting.** A shopkeeper deciding whether to expand into the shop next door will feel the fear of failure right away, while the regret of never trying would only arrive years later and so gets no vote today. Asking yourself both questions, "how will a failure feel next month?" and "how will skipping this feel after many years?", puts the two clocks next to each other. It does not settle the choice, but it keeps the quieter cost from being counted as zero.$$

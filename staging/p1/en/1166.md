Look at the roster of one of Canada's best junior hockey teams. You'd expect the players' birthdays to be scattered across the calendar. They aren't. An unusually large number of them were born in the first few months of the year. It turns out to be one of the most revealing facts about talent you'll ever read.

[[story]]
Malcolm Gladwell opens *Outliers* with a hockey team: the Medicine Hat Tigers, who played in the final of the 2007 Memorial Cup, the championship of Canadian junior hockey. These were some of the most promising young players in the country, many of them on their way to professional careers.

Glance at their birth dates, and a pattern jumps out. January. February. March. April. Again and again, the players were born early in the year.

The pattern had first been noticed more than twenty years earlier. In the mid-1980s, a Canadian psychologist named Roger Barnsley was at a junior hockey game in Lethbridge, Alberta, with his wife. She was reading the program and noticed the players' birthdays. Barnsley went on to study it properly. He found that in any elite group of Canadian hockey players, about 40 percent had been born between January and March. If talent were spread evenly across the year, you'd expect about a quarter.

##The explanation wasn't in the players at all. It was in the calendar.##

In Canadian youth hockey, the cutoff date for age groups is January 1. A boy who turns ten on January 2 plays in the same age class as a boy who won't turn ten until the end of December. For adults, a year is nothing. For a nine-year-old, it's a huge difference in size, strength and coordination.

So when coaches pick the best nine- and ten-year-olds for elite teams, they naturally choose the bigger, faster, more coordinated boys. They think they're spotting talent. Very often, they're spotting maturity.

And then the advantage starts to grow. The boys who are selected get better coaching. They play more games, sometimes two or three times as many. They practice alongside stronger teammates. By the time they're thirteen or fourteen, they really are better players. Not because of their birthday alone, but because of everything their birthday got them.

Gladwell calls this **accumulative advantage**, and he borrows a name for it from the Bible: the **Matthew Effect**, after the verse saying that to those who have, more will be given. A small, arbitrary edge at the start turns into a large, real one later.

The most striking part is what it means for the boys born in November and December. Many of them might have been just as gifted. They simply never got the same chance to prove it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Early maturity can look like talent.** Coaches choosing the "best" nine-year-olds were often just choosing the oldest. In any field where people are compared early, a head start of months can be mistaken for natural ability. It's worth asking, whenever you judge someone young, what exactly you're seeing.$$

$$**Small edges compound.** The January-born boys got better coaches, more games and stronger teammates, and in time became genuinely better. That's the Matthew Effect: an arbitrary early advantage turns into a real one. Advantages rarely stay the same size. They either grow or disappear, depending on the opportunities attached to them.$$

$$**Systems create winners, not only individuals.** The January 1 cutoff wasn't chosen to favor anyone. It still shaped entire careers. Gladwell's point is that we could design systems differently, with later cutoffs, more selection rounds or separate groups, so that more of the late bloomers get their shot.$$

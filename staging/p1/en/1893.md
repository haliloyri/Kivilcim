A microbiologist hands a doctor a beaker of cloudy liquid. The doctor tips it back in one go, the way you would swallow a glass of something you are not looking forward to. He is perfectly healthy, and the only point of the drink is to make that stop being true.

[[story]]
The trouble began over an Easter weekend in 1982, when a culture dish was left forgotten in a laboratory in Western Australia. When Barry Marshall came back, the bacteria he had been trying to grow from stomach biopsies had finally appeared. He and the pathologist Robin Warren had been working together since 1981, and they were finding the same spiral-shaped bacterium in nearly everyone with an inflamed stomach or an ulcer.

Almost nobody believed them. Doctors held that ulcers came from stress and acid, and that the stomach was too harsh a place for bacteria to live. Marshall had a bacterium, a pile of correlations and a room full of sceptics. What he lacked was a healthy person who became ill after being infected.

In 1984 he supplied one himself. According to Marshall's own later account, he first had a camera passed down his throat to confirm that his stomach carried no such bacteria. Some weeks afterwards, Neil Noakes, a microbiologist at Fremantle Hospital, prepared a thick suspension of the organism, and Marshall drank about 50 millilitres of it.

For a few days nothing happened. Around the fifth day he began to feel ill: stomach pain that woke him at night, nausea, daily vomiting. A second look inside showed an inflamed stomach lining, and the bacterium was growing there.

He had not developed an ulcer, so the swallow did not prove that ulcers come from the bacterium. What he had shown was narrower and still hard to dismiss: a healthy stomach, a known starting point, an infection, and then gastritis. The bacteria were found in his stomach afterwards, and they had not been there before.

The wider case then rested on treatment. Marshall and Warren showed that ulcers could be cured with antibiotics aimed at the bacterium, which turned a long, often disabling illness into a curable one. They shared the 2005 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for it.

The detail worth keeping is the baseline. Before drinking anything, Marshall had a recorded picture of his stomach as it was. Without that first look, the second would have been an anecdote about a man with a sore stomach. With it, the change had a clear before and after, and the change could speak for itself.

[[lessons]]
$$**Record where you stand before you change anything, so the change can prove something.** A cook who swaps the flour in a family recipe has no way to judge the new loaf unless she remembers how the old one rose and tasted. A note, a photo or a number taken at the start turns a feeling that things got better or worse into a comparison someone else can check. The habit costs a few minutes, and it protects you from your own hopeful memory. It also lets you say, with a straight face, that something worked or did not, because the starting point was written down while nobody had a stake in the answer.$$

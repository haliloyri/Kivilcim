A phone rings in a small cottage near a president's home. The valet's wife picks it up. It's the President of the United States. He's calling to tell her that there's a bird sitting outside her window, the kind she once asked him about.

[[story]]
Dale Carnegie loved this story. In *How to Win Friends and Influence People*, he quotes it from a book by James E. Amos, who worked as Theodore Roosevelt's valet.

Amos's wife had once asked Roosevelt what a bobwhite was. She had never seen one, and he described it to her. Some time later, the phone rang in their cottage. Roosevelt himself was on the line. He wanted to let her know that there was a bobwhite right outside her window, so she could finally see one.

Amos wrote that little things like this were typical of the man. Whenever Roosevelt passed their cottage, even if they were out of sight, they'd hear him call out a greeting to them.

Carnegie then tells another story. After Roosevelt had left the presidency, he visited the White House one day while President Taft and his wife were away. He went around greeting all the old White House servants. And he greeted them by name, every one of them, including the kitchen maids.

When he saw Alice, a kitchen maid, he asked her whether she still made corn bread. She told him she sometimes made it for the servants, but nobody ate it upstairs.

> They show bad taste, and I'll tell the President so when I see him.

Alice brought him a piece of corn bread on a plate, and Roosevelt ate it as he walked over to the office, greeting gardeners and laborers along the way.

##One of the old employees said, with tears in his eyes, that it was the only happy day they'd had in nearly two years.##

For Carnegie, the lesson is simple and very practical. Most people, he observes, pay more attention to their own name than to all the other names in the world combined. So he states it as one of his core principles: remember that a person's name is to that person the sweetest and most important sound in any language.

Remembering a name is not a trick. It's evidence. It tells the other person that when you met them, they registered with you as someone who mattered. Roosevelt was one of the busiest men in the world. The kitchen staff knew that. Which is exactly why hearing their names from him meant so much.

Carnegie is also frank about the effort involved. Most of us, he says, don't remember names because we don't take the time to fix them in our minds. We make excuses about being too busy. Roosevelt, far busier than most, made the time.

[[lessons]]
$$**A name says "you matter."** Roosevelt greeted every servant by name, even years after he'd left. Carnegie calls a person's name the sweetest sound to them in any language. Using it shows that someone registered with you as a person, not as a role, and people remember that feeling for a long time.$$

$$**Small details carry enormous weight.** A phone call about a bird. A question about corn bread. None of it cost Roosevelt much. To the people involved, it was unforgettable. Remembering one small thing about someone's life often means more to them than any grand gesture you could make.$$

$$**Busy is not an excuse.** Carnegie points out that most of us blame our schedules for forgetting names. Roosevelt had perhaps the most demanding job in the country and still made the effort. The difference usually isn't time. It's whether we decide people are worth remembering.$$

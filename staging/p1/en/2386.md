Night over a West Virginia coal town. A few boys stand in a yard and watch a small light slide between the stars, steady and silent. Down in the hollow the mine keeps its shifts, and up in the sky something built by people is heading where no miner's cart has ever gone.

[[story]]
Homer Hickam's memoir *Rocket Boys* begins in Coalwood, West Virginia, a company town where the coal mine decided nearly everything. His father was the mine's superintendent, and on both sides of the family the men had been miners for generations. Homer, known as Sonny, was fourteen that autumn. He liked science fiction more than football.

On 4 October 1957 the Soviet Union launched Sputnik, the first artificial satellite. Hickam has said that he divides his youth into the time before that date and the time after. In his memoir the satellite crosses the sky over Coalwood, and Sonny and his friends watch it go.

Then they decided to build something of their own. Sonny and five friends from Big Creek High School, Roy Lee Cooke, Willie Rose, Jim O'Dell Carroll, Quentin Wilson and Sherman Siers, began making rockets and named themselves the Big Creek Missile Agency. Publishers Weekly notes that the first attempt, with twelve cherry bombs, went badly. Another reviewer reports that an early launch burned down his mother's garden fence.

His mother, Elsie, hated the coalfields, and the Publishers Weekly review says she saw the rockets as a possible road out of the mines. His father saw the town differently. Hickam's story does not make either of them wrong.

Sputnik changed nothing about the work in Coalwood itself. The mine was still the mine. What changed was what a fourteen-year-old could imagine doing with his evenings, and who he could imagine doing it with. Years later Hickam described his motive this way: "I had a need to learn things that seemed hidden from me."

Look at how small the first step was. Twelve cherry bombs is not a space program. The boys tried something crude, it went badly, and they tried again. Hickam later said the rocket work took three years of development, so that first crude try was only the start of a long road.

The satellite was far away and the work of a state. The rocket on the coal dump was the work of six teenagers. Hickam's account turns on that step from watching to building. A distant event stayed distant until someone picked up some powder, some pipe and a friend to hold the other end.

[[lessons]]
$$**A distant event becomes yours on the day you build something small beside it.** News of a new technology, a market shift or a rival's launch tends to stay a topic of conversation until someone gives it an object to make and a friend to make it with. The six boys could not touch the satellite, but they could build a first rocket and watch it fail, and that failure was already theirs. Pick one small thing you could put together this month, however crude, and ask who would hold the other end while you try it and what the first failure might teach you.$$

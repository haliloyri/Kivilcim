[[open]]
A dinner table holds wild boar, wild morel mushrooms, Bing cherries, lettuce from a backyard bed and bread raised with wild yeast. Behind each dish stands a hunt, a gathering trip or a garden job. None of those stories begins on a supermarket shelf.

[[story]]
"Morels love burnt pine forests, and therefore I love to find burnt pine forests," wrote Anthony Tassinello in a 2007 magazine piece. He is a private chef in the Bay Area who has cooked at Chez Panisse on and off since 1996, and he and his wife run a small business in local, seasonal wild mushrooms. Michael Pollan went after morels with him on burned California hillsides, for the last of the four meals in *The Omnivore's Dilemma*.

The four meals run from a McDonald's lunch eaten in a car to an organic meal from Whole Foods, and then to a dinner from Joel Salatin's Polyface Farm. The fourth meal was different. Pollan hunted a wild boar, foraged wild mushrooms, Bing cherries and the yeast for the bread, and grew the lettuce and herbs himself.

His guide for the boar was Angelo Garro. A review published on Pollan's own site calls Garro a San Francisco iron artist and a poster boy for the Slow Food movement, and describes the animal as a large grey sow. Tassinello adds that morels also like disturbed ground, which is why logged areas can be productive. For him the hunt starts with finding burned or disturbed ground, long before anyone bends down to look at the soil.

Laid out by source, every ingredient has its own route. Boar arrives by way of a guide and a hunt. Morels come from a burned slope and a forager who knows it. Bread rises on yeast nobody bought, and lettuce and herbs come from a garden outside the kitchen. What the items share is that each one was something Pollan hunted, gathered or grew, and for each there was someone who could tell its story.

The other three meals had people in between: a kitchen assembling a hamburger, a supermarket stocking its organic aisle, a farmer doing the slaughter. At the fourth the eater had to look after every step personally. The university's write-up notes that most readers could not realistically make a meal like this. That is also why it works as a yardstick: it shows how much work, time and knowledge a ready-made meal hides behind a price tag, a menu board or a shelf label.

[[lessons]]
$$**Do one thing yourself from the first step, and write down what each step costs.** Someone who bakes bread at home for the first time, starting from the yeast, notices the labour and time behind a packaged loaf and reads the shelf price differently afterwards. When every step passes through your own hands, you see which ones show up in the price and which stay invisible. You do not have to do this for everything, since one example is enough to give all your habits a new measure. Next time you stand at a shelf you will know how many steps a price can contain.$$

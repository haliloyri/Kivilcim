Pour a glass of water onto uneven ground and watch what happens. It doesn't argue with the rocks. It doesn't push uphill. It finds the low places and flows around every obstacle. More than two thousand years ago, a Chinese strategist saw in that simple behavior the essence of winning.

[[story]]
*The Art of War* is an ancient Chinese text on strategy, traditionally attributed to a general named Sun Tzu. Its exact date and authorship are uncertain, but for centuries it has been read by military leaders, and more recently by people in business, sport and politics.

In one of its most famous passages, from the chapter on weak points and strong, it compares good tactics to water. In Lionel Giles's classic English translation:

> Military tactics are like unto water; for water in its natural course runs away from high places and hastens downwards. So in war, the way is to avoid what is strong and to strike at what is weak.

##Water never attacks a mountain head-on. It goes around, through the gaps, toward the low ground.##

The first lesson is about where to direct your effort. A strong position is like high ground: attacking it directly is costly and often fails. The wise strategist, like water, doesn't waste strength hurling itself against strength. Instead, it looks for weakness: an unguarded flank, a gap, a place where resistance is low. That's where effort produces the greatest result.

The passage continues:

> Water shapes its course according to the nature of the ground over which it flows; the soldier works out his victory in relation to the foe whom he is facing.

The second lesson is about adaptation. Water has no fixed plan for how it will flow. It responds to the terrain. In the same way, a good strategist doesn't impose one formula on every situation. They study the opponent and the conditions, and shape their approach to fit.

And then comes the third lesson:

> Therefore, just as water retains no constant shape, so in warfare there are no constant conditions.

Circumstances change. A plan that worked yesterday may fail tomorrow. What stays constant is not the tactic, but the ability to adjust. The text says that someone who can modify their tactics in relation to the opponent, and thereby succeed, may be called heaven-born.

Outside the battlefield, the image translates easily. The most effective people and organizations often aren't the most forceful. They're the ones who see where resistance is lowest, adapt to changing conditions, and keep moving toward their goal without insisting on a single route.

[[lessons]]
$$**Avoid strength; move toward weakness.** Water runs away from high places toward the low. Sun Tzu's advice is to stop wasting effort attacking the strongest point. In competition, negotiation or problem-solving, the greatest gains usually come where resistance is lowest, not where you can prove how tough you are.$$

$$**Let the terrain shape your route.** Water shapes its course to the ground; the strategist shapes their approach to the opponent. A fixed plan applied everywhere tends to break. Studying the specific situation, and adapting to it, is what turns a general goal into a path that actually works.$$

$$**No condition stays the same.** "Water retains no constant shape." What worked last year may fail this year. Sun Tzu's deeper lesson is that flexibility itself is the strength. Holding firmly to the goal while staying fluid about the method is what lets you keep advancing when circumstances shift.$$

[[open]]
In an old office of a London government building, a few civilians are reading enemy wireless messages that the enemy believes nobody can read. Their work is worth more the fewer people know it exists. One of the largest ships in the world is about to cross their patch of sea, and it has just been sunk.

[[story]]
The group was called Room 40, and it appears in Erik Larson's *Dead Wake* as part of the Admiralty. It had been formed in October 1914 when Rear-Admiral Henry Oliver handed Alfred Ewing a pile of intercepted German signals and Ewing recruited civilians and naval-college staff to work on them. On 6 November 1914 they moved into a room in the Admiralty Old Building. Its whole value was secrecy: if Germany guessed that its naval codes were being read, it would change them.

When the Lusitania was sunk, the Admiralty had to explain itself. Larson says in an interview that the Admiralty went after Captain Turner immediately, within 24 hours, and that he believes it was trying to protect Room 40. He is careful not to claim a plot: "I'm not saying that there was or there wasn't," he says, and he notes that keeping Room 40 secret was one of Churchill's top priorities at the Admiralty.

The inquiry that followed, chaired by Lord Mersey, shows what the Admiralty's effort looked like. Hearings were held at Westminster Central Hall from 15 to 18 June 1915, and the sessions on navigation were closed to the public. According to published accounts of the evidence, internal Admiralty notes by Fisher and Churchill showed an intention to lay the blame on Turner, and an official, Richard Webb, compiled a dossier of signals. A signal that counsel quoted in court turned out not to exist in some versions of the evidence, and Mersey became more critical of the Admiralty's evidence afterward.

Mersey's own words in the report are measured. He wrote that the Admiralty "had devoted the most anxious care and thought to the questions arising out of the submarine peril," and he found that blame ought not to be imputed to the captain. What exactly Room 40 knew about U-20 in the days before the sinking is still argued by historians, and some of the Admiralty's radio exchanges with the ship are said to remain classified, so the question is open.

The structure of the episode is the point. An organisation held something valuable and fragile. When the worst happened, protecting it and telling the whole story pulled in different directions. In the end the inquiry's finding kept the blame off the one man the Admiralty had first pointed at.

[[lessons]]
$$**When you protect something valuable, name who is paying for the protection.** A secret, a reputation or a budget can feel too important to put at risk, and the cost of guarding it often falls on someone less visible than you. When you cover for a team, a product or a decision, ask what a person outside your circle would have to give up so that you can keep it safe, and then decide on purpose whether that price is right. People trust you more when they can see that you counted the cost before you asked them to carry it.$$

[[story]]
When Eric Schmidt became Google's chief executive in 2001, the venture capitalist John Doerr suggested he sit down with Bill Campbell. Schmidt had run Novell, and he told Fortune in 2008: "My initial reaction was 'I don't need any help.'" Fortune's profile of Campbell, the figure at the centre of *Trillion Dollar Coach*, records how the first session went.

Asked what he wanted from Google, Campbell said he was "doing payback" and trying to help others. Then he asked Schmidt about the most interesting problems at the company. The first one on the list was building an executive team. Campbell began with Schmidt's agenda.

He kept turning up. Fortune reports that he attended the Monday executive meeting, often as the only person in the room from outside Google, and that he frequently sat in on the Tuesday product pitches. He helped set up a product management group designed so that it would not step on the engineers' toes, and he helped shape the board, two of whose members also sat on Apple's board, so Campbell stood where the two companies overlapped. On a candidate for a job he was blunt: "Ehhhhh … no. There's something wrong with his character."

Schmidt came to describe the arrangement as a tag team. Campbell would help him pick the three topics that mattered most for a staff or board meeting. He also helped Schmidt and the two founders reach decisions together. According to Fortune, Schmidt, Larry Page and Sergey Brin settled their disagreements privately and then presented a united front. A Google executive, Chris Sacca, told the magazine: "There's no doubt that it was all Coach."

By 2008 Schmidt's view had reversed. He told Fortune that Campbell's contribution to Google was "literally not possible to overstate." The man who had said he needed no help ended up giving a coach a seat in his Monday meetings for years, and in 2008 Schmidt was still describing the effect in superlatives.

The detail that explains the reversal is the order of events. Campbell began with questions. He asked about problems, found out which of them Schmidt cared about, and made himself useful on those. By the time he started giving opinions, he had already shown that he was listening.

[[lessons]]
$$**Start by asking what the other person is struggling with, and wait to give your view.** Schmidt came in sure he needed no help, and what changed his mind was a first meeting spent on his own problems instead of someone else's solutions. If you want to coach or advise a person who resists, open with a question about the hardest thing on their plate and let them rank the answers. Advice that attaches itself to a problem someone already cares about and has named for themselves gets a real hearing, while the same advice delivered cold usually gets politely ignored.$$

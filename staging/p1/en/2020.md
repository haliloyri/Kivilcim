In a gorge outside the Italian town of Gubbio, a band of clay no thicker than a finger runs through the limestone. Below it is limestone from the age of the dinosaurs, above it limestone from the age that followed. A geologist takes a sample of the clay, and at first it tells him nothing.

[[story]]
Elizabeth Kolbert covers this episode in *The Sixth Extinction*, in the chapter on the Cretaceous extinction that ended the dinosaurs. The geologist was Walter Alvarez, and the clay he sampled at Gubbio marked the boundary between the Cretaceous and the Tertiary. It was only about half an inch thick. Analysed in the ordinary ways, it yielded nothing of interest.

His father, the Nobel Prize-winning physicist Luis Alvarez, suggested a different question. Look for the platinum-group metals, he proposed, elements that are scarce in the Earth's crust but more common in meteorites and asteroids, which makes them a tracer for material that fell from space. The amounts to be measured were parts per billion, far beyond what a geologist's usual tools could detect, so the chemists Frank Asaro and Helen Michel joined the team to do the measuring.

The clay held much more iridium than the rock above or below it. The same spike turned up at other sites. In the paper that appeared in *Science* on 6 June 1980, the iridium was about 30 times the background level in Italy, about 160 times in Denmark and about 20 times in New Zealand, at the exact layer that marked the extinction.

The authors drew a bold conclusion. A large asteroid, they wrote, had struck the Earth and thrown up about 60 times its own mass in pulverised rock. Part of that dust would have stayed in the stratosphere for years and spread around the globe, and the resulting darkness would have suppressed photosynthesis. Four separate estimates put the asteroid's diameter at about 10 kilometres, give or take four. They also pointed to one prediction that had already checked out: the boundary clay differed in chemical composition from the clay mixed into the limestone above and below it.

Many scientists stayed doubtful, because no crater had been found, and the doubters had a fair point: an impact by a body of that size should have left a very large mark on the planet. The case needed a scar. In 1991 a team led by Alan Hildebrand published a description of Chicxulub, on Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula, as a possible impact crater from the boundary between the Cretaceous and the Tertiary. The crater was already sitting under the Yucatán when the iridium was measured in Italy, and it turned out to be the missing half of the argument.

[[lessons]]
$$**When the usual measurement shows nothing, change what you measure.** The Gubbio clay looked like a dead end until someone asked a different question of the same sample, and the new question came from outside the geologist's own field. A shop owner who checks total sales for the slow Tuesdays and finds nothing odd might count the people who come in and leave without buying, and find the pattern there. The same sample and the same situation can answer a question nobody asked yet, so list two or three quantities you have never looked at before you give up on a puzzle.$$

[[story]]
In March, a heap of cattle manure, straw, wood chips and corn stands about three feet high on a farm in Virginia's Shenandoah Valley, and the pigs are about to go in. Joel Salatin runs Polyface Farm there with his family. Michael Pollan visited and wrote about it for the *New York Times Magazine* in 2002, and the farm became the third meal of *The Omnivore's Dilemma*.

Salatin keeps six kinds of food animals: cattle, pigs, chickens, rabbits, turkeys and sheep. His stated aim is to let each species express its physiological distinctiveness. Six species share one farm.

What makes that possible is order. Once the cattle have grazed a pasture, Salatin moves them out and brings in the eggmobile, a portable coop that houses several hundred laying hens. The hens eat the short grass, pick larvae out of the cow manure and scatter it, which also cuts down the farm's parasite problem. The farm's own website describes its animals as moved frequently to new "salad bars."

Sheep follow later and eat the new growth and the weeds the others avoid. Through the winter, Salatin layers the cattle manure with straw, wood chips and corn, which is how the heap reaches its three feet by March. The pigs root through it, find the fermented corn and aerate the pile as they dig.

He also kills the chickens and rabbits on the farm, in an open-air abattoir behind the farmhouse. Every other Saturday in summer the work is done with visitors welcome to watch, and he would process cattle, pigs and sheep there too if USDA rules allowed it.

Follow the sequence and each animal arrives to find the last one's leftovers waiting. The cows leave manure for the hens, the hens leave scattered pasture for the sheep, and the winter heap leaves a corn-laced pile for the pigs. Each job is attached to the one before it. The hens are useful partly because the cows came first.

Pollan also reports a visitor who came to the farm after 16 years as a vegetarian, killed a chicken himself and said afterwards that he could eat meat again. The slaughter sits behind the house, in the open, and it follows the same logic as the rest of the farm: whatever happens there can be seen.

[[lessons]]
$$**Build each step so that what it leaves behind feeds the next one.** A café owner who turns yesterday's unsold bread into this morning's toast and soup cuts the waste and skips a separate purchase as well, which is the same rule applied to a counter instead of a pasture. The same thinking works in an office: the notes from one meeting can become the ready-made starting document for the next team. When you plan a sequence of tasks, ask what each step leaves behind and who could use it, and you will look at what you already have on the table before you go looking for something new.$$

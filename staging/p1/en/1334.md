[[open]]
Two Latin words: memento mori. Remember that you will die. It sounds like the start of a gloomy evening. The Stoics who used the phrase had something more practical in mind. The reminder was a tool, something to carry and reach for when a choice needed making.

[[story]]
The idea is attributed to Mark Manson, author of Everything Is F*cked, and it's simple to state. Remembering that you will die clarifies what matters and what doesn't. The phrase comes from the Stoics, and the practice is old. What's surprising is the direction it points in. It isn't a way to feel worse. It's a way to choose.

Start with the plain fact the phrase points at. Your time is limited. Most days, that fact sits somewhere in the background, easy to ignore. Life feels open-ended, as if there will always be another week, another year, another chance to get around to things.

That sense of endless time has a cost. When time feels unlimited, nothing has to be ranked. Every worry, obligation and small irritation can claim a share of attention, because there seems to be room for all of them. Trivial concerns don't get cleared out. They just pile up.

Facing mortality works against that pile. ##A limit on time forces a question that endless time lets you dodge: given that this runs out, what deserves it?## Once the question is asked seriously, some concerns shrink. Others, often the ones you've been postponing, move to the front.

That's the core claim: facing mortality, rather than avoiding the thought, helps people drop trivial concerns and choose what they value. Avoiding the thought doesn't make time any less limited. It only hides the limit, and with it the reason to prioritize.

This is also why the practice is something to carry rather than a one-time reflection. A reminder you return to can be used at ordinary moments of choice: how to spend an evening, which worry deserves more of your attention, which commitment to keep. The phrase is short enough to fit anywhere.

There's no dramatic case attached to this idea, and no transformation story. It works at the scale of small decisions. You hold the fact of a limit next to a choice, and the choice gets easier to see clearly.

[[lessons]]
$$**A limit makes priorities visible.** Memento mori puts an edge on time, and an edge forces ranking. When the supply seems endless, everything can wait its turn and nothing has to be chosen over anything else. Once you remember the supply runs out, the question changes from "what could I do?" to "what deserves this?", and the honest answers tend to be shorter. The reminder doesn't pick for you; it makes you pick.$$

$$**Avoiding the thought leaves room for the trivial.** Not thinking about death doesn't give you more time. It just removes the pressure to choose, so minor worries and small obligations spread out to fill the space. Facing the limit is what lets you drop some of them. The claim isn't that you should dwell on mortality, only that you shouldn't hide from it when deciding how to spend your time.$$

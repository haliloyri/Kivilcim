A company letter is about to cover the future, the finances and the next chief executive. Before readers reach any of that they meet a single sentence, and it talks about no numbers or strategy. It talks about what a family might ask.

[[story]]
Warren Buffett runs Berkshire Hathaway and writes a letter to its shareholders every year. In the letter marking the company's 50th anniversary, a sentence of introduction came before the sections on strategic outlook, finances and the future chief executive. Buffett wrote that he would tell shareholders what he would say to his family today if they asked him about Berkshire's future.

Robert Cialdini reads this sentence as a good example of pre-suasion. That is his interpretation, not knowledge of Buffett's intent. According to Cialdini, by saying he would advise the reader the way he would advise a family member, Buffett made the letter more convincing.

A company letter is usually a distant document. An executive informs, a shareholder looks at the numbers, and the relationship between them is formal. The sentence about the family changes that. Readers find themselves seated at a family table, where frankness and sincerity are expected and polish looks out of place. Same numbers, but they now arrive from inside a different relationship.

What the sentence does is set a frame, which is a different job from giving information. Once a frame is set, readers know what kind of speech they are hearing: not a sales pitch but an open assessment given to someone close. They read the rest of the letter with that expectation. The frame also sets a standard for what follows, because if the later pages are less open than a family conversation, the sentence rings hollow.

A related experiment is mentioned in the news piece about Cialdini. In a 2010 study in the *Journal of Consumer Research*, business students showed participants a fictional fast-casual restaurant called "Splash!". Participants who were asked for "advice" about it reported a significantly greater desire to eat there than those asked for their "opinions" or "expectations". One possible reading is that being asked for advice puts a person in the role of an advisor who is on the restaurant's side, but that is an interpretation.

It would be a mistake to draw more from this than it holds. There is no measurement here of how much the sentence added to the letter, and Buffett's long reputation probably shaped how shareholders read it anyway. The logic Cialdini points to is plain: before people weigh a claim, they want to feel what relationship the speaker has with them. Buffett set that relationship with a single sentence before giving the content.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you explain something important, state clearly what relationship you are speaking from.** A repair person who opens an estimate with "I will explain this the way I would want it explained to me for my own house" can turn the same itemized list from a bargaining text into a piece of advice. The frame is honest only if the speaker really will act that way, and otherwise it falls apart quickly and costs trust. So first decide what relationship you are setting, then ask whether what you say next can carry it, so the opening line makes a promise to the rest of your words and you keep it.$$

NASA releases a photo from Mars, and everyone who looks at it sees the same thing: a human face staring up from the dust. Twenty-odd years later a sharper camera passes over the same spot, and the face is gone. What is left is a shadowy hill.

[[story]]
Carl Sagan takes up this photograph in *The Demon-Haunted World*. The Viking 1 spacecraft had shot an image of the Cydonia region of Mars, and NASA made it public on 25 July 1976. In the frame sits a hill half buried in shadow. Across its top are dark patches that read as two eye sockets, a nose and a mouth. A face, on the surface of another planet.

The picture became known as the Face on Mars. Its resolution was poor, and the camera of that era could not pick out fine detail. Viewers filled in the missing pixels themselves, and almost all of them filled them in the same way. Nobody had enough data to ask whether the thing was really a face, so the first impression stood unchallenged.

Then years went by. On 5 April 1998 the Mars Global Surveyor passed over Cydonia and photographed the hill about ten times sharper than Viking had managed. In the new picture there was no face. On 8 April 2001 the spacecraft returned to the same region and took a second image, which agreed with the first.

NASA's explanation was plain. The feature is a mesa: a hill with a flat top and steep sides. The impression of eyes, nose and mouth in the old photo came from shadows. Photograph the same hill with a better instrument and the face dissolves.

None of this was a hoax. It was a habit of the human brain on display. We are tuned so tightly to spot faces that we find them in clouds, in rock surfaces, in the three holes of a wall socket. The name for it is pareidolia. The people who read the 1976 image were not being dishonest; they had a blurry picture and a strong instinct, and the instinct won.

The real lesson sits in the gap between the two photographs. For twenty-two years the hill did not change. It did not grow, shrink or rearrange itself. The only thing that changed was the sharpness of the instrument pointed at it. Until the better image arrived, "that is a face" sounded reasonable, simply because no data existed that could say otherwise.

[[lessons]]
$$**Do not treat a conclusion drawn from blurry data as settled, and update it the moment sharper data arrives.** When you read customer feedback from one sentence, a sales dip from one monthly figure, or a colleague's attitude from one meeting, the first impression is strong but not sharp. Look at the same question a month later with more evidence and your reading may shift; that is not weakness. Re-testing your first call each time a new measurement lands costs far less than defending it, and it lets you change course while changing course is still cheap, before a team or a budget has been built around the blurry guess.$$

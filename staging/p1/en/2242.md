A woman walks onto a stage carrying a real human brain, spinal cord hanging beneath it. She holds it in front of a seated audience, then starts telling them about the morning her own brain began to fail. She is a brain scientist, and she was watching it happen.

[[story]]
Jill Bolte Taylor woke on 10 December 1996 with a pounding pain behind her left eye. She went to exercise anyway. On her cardio glider she looked at her hands and, by her own account, they seemed like primitive claws gripping the bar. Then her right arm hung paralysed at her side. A trained neuroanatomist, she understood that she was having a stroke.

What came next, she told a TED audience in February 2008, felt closer to wonder than to fear. She could no longer tell where her body ended and the room began. The feeling she describes is one of expanding and growing quiet, and she calls it finding nirvana. Yet one part of her mind still worked on the problem: she had to get help.

Her office number had vanished from her memory. She picked up a stack of business cards, but the words and symbols looked to her like pixels. By her telling, getting one inch into the stack took about forty-five minutes. She matched the shapes on a card to the keypad, and used her paralysed arm to keep track of the digits she had already dialled. When a colleague finally answered, what came out of her mouth sounded, to her, like a golden retriever.

According to her account, about two and a half weeks after the haemorrhage surgeons removed a clot that was pressing on her language centres. Recovery took her eight years.

On the TED stage she turned all this into a lesson about the two halves of the brain. The right hemisphere, she said, lives in the present moment, in images, sensation and movement. The left is linear and verbal, tied to past and future, and it carries the voice that says "I am", the one that makes us feel separate from everyone else. Each of us, she argued, can choose moment by moment which side to step into.

The talk went on to become one of the most-watched in TED's history. Part of the reason is its construction. She joined a scientific claim to testimony from her own body and to a real brain in her hands, so the audience never had to take an abstraction on trust. They listened to a morning, and the idea came attached to it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Carry an abstract idea on a scene you have lived and an object people can see.** If you explain risk to a team, a product to a customer or health to a child, a concept left on its own is easy to forget. A concrete moment from your own experience, plus one thing on the table, makes the same information stick. Before you present, ask what the scene of this idea is and what you can put in the listener's hands. Then you stop defending a claim and start inviting people to see it for themselves, especially when the topic is complex and the audience is cautious.$$

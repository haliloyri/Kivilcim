"The last time he came here, he parked far away in a spot where there were no meters so he could save the coins." That is how a Vermont lawyer remembered a client, an elderly man. In town, nobody knew how much money he had.

[[story]]
Ronald Read had worked at a gas station in Brattleboro, Vermont, and later as a janitor. He was the first in his family to finish high school. He died in June 2014, at 92, and most people around him had no idea what he owned.

After his death it came out that his estate was worth about $8 million. According to NBC News, Read's visible life gave nothing away. He wore worn flannel shirts, gathered fallen branches to heat his home and drove a second-hand Toyota Yaris. His one indulgence was breakfast at a local coffee shop. He read the Wall Street Journal every day and invested in stocks.

His lawyer, Laurie Rowell, told NBC: "Investing and cutting wood, he was good at both of them." Rowell also said: "You'd never know the man was a millionaire." Rowell added that on his last visit he had parked far from the office, where there were no meters, to save the coins.

In his will he left $4.8 million to Brattleboro Memorial Hospital, founded in 1904, and $1.2 million to Brooks Memorial Library, founded in 1886. These were the largest bequests either institution had ever received. So the town's hospital and library got the biggest gift in their history from a man who had worked at a gas station and cleaned buildings.

Look at the logic of that life. Two habits ran side by side: keeping spending small and putting what was left to work in stocks. Low spending enlarged the share that could go into investments. Reading the paper backed the investing with knowledge, because a person who reads about what they own tends to decide better. None of it was flashy on its own, but across years it stacked up.

An outsider sees only the spending and cannot see the investing. That is why judging how much someone has saved from how much they spend often misleads. In Read's case, the distance between the visible life and the real fortune was the town's biggest surprise.

The second cure in Clason's book is to control your expenses. Read's story comes from real life and is nowhere in the book, yet it carries the same logic: a plain-looking life can hide a large accumulation.

[[lessons]]
$$**Keep your spending small and get to know what you put your money into by reading about it.** Read ate breakfast at a coffee shop and drove a second-hand car, yet every day he read the newspaper and followed his investments. You can go through one month of your spending, cut the item that gives you the least pleasure, and put that amount somewhere you understand. Together the two habits build a quiet but large accumulation over time and let you decide from knowledge instead of fear. Picking a single item for next month is the easiest and least painful way to start.$$

[[open]]
A fund reports its capital at the end of July: 4.1 billion dollars. A month later the same line reads 2.3 billion. Nothing was stolen and nothing was hidden. The 1.8 billion simply drained away in thirty-one days, from positions spread across markets that were supposed to have little to do with one another.

[[story]]
Roger Lowenstein's *When Genius Failed* follows the rise and fall of Long-Term Capital Management, or LTCM, and August 1998 is the month the fall picked up speed.

Start with what the fund held. About 80 percent of its balance-sheet positions were in government bonds of the G-7 countries. Much of its trading was what the 1999 report of the President's Working Group on Financial Markets calls convergence trading: buy corporate debt, sell government securities, and wait for the gap between the two prices to close. The fund had more than 60,000 trades on its books.

On 17 August 1998 Russia devalued the ruble and declared a moratorium on its debt. Investors ran from risk and toward liquidity, a "flight to quality" in the report's words. Risk spreads and liquidity premiums rose sharply in markets around the world. For a fund betting that spreads would narrow, that was exactly the wrong direction.

The numbers show how fast it went. On 31 July LTCM held 4.1 billion dollars in capital, about 15 percent less than at the start of the year. In August alone it lost another 1.8 billion. Capital fell to 2.3 billion, and the loss for the year passed 50 percent. A letter dated 2 September told investors about the losses and said the fund was looking for fresh capital. Through the first two weeks of September the losses continued, and the fund's condition became one of the main topics of conversation in the markets.

The report is blunt about why the models failed. Losses in individual markets greatly exceeded what risk models, built on data from calmer periods, said was probable. The models assumed that prices in different markets would move with little correlation. Instead, the shock hit many markets at once, and a portfolio that looked well diversified turned out to be less diversified than assumed.

Liquidity made it worse. The models implicitly assumed that positions could be reduced quickly. In a market that had gone thin, every sale cost more losses, and LTCM's positions were so large in some markets that getting out was slow by nature.

Diversification works only while the pieces pull in different directions. In August 1998 they pulled in one. No single bet sank the fund; what hurt was the shared assumption underneath all of them.

[[lessons]]
$$**Test your diversification against the day everything goes wrong together.** A team, a client list or a set of income sources can look independent in normal times and still share one weak point, such as one big customer, one platform or one supplier. Ordinary days show you the separation, while a crisis shows you the link, so ask a concrete question: if my backups fail for the same reason, what is left? Answering it now, while nothing is burning, costs you one honest conversation and lets you add a truly separate backup, one that does not share the weak point, before you need it.$$

[[story]]
Alain de Botton gives Socrates the chapter on unpopularity in *The Consolations of Philosophy*, and reviewers say it centres on the death sentence a court handed him. The trial took place in Athens in 399 BC. Socrates stood accused of impiety and of corrupting the young, and our closest account of what he said comes from Plato's *Apology*.

The vote went against him, but only just. In his own words, had thirty votes gone the other way he would have walked free. The prosecution asked for death, and the custom was that Socrates now name a counter-penalty.

He named a reward. He described himself as a poor man who had spent his life urging Athenians to look after their souls before their private interests, and he said the fitting return was meals in the Prytaneum, where the city fed Olympic victors. Those men, he told the jury, give you the appearance of happiness, and I give you the reality. Only then did he mention money: since he had none, he proposed a fine of one mina, and his friends Plato, Crito, Critobulus and Apollodorus asked him to say thirty, which they would guarantee.

The jury voted for death. Socrates told them he felt no grief and was surprised only that the margin was so narrow, because he had expected to lose by far more. The sentence was carried out when he drank hemlock in prison.

What stands out is how many exits he declined. A defendant could weep, beg and bring his children into court to soften the jury. Socrates had said plainly that he would not, and at the moment of sentencing he still would not trim what he believed. He kept repeating the one conviction that had made him unpopular: that a person should look to virtue and wisdom before comfort, and that a life never examined is not worth living.

The room stayed against him, yet the account Plato left shows a man who spoke at the end exactly as he had spoken through the years, with the same questions, the same calm and the same refusal to buy a better verdict with a worse version of himself.

[[lessons]]
$$**Keep saying what you think when the room goes cold.** Unpopularity usually arrives in small doses: a proposal that gets no support at the team meeting, an opinion at a family dinner that makes everyone go quiet. The pull in those moments is to soften the view until it pleases, and the cost is that you can no longer tell which of your words you still stand behind. Socrates shows that you can hold a plain position without raising your voice, and that being disliked for something you believe is easier to live with than being liked for something you said to be liked.$$

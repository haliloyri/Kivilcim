Someone hands you a blank page and asks for 101 goals. Not three, not ten: a hundred and one. There's also a rule: you're not allowed to stop and ask whether any of them are actually possible. Your first reaction is probably that nobody has that many goals. Your second is that this sounds like a party game. Hold on to both reactions. The book this comes from thinks they're exactly the point.

[[story]]
The book is *The Power of Focus*, written by Jack Canfield, Mark Victor Hansen and Les Hewitt and published in 2000. Its argument is simple. If you want to live a focused, successful life, you need clearly written goals underneath it. A vague intention you keep in your head is not a goal. The authors' view is that it usually stays a wish.

That sounds obvious until you check yourself. Most of us would say we know what we want. Ask us, and we can talk about the job, the trip, the thing we keep meaning to learn. But ask where it's written, and the answer is usually nowhere. The book says this is where goals that happen part ways with goals that stay dreams. It's not a gap in talent or luck. It's a gap between the head and the page.

So the authors lean on paper, and they lean on it hard. One exercise associated with them is writing a long list of goals, around 101 of them, before judging whether any single item is realistic. Canfield has described keeping a list like this himself.

Picture actually doing it. The first stretch is easy. You write the goals everyone writes: get fitter, save more, travel somewhere. Maybe fifteen or twenty come out without much effort. Then the pen slows down. You've said the respectable things and the list isn't close to finished. A voice starts up:

> *That's all I've got.*

But the page isn't full, so you keep going. You start writing things you'd normally be embarrassed to say out loud. You write the ambitions you dropped years ago because they seemed unlikely. You write the small, specific wants you never counted as "real" goals.

##The authors suggest this is where the deeper, more genuine desires tend to appear: not at the top of the list, but far down it, after the obvious ones run out.##

That's the first half of the logic. Asking for so many goals forces you past the answers you give by reflex. The second half is about what the writing itself does. In the book's account, putting a goal on paper turns a loose wish into something you feel committed to. And commitment is what gets you moving. A thought can drift away without costing you anything. Once a sentence is written down, it looks back at you.

The authors don't dress this up as science. They present written, reviewed goals as far more likely to be achieved than goals you never write down, and they say this comes from their own experience, not from a cited study. It's a practitioner's claim. Whether you trust it depends on something you can test yourself: what happens when you stop carrying your goals around in your head and put them somewhere you'll see them again.

That's why the odd rule matters. You write first and ask "is this realistic?" later. The oversized number isn't a target to hit perfectly. It's there to keep you writing long enough to reach the goals that are really yours.

[[lessons]]
$$**The page is a contract with yourself.** The book's central move is getting goals out of your head and onto paper. The authors' reasoning is that a written goal stops being "something I'd like" and becomes "something I've committed to." The wish is the same, but once you can see it in ink, you feel far more obliged to act on it.$$

$$**The real goals sit deep in the list.** The first dozen items come easily and sound like everyone else's list. The point of asking for around 101 is that you run out of those. When the pen slows and you keep going anyway, the authors suggest that's where the honest wants start to show up. Quantity is how you get to honesty.$$

$$**Write first, judge later.** The rule is that you don't ask whether a goal is realistic while you're listing. Self-censorship works fast. It can cancel an ambition before it has even become a sentence. If you put off the judging, those ambitions reach the page, and you can weigh them there in the open.$$

[[reflect]]
- Which of your goals right now exist only in your head, never written down anywhere?
- Think back to the moment the pen slows down. If you kept listing past the obvious goals, what do you suspect would come out?
- Is there a goal you've quietly written off as unrealistic without ever giving it a single line on paper?
- Where would a written list actually have to live for you to see it again?

In a letter, a man asks for one thing: a little cheese. He wants it so that on the day he feels like holding a feast, he will have the means. The writer is Epicurus, and his everyday diet, he says in the same letters, is water and plain bread.

[[story]]
Alain de Botton gives Epicurus the chapter on not having enough money in *The Consolations of Philosophy*. One reviewer calls it the book's freshest chapter, with simple food, water and friendship at its centre. The ancient biographer Diogenes Laertius, writing centuries after Epicurus died, supplies the details of how the man actually lived.

Epicurus taught in Athens, in a garden. According to Diogenes, who cites a chronicler named Apollodorus, he bought it for eighty minae. Friends came to him there from many places and lived beside him. Greece was passing through hard times, yet Epicurus stayed in his own city and left only two or three times, to visit friends in Ionia.

Diogenes also reports, from a writer called Diocles, how the household ate and drank. They were content with a small cup of light wine, and the rest of their drink was water. Epicurus says in his own letters, as Diogenes quotes them, that water and plain bread are enough for him, and then comes the request: send me some Cytherean cheese, so that if I wish to have a feast I may have the means.

That sentence holds the whole method. A feast, for him, was a little cheese beside the bread. Because his daily needs were so low, a modest treat counted as a real pleasure, and it was one he had chosen himself rather than one that wealth had delivered in bulk.

There is a detail about money as well. Diogenes says Epicurus would not let his followers pool their property, as the followers of Pythagoras did. His reasoning, as Diogenes gives it, was that such an arrangement suits people who distrust one another, and people who distrust one another are not friends. The garden therefore had no common purse. Each person kept what was theirs, and the bond between them rested on trust.

Put it all together and the picture is quite plain: a garden, bread and water, one treat on request, and friends who stayed because they wanted to. Diogenes adds that this was the man who taught that pleasure is the chief good, and the household he ran asked very little of any purse.

[[lessons]]
$$**Set your baseline low enough that one small treat feels like a feast.** Someone who buys a coffee every morning on the way to work soon stops tasting it, while someone who drinks coffee at home most days and goes out for one on Saturday tastes every sip. Pleasure that arrives on demand wears out, and pleasure that you plan and wait for keeps its colour. Choose one modest luxury and turn it into a deliberate occasion rather than a habit, and you may find that a smaller budget stops feeling like a loss and starts to feel like a choice you made on purpose.$$

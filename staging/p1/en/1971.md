A surgeon drives a nail through the middle of a freshly amputated hand and hangs a weight from it. At a pull well below the weight of a grown man, the skin between the fingers tears. The picture that painters had repeated for centuries has just failed its first physical test.

[[story]]
Mary Roach's *Stiff* devotes a chapter to cadaver experiments in the study of crucifixion. The first name in it is Pierre Barbet, a French surgeon who published his results in a 1937 book, *Les Cinq Plaies du Christ*. This story is about his experiments and the argument they started, not about the cloth that inspired them. He took the wounds on the Shroud of Turin as his guide, and the marks at its wrists troubled the usual picture.

In the usual picture, the nails go through the palms. Barbet tested that on freshly amputated hands. When he drove a nail through the middle of a palm and loaded it, the skin between the fingers tore at a pull of about 88 pounds, according to the account of his work by the pathologist Frederick Zugibe. With the arms stretched at about 68 degrees from the upright, his calculation put the pull on each hand above the weight of the whole body. A palm could not hold a man.

So he looked for another path. He found a gap among the small bones of the wrist, known as Destot's space, where a nail could pass and be gripped by the surrounding bones. A nail there would carry the full weight. He also claimed the nail would sever half to two thirds of the median nerve, which Zugibe disputed on the grounds that the nerve does not pass through Destot's space. His book includes a photograph of a cadaver nailed to a cross, and he had a sculptor make a crucifix to his specifications.

Then his own work was tested. Zugibe repeated and extended the experiments in the 1970s and 1980s, and described them in *The Cross and the Shroud*, which appeared in 1981 and in a second edition in 1988. In one test he nailed the severed arms of fresh cadavers at an angle through the palm and found that the hand could hold several hundred pounds. He also argued that Destot's space lies on the little-finger side of the hand, while the wound on the Shroud sits on the thumb side, and he called Barbet's suffocation theory of death completely untenable.

Zugibe took the same approach, with real tissue and real weights, and reached different conclusions. That is a normal fate for a good measurement. Barbet's pulling weights overturned a comfortable assumption, and Zugibe's later arm tests overturned some of Barbet's. Each of them put a number on a question that had been settled by pictures alone.

[[lessons]]
$$**Put an accepted picture to a physical test, and expect your own test to be tested.** A shop owner who assumes the old shelves will carry next season's stock can load one shelf with the real weight on a quiet evening and see what it does before she orders anything. If the shelf bends, she has saved a collapse. If a colleague later loads it from another angle and gets a different answer, her number was still useful, because now there are two measurements to compare instead of two opinions. Whatever you are sure of at work, a quick real test turns that confidence into something others can check.$$

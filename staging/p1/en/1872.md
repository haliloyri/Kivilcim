Round a dinner table sit men from rival banks. The host is a trader at Deutsche Bank. What is on the table is not a trade but a rule: one contract that everybody can buy and sell in the same words.

[[story]]
Greg Lippmann is one of the livelier figures in *The Big Short*. He is a Deutsche Bank trader, aged 41 in 2010, and he believes the housing market will fall. Believing is not enough, though. To act on it he needs a product that can be bought and sold, because if everyone uses a different contract then the price, the deal and the trust all stay vague.

In early 2005 Lippmann hosts a dinner. At the table are men from Goldman Sachs, Bear Stearns, Citi and JPMorgan, representatives of firms that are rivals and also trade with each other every day. The purpose is to agree on a common contract standard for credit default swaps on subprime mortgage bonds.

Lippmann is credited with producing that standardized swap. With a standard in place, protection bought at one bank can be priced and passed on at another far more easily. Markets very often begin with small agreements like this one, made by people who could just as easily have gone on competing over wording for years. Once everybody defines a thing in the same way, that thing can be traded, and where the definitions differ, every deal turns into its own negotiation.

By autumn 2005 Lippmann is betting heavily on a fall in housing. He prepared the ground first and then played on it. In the period that followed, he also pitched the idea to hedge funds.

The order of events is the interesting part: common ground first, then the bet. In a world where rival banks each insisted on their own terms, finding a single buyer would have been hard, since every contract would need negotiating from scratch. At first the dinner looks like an ordinary business dinner. Bringing rivals to one table and getting them to accept the same contract gave Lippmann the arena for his own bet, and it also opened him a customer base: because others could use the same contract, the idea he was selling became easy to trade. Building a structure that serves other people as well is what keeps your idea standing.

[[lessons]]
$$**If your idea depends on other people playing by the same rules, set the shared rules first.** When a team builds a new workflow or an industry wants a new standard, even the best idea cannot work without a common language. Lippmann's dinner opened the way for swaps to be written the same at every bank. Call everyone concerned to the table, write the rule together, and only then make your own move. That way your idea stops being only yours and becomes part of a shared structure that grows stronger as others use it.$$

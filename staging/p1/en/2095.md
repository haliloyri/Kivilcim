Two days after breaking twelve ribs in a bike accident, a chief executive boarded a flight to Japan. It was one of more than thirty trips he made there on the same errand. He was looking for someone to put money into a startup that, as it turned out, would not survive.

[[story]]
Before Intuit, before the Apple board, Bill Campbell ran a company that failed. The company was GO, which built software for computers you write on with a pen. According to Fortune's 2008 profile, the venture capitalist John Doerr suggested Campbell as the "professional" chief executive for the young firm, and GO announced his appointment effective January 1991. The founder, Jerry Kaplan, later said Campbell had taught him that a rational argument does not always motivate people.

The money was the constant problem. GO burned through about $75 million of venture funding, and Campbell kept looking for another investor. Fortune reports that he flew to Japan more than thirty times in that search, once only two days after breaking twelve ribs. That detail rests on a single account, but it fits everything else described about how he worked.

The product never found its market, even though its PenPoint software ran on tablets from IBM, NCR and AT&T. GO had spun its hardware side off as a separate company, EO, in 1991. In 1993 AT&T, which had bought EO, also agreed to buy GO itself. Two weeks after the deal closed, AT&T cancelled the processor line the whole plan depended on, and GO closed in July 1994. Kaplan said the company had generated no meaningful sales.

Campbell did not choose the ending, but he did choose how it was handled. Fortune says he arranged the sale to AT&T in a way that saved some jobs. Doerr described his priority as making sure people left the venture with dignity. A GO manager, Danny Shader, put it this way: "Bill got everybody to believe that they were in it for something bigger than themselves."

That belief is what the alumni list hints at. GO alumni went on to lead VeriSign, Netscape and LucasArts, according to Fortune. Nobody can say how much of that traces back to Campbell, and the claim here is smaller: a company with almost no sales still produced people other companies wanted to put in charge.

The usual way to judge a chief executive is revenue, and GO produced almost none. Judged by the one measure Doerr named, people leaving with their dignity, the ending looks very different from the balance sheet.

[[lessons]]
$$**When you cannot save the project, put your effort into how it ends.** Failing companies, cancelled products and closed teams still leave people with a memory of how they were treated, and that memory travels to their next job and their next boss. Make the end of a project as deliberate as its start: tell people early, secure what you can for them, and give them a reason to say the work mattered, and make sure those who stay behind see how you treated those who left. What you get is a team you can still call on years later, which is worth more than a spotless record.$$

Look at a typical to-do list. Call the dentist. Finish the report. Go for a run. Plan the weekend with the kids. Every item is real, and every item matters. But look again: not one of them has a time next to it. So when, exactly, is any of it going to happen?

[[story]]
In *Indistractable*, Nir Eyal makes a claim that sounds almost too simple. You can't call something a distraction, he argues, unless you know what it is distracting you from.

Think about that for a second. If your afternoon has no plan, then scrolling a feed isn't really pulling you away from anything. There is nothing on the other side to be pulled away from. So before you can fight distraction, you have to decide what you meant to be doing instead.

This is where Eyal takes aim at the to-do list. A list tells you what to do. It doesn't tell you when. Items drift from day to day because nothing in the list gives them a place in time.

His alternative is timeboxing. You decide in advance what you'll do and when you'll do it, and you put it on the calendar. The task stops being a wish and becomes an appointment.

Eyal frames this as "turning values into time." Your calendar, in his view, should reflect what you actually care about. If something matters to you and never appears there, the calendar is quietly telling a different story about your priorities.

To make this concrete, he splits life into three domains: you, relationships, and work. And he orders them on purpose. Time for yourself gets scheduled first, things like sleep, exercise and reflection. His reasoning is that without you, the other two domains suffer.

Relationships come next. Time with family and friends, he argues, should also go on the calendar, rather than being left to whatever hours happen to be left over.

##Most people plan their work carefully and hope that time for themselves and the people they love will simply appear, and in Eyal's view it rarely does.##

Then there is the question of what counts as success. Here Eyal sets a surprisingly forgiving bar. If you did what you planned, for the time you planned, you succeeded. It doesn't matter whether the report was finished or the run was fast. The measure is whether you showed up to the box you set.

And the schedule isn't fixed. You revise it as you learn how long things take and what you really need. He also recommends reviewing the calendar with others, a regular "schedule sync" with your manager and your family, so that the plan reflects shared priorities, not only your own guesses.

The result is a calendar that works less like a record of other people's requests and more like a statement of what you've decided matters.

[[lessons]]
$$**Planning comes before resisting.** A distraction only exists relative to a plan. Without a timeboxed afternoon, there's nothing to measure a detour against. That's why Eyal starts with the calendar instead of with blockers or willpower: you need to know what "traction" looks like before you can spot what pulls you off it.$$

$$**Leftover time isn't a plan.** Eyal schedules the "you" domain first, then relationships, then work. It's a deliberate reversal of how most weeks get built. Sleep, exercise and an evening with family usually get whatever the job leaves behind. Giving them a slot is how they stop being the first thing sacrificed.$$

$$**Success means showing up to the box.** Doing what you planned, when you planned it, counts as a win even if the task isn't finished. That standard makes the calendar something you can keep, and it gives you honest information for revising next week's plan.$$

A hedge fund's risk list holds some odd questions. One asks what happens if a terrorist sets off a nuclear bomb in New York harbor. The answer is surprisingly ordinary: the firm's prime broker says duplicates of the records are safe in a vault called Iron Mountain.

[[story]]
Edward O. Thorp, author of *A Man for All Markets*, described how Princeton Newport Partners handled risk in a 2003 article, and this account follows it. At first the firm measured risk position by position, for individual warrant and convertible hedges. By 1973 the approach covered the whole portfolio. Thorp separates two kinds of risk: "local" risk is the normal fluctuation of prices, while "global" risk is a sudden large, even catastrophic, jump.

To manage local risk, in 1973 and 1974 they studied the terms of the power-series expansion of the Black–Scholes option formula: delta, gamma (which they called curvature) and others the financial community would later name theta, vega and rho. For global risk they asked a different question: how would the portfolio's value change if the market index, the yield curve or volatility moved by specified percentages? They asked extreme questions on purpose. What if a terrorist exploded a nuclear bomb in New York harbor? Their prime broker, Goldman Sachs, assured them duplicates of their records were safe in Iron Mountain.

What if a gigantic earthquake hit California or Japan? What if Treasury bill yields went from 7 percent to 15 percent? A couple of years later, in 1981, they hit 14 percent. What if the market dropped 25 percent in a day, twice the worst day ever? Ten years later, in October 1987, it dropped 23 percent in a day. By Thorp's account, they broke even on the day and were up slightly for the month.

The rule was simple: limit global risk to acceptable levels, and manage local risks so as to stay close to market neutral. Thorp writes that they were well aware of two fallacies. One is assuming that previous historical limits on financial variables will hold in the future. The other is that the mathematically convenient lognormal model for stock prices substantially underestimates the probability of extreme moves. When he reviews any investment, he still asks two questions: "What are the factor exposures?" and "What are the risks from extreme events?"

[[lessons]]
$$**Write down the extreme questions in advance and put a limit on each.** A person running a small workshop can list what would happen if the biggest client left or the rent jumped, and decide for each how much cushion the account should still hold. When bad news arrives, she then follows a plan she wrote in a calm hour instead of improvising in a panic. The value lies less in predicting the disaster than in knowing beforehand how far its damage can reach and whether the business would still be standing afterwards. Each limit also shows which risks you choose to carry.$$

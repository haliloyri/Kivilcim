The name on the badge says Barb, and the woman wearing it has noticed that Barb is a little meaner and sneakier than she is. The badge has been pinned to her shirt since the first morning in a big-box store in Minneapolis. Before that, a cup, a form and a stack of questions had to be passed.

[[story]]
The last of the six jobs in Barbara Ehrenreich's *Nickel and Dimed* was at a Wal-Mart in Minneapolis. The pay started at $7 an hour, and according to a review in The Nation it rose to $7.75 after two years at that store. Getting hired took more than a handshake. Every interview, she said in a later talk, began with a drug test.

Then came a personality test. In a university talk she recalled that it asked whether she had stolen money in the past year and whether work is easier when you are slightly high. She called the drug test "a little ritual of humiliation" and said such tests do not cut absenteeism or raise productivity. A Salon review says she wrote that the real message of such tests was: "You will have no secrets from us."

The shop floor had its own vocabulary. Workers were called associates, customers were called guests, and a supervisor was a servant leader. Team meetings sometimes ended with a Wal-Mart cheer. The name on her badge made her Barb, and she felt that Barb was meaner and slyer than she was.

The rule that mattered most was time. Chatting with a coworker or a customer counted as "time theft", and a fifteen-minute break had to be punched out on the clock. Management's priorities, wrote the reviewer in The Nation, included wiping out time theft, mostly by enforcing a strict ban on talking and by watching smoke breaks.

She also said, in her talk, that managers at Wal-Mart sometimes told workers to clock out and then come back to the floor to finish a task: "Go punch out and then come back to the floor. I have a lot for you to do." She added that the company faced lawsuits in 26 states over unpaid overtime. These are her claims and the lawsuits were allegations, not verdicts.

Put the pieces in order and a pattern shows. The tests start by assuming that applicants might steal, use drugs or lie. The rules on the floor treat a minute of talk as a theft. The punch clock then claims minutes in the opposite direction. Time is guarded fiercely when the worker might take it and spent freely when the company wants it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you add a check or a rule, read the message it sends to everyone who passes through it.** A drug test, a no-talking rule or a sign-off on every small expense tells every honest person in the building that she is suspected before she has done anything. In a team of your own, a rule that was meant for one careless person quietly becomes the daily experience of everyone else. Ask what the rule assumes about people, and if there is a lighter way to deal with the rare problem, use it and keep the trust for the ordinary days.$$

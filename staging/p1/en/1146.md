Some people answer every call, accept every request and cover every gap. They tell themselves it's because they care. Then one day they find they can't care about anything anymore. Nedra Glover Tawwab, a therapist, sees this all the time, and she has a name for the real problem.

[[story]]
Nedra Glover Tawwab has spent years as a therapist listening to people describe why they're unhappy. In *Set Boundaries, Find Peace*, she writes that the problems they bring in often sound different on the surface. A difficult family. A job that swallows every evening. A friend who always needs something. A relationship that feels one-sided. But underneath, again and again, she finds the same issue. It's a problem with boundaries.

She defines boundaries simply. They are the expectations and needs that help you feel safe and comfortable in your relationships. They tell other people what you can and can't give, and how you want to be treated.

People who struggle with boundaries are rarely bad or selfish people. Often it's the opposite. They're kind, reliable and eager to help. They say yes because they don't want to let anyone down. They take the extra shift, answer the late-night message, lend the money, listen to the same complaint for the tenth time. Saying no feels harsh. It feels like proof they don't care enough.

##But a yes that should have been a no doesn't disappear. It gets paid for later.##

Tawwab lists the warning signs that you need healthier boundaries. You feel overwhelmed. You feel resentful toward people who ask you for help. You avoid calls, messages or certain people because you can't face another request. And eventually, you feel burned out.

That last sign is the one that surprises people. Burnout looks like a work problem, or a stress problem. Tawwab sees it very often as a boundary problem. When you can't say no, there's no limit on what others can take from you. Your time, your energy and your patience get spent until there's nothing left, including for the people you most want to help.

This is why she insists that setting boundaries is not selfish. It's a form of self-care, and it's what makes caring for others sustainable. A no today is often what protects your ability to say a real, wholehearted yes tomorrow.

Think of the instruction on every plane: put on your own oxygen mask before helping others. It isn't a lesson in selfishness. It's the only way you stay conscious long enough to help anyone at all.

[[lessons]]
$$**Resentment is a signal, not a character flaw.** Tawwab lists feeling resentful toward people who ask for help as a key sign of missing boundaries. Instead of feeling guilty about the resentment, you can treat it as information. It usually points exactly to the place where you've been saying yes when you meant no.$$

$$**Boundaries are self-care.** The kind, reliable people who struggle most with no often believe limits would make them selfish. Tawwab argues the reverse. Boundaries protect your energy and make your care sustainable, so you can keep showing up for people over the long term instead of in bursts before collapse.$$

$$**Endless yeses end in a bigger no.** Burnout, in Tawwab's view, is often where people-pleasing leads. If you never set limits, exhaustion eventually sets them for you, usually all at once and at the worst time. A small, clear no now is far kinder than a total collapse later.$$

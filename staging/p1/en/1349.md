The chapter is covered in yellow highlighter. You have read it twice, maybe three times, and every line now looks familiar. You could almost predict the next sentence. It feels like knowing. Now close the book and try to explain the main idea to someone across the table. That small test is where a lot of confident studying quietly falls apart.

[[story]]
In *Make It Stick*, published in 2014, Peter C. Brown, Henry L. Roediger III and Mark A. McDaniel start from a plain observation about how students study. When asked about their strategies, most name rereading as their main one. Go over the notes again. Read the chapter one more time.

It is easy to see why. Rereading is comfortable, it feels productive, and it never makes you look bad. The trouble, the authors report, is that it often gives little lasting benefit.

The reason is fluency. Each pass through a text makes it feel smoother and more familiar. The words come easily, the arguments seem obvious, the page feels like home. Learners take that familiarity as a sign of mastery. The book gives this mistake a name: an **illusion of knowing**.

The illusion works because two different skills get confused. Recognising material while it is right in front of you is easy. Retrieving it from memory with no cues, no page, no highlighted line to prompt you, is much harder. Rereading trains the first skill. Exams, meetings and real conversations usually ask for the second.

##The feeling of familiarity is not the same as being able to recall.##

The alternative the authors champion is retrieval practice. Instead of looking at the material again, you try to pull it out of memory. You quiz yourself. You answer questions with the book closed. You take a short, low-stakes test that counts for nothing.

This feels worse. Retrieval is effortful, and it is often embarrassing, because it immediately shows the parts you cannot remember. But that is exactly what makes it useful. The effort of recalling strengthens the memory, and the blanks you hit show you precisely what you do not yet know. Rereading hides those gaps behind a comfortable sense of familiarity. Retrieval puts them on the table.

The book backs this with classroom research. In studies it describes, material that had been covered by frequent low-stakes quizzes was remembered better on later exams than material that had only been reviewed. The quizzes were not there to grade anyone. They were there to make students reach for what they had learned.

So the strategy that feels most productive, reading the page again, gives the least. The one that feels harder and exposes your weak spots is the one that makes learning last.

[[lessons]]
$$**Familiar is not the same as known.** After a few rereadings, a text feels fluent, and that fluency is easy to mistake for mastery. The book calls this an illusion of knowing. The warning sign is subtle: you feel sure you know something only while it is in front of you. The real question is what you can produce once the page is gone. That is the version of knowing a meeting, an exam or a colleague's question will actually ask of you.$$

$$**Recall does two jobs at once.** When you quiz yourself with the book closed, the effort strengthens the memory, and the blanks show you what to revisit. Rereading can do neither. That is why the low-stakes quizzes in the classroom research beat simple review on later exams. The discomfort of not remembering is information, not a sign that you are bad at the subject.$$

A small plane drops low over the roofs of an Arkansas town, turns, and comes back for another pass. The pilot is not lost and not showing off. He is studying the lay of the land: the streets, the roads running in and the edges of a town he may build in.

[[story]]
Sam Walton's problem was distance. According to the Encyclopedia of Arkansas, he learned to fly to service his several stores in isolated small towns. His 1992 memoir *Made in America*, written with John Huey, comes back to the plane again and again, and published summaries place his first one in the 1950s.

Then the plane turned out to have a second use. Walton began flying over small towns in Arkansas and Kansas, looking for places where a store might work. TIME described his method in one line: he would buzz towns in his low-flying airplane, studying the lay of the land. He kept the habit for the rest of his life.

Picture what that gives a retailer. A map tells you a town's name and its population on paper. A low pass shows you how the houses sit around the square, how many roads feed into it and where the open ground is. The pilot sees in a few minutes what a visitor on foot would take a day to piece together. He sees it as one picture he can set beside the next town, and the next, and the one after that.

The result he got was real. The Encyclopedia of Arkansas says that the first of his stores chosen from aerial scouting stood outside Fort Leonard Wood, Missouri, and that it grossed two million dollars in its first year. Fort Leonard Wood is a US Army installation in Missouri, so that store sat beside a large community of soldiers and their families. That is one store, reported by one source, so it proves nothing about the method on its own. It is a good illustration of what Walton was after: not a better answer from a report, but a first-hand look at the ground.

His chain at the time was small. By the end of the 1950s he had only about fifteen stores, which together did around $1.4 million in sales in 1960. When a company that small chooses its next site, a mistake in one town is felt across the whole business. Each site was a bet he had to place with very little in hand.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you decide about a place, a person or a market, go and look at the ground yourself.** A report compresses a situation into the shape its author expected, while your own pass over it shows the things nobody thought to measure. You do not need a plane, since a visit to the customer's workplace, a walk through the warehouse or an afternoon on the support line does the same job. Do it before the decision, not after, and write down what surprised you, since the surprises are exactly what the summary left out, and let at least one change your plan.$$

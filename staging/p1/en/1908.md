Snow is falling outside a record store, two days before Christmas. A young man steps out onto the sidewalk with a shopping bag in his hand. Soon his head will go through a windshield, and he will stop breathing on the side of the road.

[[story]]
Matthew Dicks has told the story of that day, and in *Storyworthy* he uses it to teach something about big events. He was driving after leaving the record store when his car hit a Mercedes head-on. In his telling, his head went through the windshield, his bottom row of teeth was pushed to the back of his mouth, and his legs were badly hurt. He stopped breathing. Paramedics performed CPR, and he was brought back before he reached the hospital.

That is a story with every ingredient a listener could want: snow, Christmas, a head-on collision, a man who dies and returns. Dicks says the crash is not what the story is about. It only delivers him to the emergency room, where, according to Dicks, he is alone.

The story begins to matter in the waiting room. Dicks's friends fill it and cheer for him. By his own account, the story is about "my friends standing in the place of my family when I need them most."

Dicks says audiences do not cry at the crash. They cry in the waiting room. A collision at speed is something very few listeners have lived through, and so it slides past them. A room full of friends cheering is something they can imagine being on either side of.

Dicks calls this a "big story" and says big stories are the hardest to tell, because their biggest moments are the hardest for an audience to relate to. The temptation is to spend your time on the part that sounds most impressive. The skill is to see that the impressive part is only the way in, and to give most of the time to the small human scene on the other side.

There is a plain logic to this. Few listeners have been in a head-on collision, so they watch it from outside, the way you watch a film. Everyone has wondered who would come for them on the worst day, or has been the one who came. The crash gives the story its stakes, but the waiting room gives listeners somewhere to stand.

The structure is worth borrowing. The accident can be told in a few sentences, because its only job is to get the narrator to the emergency room. The friends are what the story is for.

[[lessons]]
$$**When your event is huge, give your listener the small scene they can stand in.** Dicks's crash is far from any listener's experience, but a waiting room full of friends is not, and that is where audiences cry. The same holds when you describe a near-miss at work, a family crisis or a hard year: the dramatic fact gets attention, and the human detail earns the connection. Ask who was in the room with you, what they did, and what that told you about your own life, and let that carry the story while the headline event shrinks to a sentence or two.$$

A small-town telephone company can ring every one of its own customers, and every plug on its switchboard finds its socket. Then a customer asks for a cousin two states away, and the call dies at the edge of the company's wires. Those wires belong to a rival, and the rival has no intention of lending them.

[[story]]
The telephone chapter of Tim Wu's *The Master Switch* belongs to Theodore Vail, who had run AT&T since 1907 and wanted one system under one policy. The Bell system owned the longest long-distance network in the country, and it refused to connect that network to the independent telephone companies. For an independent, this was close to a death sentence, because a phone company that cannot reach beyond its own town has little left to sell.

Complaints piled up, and they caught the attention of the Justice Department. An antitrust suit against AT&T looked possible. Talks went on through 1913, and on 19 December of that year AT&T vice president Nathan Kingsbury sent a letter to Attorney General James McReynolds. History remembers it as the Kingsbury Commitment.

The letter made three promises. AT&T would give up its controlling interest in Western Union. It would provide long-distance connections to independent exchanges, under certain conditions. And it would not buy further companies if the Interstate Commerce Commission objected.

On paper this was a real retreat. Leaving Western Union closed the road to holding telephone and telegraph together, and long-distance access gave independents room to breathe. According to published accounts, Justice Department officials thought it gave the government more than a court would have.

Read the terms again and a different picture appears. AT&T had not agreed to connect its local service with other local companies. Western Electric, Bell's manufacturing arm, went unmentioned, so independents that wanted to join the long-distance network still had to buy Bell equipment to do it.

The commitment did not rescue competition. It organised the network, and the company that ran its backbone kept running it. In the years that followed the limits on buying companies were loosened, and a law in 1921 let AT&T acquire local systems under the commission's supervision.

That is the pattern worth holding on to. Faced with the threat of a break-up, the company had fixed the most visible complaint with its own hands and left the centre where it was. The rules were written in a letter from the company, not in a courtroom, and each promise in it was one the company could keep without losing its grip on the network.

[[lessons]]
$$**When a concession arrives, look at what it leaves untouched before you count what it gives.** The thing that hurt most loudly is usually the first thing a counterpart gives up, while the real source of their strength sits elsewhere in the deal. If a supplier cuts the price and leaves the lock-in clause exactly as it was, the discount is the visible gain and the clause is the real negotiation. A generous-looking offer can be built entirely from things the giver never needed. Before you sign anything, ask what the text never mentions, because that silence shows you how much you actually won.$$

[[open]]
A graduate student presses another soil sample onto a dish and waits less than a week to see whether anything on it will kill tuberculosis. He has done this ten times already without a result. Years later, when the prize for the discovery is given out, his name is not on it.

[[story]]
Albert Schatz was a PhD student at Rutgers University in New Jersey, working under the soil microbiologist Selman Waksman. He had been drafted into the army, released after a few months, and sent back to the laboratory with a task: find an antibiotic that works against tuberculosis. Each soil plating took less than a week, and he tested what grew against indicator bacteria, among them the tuberculosis bacillus itself and *E. coli*.

His eleventh consecutive plating, made with farm soil from the Rutgers agricultural school, produced a strain of *Streptomyces griseus* that held back the bacteria. In Schatz's own later account the day was 19 October 1943, when he was 23. According to a published account of his work, a second strain came from a swab of a chicken's throat.

He and Waksman named the substance streptomycin. Doctors Feldman and Hinshaw at the Mayo Clinic reported that it was not toxic to a range of animals, and the Mayo team eventually found that it could overcome tuberculosis in humans. The first paper, published in January 1944, listed Schatz first, then Elizabeth Bugie, then Waksman.

The credit did not stay shared. In 1950 Schatz sued Waksman and the Rutgers research foundation, asking for a share of the royalties and recognition for his part. The suit was settled. Waksman then proposed that part of the royalties go to the 26 students and staff who had worked on streptomycin, and Schatz received the largest share.

In 1952 the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine went to Waksman alone. The presentation speech praised his ingenious, systematic and successful studies of soil microbes. A later account by a Waksman associate argues that the prize was justified by the decades of soil research behind the work, and that account comes from one side of the dispute. Waksman had studied soil microbes for years before Schatz came to the laboratory, and that is the core of the argument.

Years later Rutgers students insisted on a plaque naming Schatz as a co-discoverer, and the university gave him its medal. The dispute never produced one agreed account. It left a lasting record, though, of how easily a shared discovery can be remembered as one person's.

[[lessons]]
$$**Agree in writing who did what before the result arrives.** While the work is going on, everyone is generous and nobody wants to be the first to raise credit, so the question gets postponed until success makes it sharp. Say two colleagues who build a report together can spend ten minutes at the start noting who collects the data, who writes it up and whose name goes first, and then update the note whenever roles change, so that nobody has to rely on memory. A short note made in a friendly week is far cheaper than a bitter dispute made after the praise has been handed out.$$

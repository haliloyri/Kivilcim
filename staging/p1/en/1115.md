Two men. One spent his working life fixing cars and mopping floors. The other ran money at one of the biggest firms on Wall Street. By the end, only one of them had quietly become a multimillionaire, and it wasn't the one with the Harvard degree. Morgan Housel opens *The Psychology of Money* with this pair for a reason. Their stories upend what most of us believe about who gets rich.

[[story]]
Start with Ronald Read. He lived in Vermont, and he was the first person in his family to graduate from high school. That was about as far as his formal credentials went.

For roughly 25 years, Read fixed cars at a gas station. After that, he spent about 17 years as a janitor at J.C. Penney, the department store. He lived modestly and kept to himself. Nobody who knew him thought of him as a wealthy man. There was simply nothing about the way he lived that suggested it.

Then, in 2014, Ronald Read died at 92, and people found out what he'd left behind.

> A gas station mechanic. A department store janitor. More than $8 million.

Most of that fortune went to his local library and his local hospital.

So how did he do it? Housel is almost blunt about it. There was no hidden talent and no lucky break. Read saved whatever he could. He put that money into blue-chip stocks. Then he did the hardest part, which was nothing at all. He held those stocks for decades and let compounding do its slow, quiet work.

Now the second man. Richard Fuscone had every advantage we associate with financial success. He was educated at Harvard. He rose to become a senior executive at Merrill Lynch. He did so well that he retired early to focus on philanthropy. If you had to bet on which of these two men would end up rich, you'd pick Fuscone without thinking.

But Fuscone borrowed heavily. He took on large debts to expand and maintain a very large mansion in Greenwich, Connecticut. As long as everything kept going smoothly, that kind of leverage can look manageable. Then came the 2008 financial crisis, and his heavily indebted finances took a severe hit. Fuscone declared personal bankruptcy. The mansion was later sold in foreclosure.

Housel sets the two lives side by side, and the contrast almost feels like a trick. The man with every credential lost his house. The man with none left millions to his town. The lesson Housel draws is that financial outcomes depend more on behavior than on intelligence, education or income.

##In few other fields could a janitor so clearly outperform a top professional. In money, it happens.##

Think about almost any other skilled profession, and the idea sounds absurd. The untrained amateur doesn't usually beat the elite expert. But with money, what you know matters less than what you do, day after day and year after year. Fuscone knew far more about finance than Read ever did. Read simply behaved better with what he had.

[[lessons]]
$$**Behavior beats brilliance.** Fuscone had the Harvard education, the Merrill Lynch career and the expert knowledge. Read had a high school diploma and a mop. Read still came out far ahead, because he kept saving and never put himself at risk of being wiped out. Knowing a lot about money didn't protect Fuscone from making choices that could ruin him. Consistent, unglamorous habits carried Read all the way.$$

$$**Time does the heavy lifting.** Read's paychecks were never large. What turned them into more than $8 million was patience. He bought blue-chip stocks and held them for decades, giving compounding time to work. That patience was also why nobody suspected he was rich. His wealth was mostly money he never spent, and money you don't spend is invisible.$$

$$**A big income is not a safety net.** Fuscone earned enough to retire early, and it still wasn't enough. Heavy borrowing to expand and keep up a huge mansion left him exposed, and when 2008 hit, the leverage turned a rough year into bankruptcy and foreclosure. How much you earn matters far less than whether your finances can survive a bad year.$$

[[reflect]]
- Look at your current money habits. Which ones would still make sense if you kept them up for 30 years, the way Read held his stocks?
- Where are you counting on a good salary or your own cleverness to keep you safe, instead of on steady behavior?
- If a 2008-style shock hit tomorrow, what debts or commitments would put the most pressure on you?
- What part of your wealth is visible to others, and what part is quiet, like Read's?

&&If your finances had to survive a bad year, would you look more like Read or like Fuscone?&&

In one winter a ballclub's best slugger, its centre fielder and its closer all walk out the door and put on other teams' uniforms. The man left running the club has no money to match what they were offered, only three holes that still have to be filled by spring.

[[story]]
Michael Lewis's *Moneyball* centres on the Oakland Athletics, and this story is the winter after their 2001 season. On 5 November 2001 Jason Giambi, Johnny Damon and Jason Isringhausen all became free agents. Giambi went to the New York Yankees, Damon to the Boston Red Sox and Isringhausen to the St. Louis Cardinals. Press reports said Giambi signed with the Yankees for seven years and 120 million dollars.

Oakland could not come near that. Spread over seven years it averages about 17 million dollars a season, a very large slice of what a club like Oakland spent on everyone. According to a Sports Illustrated preview of the 2002 season, the club had the second-lowest payroll in baseball in 2001, about 34 million dollars. ESPN's coverage of the 2002 playoffs later listed the club at 41.9 million, 25th in the league. Buying the same players at the same price was out of the question, so Oakland went another way.

The signings arrived one after another. On 7 December the club got closer Billy Koch from Toronto, and he took over the ninth innings Isringhausen had pitched. On 14 December David Justice came from the New York Mets. On 2 January 2002 the club signed Scott Hatteberg as a free agent, on a one-year deal worth just under a million dollars. Giambi's departure left one more thing behind: the club that loses a player like that receives extra draft picks, and Oakland used one of them on Alabama catcher Jeremy Brown.

Then the results. In 2002 Oakland won 103 games, lost 59 and finished first in the American League West. It was not a clean triumph, though, because the Minnesota Twins beat them three games to two in the Division Series. A club with the 25th-largest payroll could win its division, and that was as far as it went.

What stands out is how the holes were filled. Oakland did not go looking for another expensive star to stand where Giambi had stood. It looked for pieces it could afford: a closer, a veteran bat, a player who came cheaply because of an injury. Each loss was treated as its own job with its own fix. According to Sports Illustrated, Hatteberg and Justice were the answer to Giambi and Damon, while Koch was the answer to the ninth inning. Whether the approach worked has a mixed answer, since the division was won and the pennant was not.

[[lessons]]
$$**When a strong person leaves, split what they did into separate jobs and buy each job where it is cheap.** Say the best technician at a small repair shop quits, and the owner cannot pay for another one at that level. She writes down what that person actually did: diagnosing faults, talking to customers, rushing urgent orders. Then she finds someone less sought after who can do each piece well enough, even if no single hire matches the original, and she sees early which pieces nobody covers yet. Seen that way, the loss stops being one impossible replacement and becomes several jobs you can actually staff.$$

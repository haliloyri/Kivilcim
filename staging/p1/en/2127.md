[[open]]
In a laboratory, two kinds of jewel wasp mate and produce hybrid larvae, and most of those larvae die. Then a researcher raises a new batch with no gut bacteria at all, and they live. The wasps never changed. Only what was inside them did.

[[story]]
This wasp study asks the question that runs through Ed Yong's *I Contain Multitudes*: how much of an animal is really its microbes? In 2013 Robert Brucker and Seth Bordenstein at Vanderbilt University published their result in *Science*. It concerns the jewel wasps of the genus *Nasonia* and one of the oldest puzzles in biology: why closely related species cannot simply merge into one, and why their hybrids so often fail.

The three species differ in how long ago they split. *Nasonia giraulti* and *N. longicornis* diverged about 400,000 years ago, and *N. vitripennis* about a million years ago. According to published accounts of the study, about 8 percent of hybrids between *giraulti* and *longicornis* died, while hybrids between either of them and *vitripennis* died at rates above 90 percent. Dying larvae darken, possibly from melanin made in response to inflammation.

Then came the experiment. The team first compared the gut microbes of four *Nasonia* species, then raised hybrid wasps without microbes by killing the gut bacteria with antibiotics, and many hybrids were rescued. The germ-free hybrids survived about as well as purebred larvae. When those hybrids were given gut microbes taken from ordinary hybrids, their survival dropped sharply. Same wasp genes, with and without microbes: whatever differs in the outcome has to come from the microbes. The pattern also tracked relatedness: for the two recently split species, removing microbes made no noticeable difference.

Bordenstein said the result moved the controversy over the "hologenome," the host together with its microbes, "from an idea to an observed phenomenon." The mechanism is still unknown. Immune genes evolve quickly, so they may change which microbes a host accepts. The darkening of the dying larvae may be one visible sign that the body is reacting to something it cannot tolerate.

The finding has critics. In 2014 Chandler and Turelli accepted that the germ-free data show bacteria contribute to hybrid deaths, but disputed that the bacteria and wasp species had co-adapted, arguing that hybrids may already be weakened and so be more exposed to free-living bacteria. Even Bordenstein called the word "hologenome" controversial. What the study firmly shows is that the microbes are part of why these hybrids die.

[[lessons]]
$$**When a pairing fails, look at the third party both sides depend on.** Two things that work well alone can fail together because of a shared element neither one controls, and blaming the two parties alone hides it. Two teams that merge badly may each have adapted to different reporting tools, and the clash may live in the tools more than in the people. Before you decide that two people or two departments cannot work together, list what each of them quietly relies on and test whether removing that dependency changes the outcome, because the answer can change who you think is at fault.$$

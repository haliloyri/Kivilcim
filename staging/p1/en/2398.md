Iron braziers full of burning coals are carried into a great hall in Florence and set near a wall. The painter wants his colours to dry faster. Instead, the wall starts to run, and the picture he has been working on begins to slide down in streaks.

[[story]]
Walter Isaacson's *Leonardo da Vinci* is the setting for this story of one of the artist's best-known failures, the Battle of Anghiari. In 1503 the Florentine government gave Leonardo a commission for the Hall of the Five Hundred in the Palazzo Vecchio. The subject was a Florentine victory over Milan in 1440, fought near the town of Anghiari.

It was a major job in the most visible room in the city. Leonardo did not want to use fresco. Published accounts say he turned to an ancient source, the Roman writer Pliny, whose description of a method with oil paint and heated wax he tried to copy on the wall. Oil paint dries slowly, which suited his way of working. On a plastered wall of that size, though, it was a gamble nobody had tested.

The trouble came with the drying. To speed it up, braziers were brought in and set against the wall. The heat did not help evenly. According to the accounts, the paint ran and dripped, and the section Leonardo had painted was damaged. Vasari, who wrote about it decades afterwards, says it began to drip so badly that Leonardo gave it up. He stopped work around 1505.

Historians disagree about how much of the picture was ever painted on the wall, and about what happened to it later. What is not in doubt is the result. The commission was not completed, and the hall never received the picture it had been promised.

One detail repays attention. The braziers were a solution, a sensible one at first sight: wet paint needs warmth. But the heat meant to dry the paint softened the wax in it, so the fix produced the damage. A problem was met with more of the thing that made it worse.

The mistake was not stubbornness, since Leonardo gave the project up. It was the missing pause between the first sign of trouble and the decision to push harder. Asking what the heat was doing to the paint is a short, cheap check that is easy to skip. Leonardo did stop in the end, but the step before stopping, reading the trend early, tends to get skipped when a deadline is close and a famous room is waiting.

[[lessons]]
$$**When a fix is making things worse, stop adding to it and look at what it is doing.** It is natural to answer a stubborn problem with more effort, more hours or more pressure, and sometimes that works. Leonardo's braziers show the other case, and with the heat, the paint and the clock all working against him, where the remedy and the damage are the same thing. When a project resists you, pause before the next push and ask whether your last action helped or hurt. A small check on a corner of the work costs far less than a large correction on the whole of it.$$

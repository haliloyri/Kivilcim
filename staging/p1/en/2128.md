[[story]]
Albert Hofmann worked as a chemist at the Swiss pharmaceutical firm Sandoz. In 1938 he made the twenty-fifth in a series of lysergic acid compounds he was studying, and it became known as LSD-25. Animal tests showed little beyond some excited behaviour, so the compound was shelved. This story belongs to the origins of the subject Michael Pollan explores in *How to Change Your Mind*, and it starts five years after that shelving.

On Friday 16 April 1943, while crystallising a few centigrams of the substance, Hofmann suddenly felt unwell and went home in the middle of the afternoon. On the Monday he reported it to his superior in a short memo. He first suspected fumes from the solvent, but breathing them did nothing. He concluded that a trace of the compound must have soaked in through his fingertips.

Three days later, on 19 April, he tested the idea on purpose. At 4:20 in the afternoon he took 250 micrograms, an amount he considered tiny, and he told only his laboratory assistant. Forty minutes later he made the one entry in his lab journal: beginning dizziness, anxiety, visual distortions, symptoms of paralysis, a desire to laugh.

Because of wartime restrictions on cars, the two men travelled on bicycles. Hofmann later described how everything in his field of vision wavered and distorted, as if seen in a curved mirror. At home he could just about ask his companion to fetch the family doctor and some milk from the neighbours, a remedy then used against poisoning. The neighbour brought the milk, and Hofmann drank more than two litres of it that evening. The doctor found dilated pupils and nothing else: blood pressure, breathing and pulse were normal. He gave no medicine and stayed until the worst had passed. Hofmann wrote that the horror softened into a feeling of good fortune and gratitude, and that by the next morning everything glistened in a fresh light.

Look at the order of events. He took a strange symptom seriously, ruled out the obvious cause, formed a guess and then built a test with a date and an hour attached. Yet the amount he called tiny showed that his sense of scale had been wrong. Being careful and being right about the size of the risk are two different things, and the first does not guarantee the second.

Notice too that he did not do this alone. His assistant knew what was happening, a doctor could be called, and milk was within reach. The test stayed survivable because a few people knew in advance what to do if it went badly.

[[lessons]]
$$**Write down the time and the detail of any strange signal the moment you notice it, and test your idea of "small" before you rely on it.** Hofmann's first unexplained illness was recorded in a memo, which is why he knew what he was looking for three days later. At work the same habit helps: when a system shows an odd reading, log the moment and the conditions straight away. Even a test that looks modest may be small only according to your own guess, not according to the data, so ask what a wrong guess would cost. Then bring in someone who knows the plan, so that a surprise has a known next step.$$

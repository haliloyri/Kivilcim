[[open]]
Picture yourself interviewing a leader who took an ordinary company to years of outstanding results. You ask the obvious thing: when exactly did it all change? What was the big decision, the bold program, the moment? And they just look at you, unable to answer. That's what happened again and again in the research behind *Good to Great*. The reason they couldn't answer might change how you judge your own slow progress.

[[story]]
Jim Collins and his research team set out to answer a big question. Why do some companies go from good results to sustained great results, while similar companies never make that leap? So they studied the ones that made it and compared each with a comparable company that didn't.

Naturally, they wanted the turning point. Every success story seems to have one: the bet, the launch, the day the fortunes flipped. So they asked the executives of the good-to-great companies to name the single defining moment or program behind their transformation.

> "When exactly did it all change?"

Most of them couldn't point to one. They weren't being modest or cagey. The moment just didn't exist.

Collins states it flatly in the book: these transformations never happened in one fell swoop. There was no single defining action, no grand program, no killer innovation, no lucky break, no miracle moment.

To explain what happened instead, he uses an image. Imagine a huge, heavy flywheel, a massive metal disk you're trying to turn. Your first push barely moves it. You keep pushing, and it creeps forward. You push again in the same direction, then again. Slowly it picks up speed. None of those pushes is the one that did it. But at some point you hit a breakthrough, and the flywheel's own weight starts working for you. Momentum takes over.

##From the outside, it looked like a sudden, dramatic change. From the inside, it felt like one more push.##

That gap matters. The press and outside observers often described these companies as having transformed overnight. The insiders described something organic and cumulative. Collins gave the pattern a name: *buildup, then breakthrough*.

Then there were the comparison companies, the ones that stayed merely good. They often fell into what Collins calls the Doom Loop. They launched new programs with fanfare, chased fads and made reactive changes of direction. They tried to jump straight to the breakthrough and skip the buildup. When results didn't show up fast enough, they reversed course and announced something new. Momentum stalled, and the cycle started over. Every time they changed direction, the flywheel lost whatever speed it had.

One more detail in the book is easy to miss. The good-to-great leaders spent little energy trying to "create alignment" or "manage change." They didn't need big campaigns to win people over. Once momentum showed up in visible results, people tended to align on their own. Nobody has to be talked into pushing a wheel that's clearly turning.

[[lessons]]
$$**No single push did it.** The executives couldn't name a defining moment because there wasn't one. The heavy flywheel moved because of many consistent pushes in one direction, each one too small to look like a turning point on its own. If you're hunting for the one dramatic act that will change everything, you may be skipping the only thing that actually works: the next push, in the same direction as the last.$$

$$**Overnight success is a view from the outside.** Outsiders saw a sudden breakthrough. The people inside saw a long, cumulative process. So when you compare your early, barely moving flywheel with someone else's visible success, you're comparing your buildup with their breakthrough. That's an unfair comparison, and it's the kind that tempts people to quit.$$

$$**Changing direction resets the wheel.** The comparison companies didn't fail from lack of effort. They failed from launching, getting disappointed, reversing and relaunching. Every new program meant pushing a stopped flywheel from scratch. And because their results never became visible, they never got what the good-to-great leaders got for free: people aligning on their own once momentum appeared.$$

[[reflect]]
- Where are you waiting for a breakthrough moment when what's needed is one more push in the same direction?
- Think of an effort you dropped or redirected after a few weeks. Was it failing, or just still in the heavy, early stage?
- Whose "overnight success" have you envied without seeing the years of pushing behind it?
- Where are you trying to persuade people to commit when steady visible results would do the persuading for you?

&&What flywheel are you already pushing that deserves another hundred pushes in the same direction?&&

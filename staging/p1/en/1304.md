Here is a simple test. Someone asks you a question with a numerical answer, and instead of a single guess, you give a range. Make it wide enough that you're 98 percent sure the true answer falls inside. Nassim Nicholas Taleb describes what happens when people actually do this, and the ranges keep coming out too narrow.

[[story]]
The test appears in *The Black Swan*, in a chapter Taleb calls "The Scandal of Prediction." It is one of several pieces of evidence he lines up against our faith in forecasting.

The setup is a calibration experiment. If people's sense of certainty were accurate, a 98 percent range should miss the true answer only about 2 percent of the time. That is what "98 percent sure" means.

It isn't what happens. People asked for these ranges were wrong far more often than 2 percent of the time. Their ranges were much too tight for the confidence they claimed. Taleb has a name for this gap between what we think we know and what we actually know: epistemic arrogance.

##People who say they're 98 percent sure turn out to be wrong far more often than 2 percent of the time.##

A reasonable response would be that this describes ordinary people guessing outside their field, and that experts do better. Taleb's next piece of evidence addresses that directly.

He cites Philip Tetlock's study of expert forecasters, people whose job involved political and economic predictions. Their forecasts did little better than chance. And like the people in the calibration tests, the experts were overconfident. Expertise gave them knowledge, but it did not make their confidence match their accuracy.

Then he turns to the methods themselves. In forecasting competitions run by Spyros Makridakis, sophisticated statistical methods did not outperform simple ones. The added complexity bought no extra accuracy.

Put the three findings together and a pattern appears. Ordinary people, experts, and elaborate models all run into the same wall. Taleb's explanation is that we keep relying on forecasts because we underestimate uncertainty and overestimate what we know. The 98 percent range feels wide to the person drawing it. The world turns out to be wider.

So what should anyone do, if predicting is this unreliable? Taleb's answer is to shift the effort. Invest in preparedness rather than prediction.

That means asking a different question. Instead of "What exactly will happen?", ask "What consequences could I handle, and which ones would I not survive?" You don't need to know which outcome arrives. You need a plan that holds up across the range, including the far end you were tempted to leave out.

[[lessons]]
$$**Confidence isn't accuracy.** In the calibration experiments, people's "98 percent sure" ranges missed far more often than 2 percent of the time. Feeling certain is a statement about your state of mind, not about the world. The two can drift a long way apart without you noticing.$$

$$**Experts miss too.** Tetlock found expert forecasts did little better than chance, and the experts were overconfident. Makridakis's competitions showed sophisticated methods didn't beat simple ones. Credentials and complexity can make a forecast sound stronger without making it more reliable.$$

$$**Prepare for a range, not a point.** Taleb's advice is to invest in preparedness rather than prediction. Since you can't reliably know which outcome is coming, focus on the consequences: which ones you can absorb, and which ones would break you. Then build your plan around surviving the bad end, so a wrong forecast costs you less.$$

[[reflect]]
- Which plan of yours depends on a single forecast being right?
- How often have your confident estimates of time or cost actually held?
- If you drew a 98 percent range for your next big estimate, how wide would it honestly need to be?

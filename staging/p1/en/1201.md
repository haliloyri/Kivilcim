Airlines move enormous numbers of people around the world every day. They create huge value for travelers and the economy. And yet, as businesses, they often earn remarkably little profit. Peter Thiel thinks this tells you something important about competition, and something most business advice gets wrong.

[[story]]
In *Zero to One*, written with Blake Masters, Peter Thiel, co-founder of PayPal and an early investor in Facebook, makes an argument that sounds almost heretical. Competition, he says, is for losers.

He means this about businesses, not about people. We're taught that competition is healthy, that it drives innovation and lowers prices. For customers, it often does. But for a company, Thiel argues, intense competition is a trap. When many firms offer similar products, they fight on price and features, and profits get competed away. In the long run, in a truly competitive market, nobody makes much money.

He illustrates this with a comparison. U.S. airlines carry a vast number of passengers and create enormous value. But they compete fiercely, flight by flight, and their profits are thin. Google, by contrast, creates less total value than the entire airline industry, but it captures a much larger share of what it creates as profit. The difference isn't that Google works harder. It's that Google isn't fighting a dozen nearly identical rivals for every customer.

##Creating value and capturing value, Thiel insists, are two completely different things.##

The companies Thiel admires are what he calls **creative monopolies**. They don't dominate through unfair tactics. They dominate because they've built something so much better, or so different, that there's no close substitute. Customers choose them not because they're slightly cheaper, but because nothing else really does the same job.

He sums it up by adapting the opening line of Tolstoy's *Anna Karenina*: all happy companies are different; each one earns a monopoly by solving a unique problem. All failed companies are the same: they failed to escape competition.

Thiel adds a sharp observation about how companies describe themselves. Monopolies tend to play down how dominant they are, presenting themselves as one player in a huge market. Companies in brutal competition do the opposite: they exaggerate how unique they are, defining their market so narrowly that they seem to be the only one in it. Both stories can mislead.

His practical advice to founders is to start small. Don't enter a vast market full of competitors. Find a small market you can completely dominate, then expand from there. PayPal, he notes, began by focusing on a small group of intense users before growing.

[[lessons]]
$$**Creating value isn't the same as capturing it.** Airlines create enormous value but keep little of it; Google keeps much more. Thiel's point is that doing something useful isn't enough to build a lasting business. You also need a position where you aren't constantly forced to give that value away in price wars.$$

$$**Be different, not just better.** Creative monopolies win because they solve a problem in a way no one else does. "Slightly better than the competition" leads to a race you can't stop running. "Unlike anything else" gives customers a reason to choose you that no discount can easily match.$$

$$**Start small and dominate.** Thiel advises owning a small market completely before expanding. It's tempting to aim at a huge market, but in a big, crowded space you're one of many. In a small one, you can become the obvious choice, and that becomes a base to grow from.$$

[[reflect]]
- Where are you competing head-on with others when you could be doing something different?
- What problem could you solve better, or differently, than anyone else around you?
- Are you describing your work as "better than X" or as "the only one that does Y"?

&&What small market, or niche, could you realistically own completely?&&

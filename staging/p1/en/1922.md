A psychiatrist confesses to the Dalai Lama that some of his patients make him feel like a fraud. The reply he gets back is a question with a number in it, and it is not about the difficult patients at all. It is about everyone else.

[[story]]
Howard Cutler practised psychiatry in Arizona, and the book he wrote with the Dalai Lama, The Art of Happiness, is built from their conversations. In one of them Cutler admitted something that most professionals keep to themselves. Some patients were very difficult to treat, he said, and with them he felt incompetent and anxious.

As Cutler recounts it, the Dalai Lama answered with a question. "Are you able to help 70 percent of your patients?" Cutler said that he was.

> No problem, then.

The Dalai Lama added a line that has stayed with readers: if Cutler could help only 30 percent, he might suggest that Cutler consider another profession.

Read the exchange slowly and a rule appears. The Dalai Lama gave Cutler a measure of his work, and the measure was a rate across all his patients, not the outcome of the hardest one. The hard cases did not vanish. Of every hundred patients, thirty might still go unhelped, and the Dalai Lama's reply allowed for that. What it took away was the verdict. A psychiatrist who is helping seven in ten is doing the job well enough to continue, and the cases that resist him are part of the work, not proof against it.

The same book records a second exchange that points the same way. Cutler described a woman who kept up self-destructive behaviour although it was clearly harming her life, and asked how to treat her. The Dalai Lama answered, "I don't know," shrugged, laughed and waited for the next question. Cutler has said that this forced him to rethink how the book would be put together.

The bar he named is worth noticing. It sits well below perfection, at a level a conscientious professional can actually reach, and it is stated as a number that can be checked. A feeling of incompetence cannot be checked, while a rate can be counted.

Taken together, the two moments are about limits. One says that a limit on how many people you can help is normal. The other says that a limit on what you know can be stated aloud, calmly, without a rescue.

[[lessons]]
$$**Judge your work by your overall record, not by the case that hurts most.** The cases that go wrong stay vivid, while the many that went well fade, so the single loss can start to feel like the whole truth about you. A hairdresser who still thinks about the one client who left unhappy can ask what share of her clients come back and recommend her friends. Looking at that wider record gives you a fair picture of your competence, and it leaves you free to learn from the failure without letting it decide your worth.$$

[[open]]
A quiet moment. Nothing is happening, nobody is talking, and your thumb is already opening an app. No buzz, no badge, no message arrived. Something sent you there anyway. Nir Eyal thinks he knows what it was, and why the answer should make both product designers and ordinary phone owners stop and think.

[[story]]
In *Hooked: How to Build Habit-Forming Products*, Nir Eyal sorts the things that prompt us to use a product into two groups.

The first group is external triggers. These are the prompts that come from outside: ads, emails, notifications, a friend's recommendation. They tell you what to do next, usually in plain terms. Tap here. Open this.

The second group is internal triggers. These live inside the user. They are feelings, routines, or situations that call up a behavior without anything on the screen asking for it.

Eyal singles out one kind of internal trigger as especially powerful: negative emotions. Boredom. Loneliness. Frustration. Confusion. Indecision. All of them are uncomfortable states.

Products usually begin with external triggers. At first, nobody reaches for a new app on their own; something has to prompt them. But through repeated use, Eyal says, the product becomes associated with an internal trigger. Once that link forms, the prompt no longer needs to come from outside.

##The user returns without any prompt at all, because a feeling has taken the notification's place.##

For designers, Eyal offers a way to find that feeling. Ask why a user would want the product. Then ask why again, and keep asking, until the answer is an emotion. That emotion is the internal trigger worth understanding.

He does not leave it there. Knowing how to attach a product to someone's boredom or loneliness is a kind of power, and Eyal addresses it with what he calls a Manipulation Matrix. It asks the maker two questions. Would you use this product yourself? And does it materially improve the lives of the people who use it?

That is where the tension sits. The same link between a feeling and a product that makes a habit valuable can also leave a person reaching, again and again, for something that never actually resolves the feeling that sent them.

[[lessons]]
$$**Lasting habits are cued by feelings.** Through repeated use, a product becomes tied to an internal trigger such as boredom or loneliness. From then on, a reminder is unnecessary; the feeling does the reminding. That is why a habit can feel automatic even when nothing prompted it.$$

$$**Finding the emotion takes more than one "why."** Eyal's method is to keep asking why a user wants the product until an emotion appears. The first answer is usually practical. The feeling behind it sits a few layers down, and that is the part that brings people back.$$

$$**Building habits brings a duty to ask if they help.** The Manipulation Matrix asks makers whether they would use the product themselves and whether it materially improves users' lives. Those two questions work from the other side too: you can ask them about the apps that have become your own habits.$$

[[reflect]]
- When you feel bored, uncertain, or alone, what do you reach for first?
- Does it usually leave you better off, or just distracted?
- Which of your habits started with a notification and now runs on a feeling instead?

[[open]]
Picture a long drive through farm country. The first cows you pass are lovely: brown, calm, grazing in the sun. By the twentieth field you've stopped looking. Now picture one cow that's purple. You'd slow down. You'd point. You'd probably tell someone about it that night. Seth Godin built a whole book on that difference, and it changes how you see your own work.

[[story]]
In *Purple Cow*, published in 2003, Seth Godin starts with that drive. You're out in the countryside, and at first the cows are charming. They fit the picture you had of a pastoral afternoon. But the charm wears off fast. After a while, one brown cow looks exactly like the next, and your eyes slide right past them.

Then he asks you to imagine a purple cow standing in the middle of a field. Nothing about the cow is more useful. It doesn't give better milk. It's simply impossible to ignore. It's the one you'd mention to a friend.

> *Wait. Was that cow purple?*

That reaction, Godin argues, is the only kind of attention that counts in a crowded market. His point is sharper than "stand out." Most people assume the enemy of a great product is a bad one. Godin says no. Bad products fail on their own. The real trap is the product that's competent and pleasant and indistinguishable from everything around it.

##The opposite of remarkable isn't bad. It's very good.##

A very good product is a brown cow. Nobody complains about it, and nobody talks about it either. In a market full of options, being very good makes you invisible.

From there he goes after the most common instinct in business: aim for the average customer, the big middle of the market. It feels sensible, because that's where the numbers are. But Godin argues it's a losing strategy, because the average customer ignores new things. They're busy, they're satisfied, and they aren't looking for anything different. Pitching to them is like waving at drivers who've already stopped watching the fields.

So who should you design for? Godin points to a much smaller group: the early adopters, the people who actively want something new. Within that group are the people he calls "sneezers," the ones who spread ideas. They see the purple cow, and they tell everyone. A product that thrills them travels. A product built to be acceptable to everyone just sits in the field.

This leads to his most counterintuitive claim. Playing it safe, by making something bland and broadly acceptable so nobody objects, feels like the careful choice. Godin argues it's actually the risky one, because it guarantees you'll be overlooked. The real safety lies in being remarkable to a specific, interested group, even if everyone else shrugs.

Being acceptable to everyone, in his view, matters far less than being worth talking about to someone.

[[lessons]]
$$**The opposite of remarkable is very good.** The brown cows on that drive weren't ugly or sick. They were perfectly fine, and that's exactly why they vanished from view. When everything around you is competent, competence stops earning attention. If your work is "very good," that might be the thing holding it back.$$

$$**Design for the sneezers, not the masses.** The average customer is the driver who stopped looking at the fields twenty minutes ago. Godin's advice is to build for the few who are still looking: early adopters who want something new and sneezers who pass it on. They're the ones who'll slow down for the purple cow and tell a friend about it.$$

$$**Being boring is the riskiest strategy.** Smoothing every edge so nobody objects feels like protection. Godin flips it: blending in doesn't shield you from rejection, it only means nobody sees you in the first place. The cow that tries hardest to look like every other cow is the one guaranteed to be forgotten.$$

[[reflect]]
- Think of the last thing you made or pitched. Was it a brown cow, fine but forgettable, or would someone go out of their way to mention it?
- Name the few people who would care intensely about your work. Did you build it for them, or for an imagined everyone?
- Where are you sanding off edges right now just so nobody objects?
- Who are the sneezers in your world, the people who pass on what excites them?

&&What would you have to change to become remarkable to a specific group, even if it made you less appealing to everyone else?&&

Two mathematicians, one in New York and one in California, finish a twenty-two page paper about curved spaces. It solves nobody's problem, and no company, ministry or laboratory has asked for it. The pages are all definitions and proofs. The paper goes into a journal, and it waits.

[[story]]
Before Jim Simons became a market legend, he was a geometer, and Gregory Zuckerman's *The Man Who Solved the Market* follows him from there. This story is about a piece of pure mathematics that carries his name. After his firing from the Institute for Defense Analyses in 1968, Simons became chairman of the mathematics department at Stony Brook University. There he worked with Shiing-Shen Chern, a geometer based at Berkeley, on a question about curvature.

Their paper, "Characteristic forms and geometric invariants," appeared in the Annals of Mathematics in January 1974 and ran from page 48 to page 69. It produced invariants, numbers attached to a curved space that help tell certain spaces apart. An invariant earns its keep by letting a mathematician say that two shapes are really different without having to lay one on top of the other. These are the kind of bent geometry that Einstein's general relativity describes, but nothing in the paper was meant for a laboratory.

Simons has said the work was good mathematics, and that was the whole claim he made for it. For about ten years, by his account, nothing much followed. Then physicists picked it up. The dates are a little looser than that memory suggests, since the famous physics paper below came fifteen years after the geometry one.

In 1989 the physicist Edward Witten published "Quantum Field Theory and the Jones Polynomial" in the journal Communications in Mathematical Physics. In it he used what is now called Chern–Simons theory to compute invariants of knots. The theory has since turned up in string theory and in condensed-matter physics, according to a published profile of Simons.

Waiting that long is the part few people plan for. The paper was written in 1974 and the physics came afterward, so nobody at Stony Brook could have written it for physicists. Its test, in the years when it sat unused, was whether the mathematics held up.

Simons left academia in 1978 to start the company that became Renaissance Technologies. His name stayed on the theory. In 2008 the Simons Foundation put sixty million dollars into founding the Simons Center for Geometry and Physics at Stony Brook, a place built on the idea that the two fields feed each other.

[[lessons]]
$$**Keep a corner of your week for work whose use you cannot yet name.** Simons and Chern judged their paper by whether it was sound, and the uses arrived from people they had never planned for. A teacher who writes a lesson just because the idea is elegant, or an analyst who builds a tidy dataset nobody asked for, is doing the same thing on a smaller scale. Protect that corner from the demand for an immediate payoff, such as a meeting that asks what it is for, and let the quality of the work stand as its own reason to do it until someone finds a use.$$

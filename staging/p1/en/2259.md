On one transmission line, a megawatt is scheduled to travel against the traffic. On another line, beyond the grid operator's sight, the same megawatt travels back. Between the two, a payment arrives for easing a traffic jam that nobody eased. A lawyer reads the trading desk's own descriptions and writes them up for his client.

[[story]]
On 8 December 2000, two lawyers at the firm Stoel Rives, Christian Yoder and Stephen Hall, sent a memo to Richard Sanders at Enron, marked confidential and privileged. Its subject was "Traders' Strategies in the California Wholesale Power Markets." It described what Enron's traders were doing in California's power market. The story of Enron's collapse, which Bethany McLean and Peter Elkind tell in *The Smartest Guys in the Room*, sits alongside this episode.

The memo noted that some strategies carried nicknames the traders themselves had given them. One was "Death Star." California's grid operator paid parties who scheduled power in the opposite direction of a congested line, to ease the jam. Under the memo's description, Enron scheduled energy from Lake Mead to the California-Oregon border, against the direction of congestion, and collected the payment. Then it bought transmission from the border back to Lake Mead, over a line outside the operator's control area.

The detail mattered. Because the return leg sat outside the system the operator monitored, the operator, in the memo's words, probably could not readily detect that the same energy was coming back at the same moment. The memo put it plainly: the operator "only sees half of the picture." Its own summary read, "The net effect of these transactions is that Enron gets paid for moving energy to relieve congestion without actually moving any energy or relieving any congestion."

Another firm, Brobeck, wrote a separate memo that took a different view. It said congestion had been relieved and that energy did flow through underused paths, and it argued the strategies carried financial risks for Enron. Enron's lawyer Robert Bennett later said, "We do not know if any of the assertions in the memo are true."

The documents surfaced in May 2002, when Enron gave the first memo to the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission and FERC posted it. Senator Dianne Feinstein called it "a smoking gun." In March 2003, FERC staff reported that many trading strategies used by Enron and other companies violated the anti-gaming provisions of the California market tariffs, and recommended that profits be disgorged.

##Everything in the scheme depended on a gap between what the rule-maker could see and what the trader could do.## The operator's checks were real. They simply covered only the half of the picture that sat inside its own territory.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask what your checks cannot see.** A school that records attendance only at the first lesson will find that some pupils sign in and leave, and the register will still look perfect to the person who reads only the register. Any measure covers a part of the picture, and anyone who understands where the edge is can behave differently on the far side of it. For each figure or control you rely on, write down the half it does not observe and add one small check there, so that a clean report means what you think it means, and a surprise shows up before it grows.$$

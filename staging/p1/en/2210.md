[[open]]
Before a puzzle task that offers both a chance to cheat and a reward for it, one person writes down as many of the Ten Commandments as they can remember. Another writes down ten books they read in high school. Then both sit down with the same pile of puzzles.

[[story]]
Dan Ariely, author of *The Honest Truth About Dishonesty* (2012), published a well-known paper in 2008 with Nina Mazar and On Amir. It rests on an idea called self-concept maintenance: people cheat to profit, but only as far as they can still see themselves as decent. A moral reminder, the theory goes, should make cheating harder to square with that picture.

Experiment 1 tested it. Before a problem-solving task that gave participants both the opportunity and the incentive to cheat, some recalled the Ten Commandments and the rest recalled ten books from high school. The authors reported that the Commandments group solved 1.45 fewer matrices than the books group, an effect size of 0.48, and concluded that moral reminders reduced cheating. The paper became widely cited in research on deception.

Then, for a long time, nobody repeated that experiment exactly. A team of researchers including Verschuere and Meijer finally did, as a registered replication report: 25 labs ran the same preregistered protocol, 5,786 participants in total. Preregistered means the plan, the method and the analysis were fixed in public before a single number came in, so nobody could adjust them afterwards to find an effect.

The main analysis pooled 19 of those replications, 4,674 participants. In it, the group that had recalled the Commandments reported solving 0.11 more matrices than the books group, not fewer. The 95 percent confidence interval ran from −0.09 to 0.31, so zero sits comfortably inside it, and the effect size came out at −0.04. The report appeared in 2018 in *Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science*.

This is one experiment, not the whole theory. The replication did not test the other experiments in the 2008 paper, and it cannot say whether some other reminder might work in some other setting. What it does remove is one of the most quoted demonstrations. Thinking about the Ten Commandments before a task that let them cheat did not make thousands of participants report fewer correct answers.

The size of the original number is worth a second look as well. A reminder that takes a few minutes and moves behavior by roughly half a standard deviation is the kind of result everyone wants to be true. Wanting is no evidence.

[[lessons]]
$$**Try a feel-good ritual on yourself before you build on it.** A small ceremony that is supposed to make people more honest, more focused or less error-prone is easy to believe in, because it is cheap and sounds sensible. Say a family starts reading the house rules aloud before a board game: play some evenings with the reading and some without, and keep a note of what actually changes. If nothing does, you have lost nothing and have one ritual fewer to maintain. If something does, you now know it from your own table instead of from a headline.$$

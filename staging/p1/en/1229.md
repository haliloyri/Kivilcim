You walk out of a tense meeting and straight into the next one. Or you finish a stressful workday and walk directly into your home, where your family is waiting. What comes through the door with you? For many of us, it's the leftover tension of whatever we just left.

[[story]]
In *High Performance Habits*, Brendon Burchard describes six habits that he says distinguish people who perform well over the long term. One of them is **generate energy**.

Burchard's point is that high performers don't simply have more energy than everyone else by nature. They manage it. They pay attention to the basics, like sleep, exercise and nutrition. But they also use small, deliberate practices to renew their energy throughout the day, instead of letting it drain away.

One place he focuses on is something most of us never think about: **transitions**. The moments between activities. Finishing one meeting and starting another. Ending a phone call. Closing the laptop and going home. Walking from the car into the house.

##Transitions, Burchard argues, are where much of our energy leaks away, and where our worst moods travel from one part of life into the next.##

Without a pause, we carry the frustration of the last meeting into the next. We bring the stress of the workday into the kitchen. We snap at people who had nothing to do with it, or show up distracted and flat to the things that matter most.

So he suggests a simple practice for every transition. He calls it **release tension, set intention**.

First, release tension. Before you start the next activity, stop for a minute or so. Close your eyes if you can. Breathe deeply. Let your shoulders drop. Consciously let go of the tension from whatever you just finished. You're clearing the slate.

Second, set intention. Ask yourself how you want to show up in what comes next. What energy do you want to bring? How do you want to treat the people you're about to see? What do you want to achieve? Maybe it's "I want to be patient and curious in this meeting." Maybe it's "I want to be present and playful with my kids tonight."

The whole practice can take a minute. But Burchard argues that repeated throughout the day, it changes the quality of everything you do. You stop being dragged along by the momentum of your last experience, and start choosing how you enter the next one.

[[lessons]]
$$**Energy leaks in the gaps.** Burchard points to transitions, the moments between activities, as the places where stress travels and energy drains. Most of us rush through them without a thought. Noticing these gaps is the first step toward using them, instead of letting them carry the worst of one moment into the next.$$

$$**Release before you restart.** A minute of deep breathing and physically letting go of tension clears some of what the last activity left behind. It's a small act, but it prevents the frustration of one meeting, or one workday, from spilling onto the next people you meet.$$

$$**Choose how you'll show up.** Setting an intention, how you want to feel and act in the next activity, turns autopilot into a decision. It's especially powerful at the door of your home, where the people who matter most often get the tired leftovers of your day.$$

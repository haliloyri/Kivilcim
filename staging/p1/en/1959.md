A man has spent years training himself against the things that frighten most people. Then one day word arrives that the city he loves has burned, in a way nobody had thought to fear. His will, which friends know as steady, bends under the news.

[[story]]
Fire has damaged many cities, Seneca writes to Lucilius in Letter 91, but it has annihilated none. Even when an enemy hurls flames at the walls, the fire dies out in many places and rarely finishes the work, so that something is left for the sword. Even earthquakes have hardly ever flattened a whole town. And yet the colony of Lyons, he says, has just burned to the ground.

The letter is about his friend Liberalis, who is downcast at the news. Seneca is not surprised that his friend never braced for this. The event had no precedent, so there was nothing to train against. Many beautiful buildings, any one of which would have made a town famous, were wrecked in a single night. It happened in deep peace, with weapons at rest everywhere, which makes it worse than anything people dread even in wartime.

Then comes the sentence that sticks. Great things usually get a warning, Seneca says, a stretch of reprieve before they fall. Lyons got one night between the city at its height and the city gone. It takes him longer to tell Lucilius that it has perished than it took to perish.

Liberalis is shaken, in Seneca's reading, because the shock is unexpected, and the unexpected lays the heaviest load on us. Strangeness adds weight to any calamity. So his remedy is a standing rule: nothing should come as a surprise. Send the mind ahead to meet every problem, and think about what can happen, not what usually happens. Exile, illness, war and shipwreck should be rehearsed in advance, and in the end we should picture the very worst that could happen.

He then widens the view to console Liberalis. Cities in Asia and Achaia, in Syria and Macedonia, have been laid low by a single earthquake, and the Lyons that burned stood on one modest hill. Founded barely a hundred years earlier, it had already survived many calamities within an old man's lifetime. Seneca guesses that its citizens will now rebuild it larger and safer.

Tacitus, writing about the same disaster, says the emperor Nero gave the city four million sesterces to rebuild. The people of Lyons, he adds, had earlier sent the same sum to Rome after its own great fire. For the friend of Seneca, the practical conclusion was simpler: the blow is easier to carry when you have already looked at it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Think through the worst case in advance, and the shock of bad news stops doing much of the damage.** A small workshop owner who depends on a single client may never picture that client leaving, so when the call comes he freezes and loses a week to indecision. A neighbour in the same trade who has already sketched a fallback still feels the loss, yet knows his first move on the morning it happens. Rehearsal does not make you gloomy: it takes the surprise out of the blow and leaves a problem you can work on. One quiet evening with a notebook is enough to do it.$$

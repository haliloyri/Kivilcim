Several thousand middle-aged twins fill in the same questionnaire about how well their lives are going. Afterwards the researchers lay the answers next to everything they can measure: schooling, family income, marriage, religious commitment. Almost none of it explains why one person scores higher than another.

[[story]]
That is the finding of a 1996 paper by David Lykken and Auke Tellegen, "Happiness Is a Stochastic Phenomenon," in *Psychological Science*. They measured well-being with the Well-Being scale of a personality questionnaire, in a sample built from birth records. Then they asked what predicted the scores.

Life circumstances barely did. Socioeconomic status, educational attainment, family income, marital status and an indicator of religious commitment each accounted for no more than about 3 percent of the variance in well-being. Between 44 and 52 percent of the variance, by contrast, was associated with genetic variation, which is what comparing identical and fraternal twins lets you estimate.

The authors went one step further. Smaller samples of twins had been retested after 4.5 and 10 years, and from those retests Lykken and Tellegen estimated that the heritability of the stable component of well-being approaches 80 percent. In plain words: once you filter out the ups and downs of a given week, the steady part of how happy someone is looks largely inherited.

Why does this belong in a book about feedback? Stone and Heen make a related point about temperament. Sheila Heen has said in a talk that people differ enormously in how long they take to recover from hard feedback, so the same comment may cost one person a week and another an afternoon. If part of that difference is wiring, then neither reaction is a moral grade, and nobody should be congratulated or blamed for the speed of their bounce-back.

A careful reader should keep the limits in view. Heritability is a statement about variation in a population, not a fixed ruling on any single person, and the study says nothing about what a person can or cannot learn to do. Its use here is narrower: it is evidence that much of how we feel, and how fast we return to normal, was set long before the meeting where we heard the criticism.

In a team, this changes a familiar scene: the slow recoverer can state a need instead of feeling inadequate, and colleagues need not read it as a flaw. That reframes the old reproach. "You take everything personally" becomes "You are built to feel it strongly and recover slowly," and the second sentence leads to something you can do, which is plan around it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Learn your own baseline and plan around it instead of arguing with it.** If a large share of your emotional pace is stable wiring, then being slow to recover from a critical comment tells you about your temperament and not about the quality of your character. A colleague who bounces back by lunch has a different baseline, not a better mind. So build your routine around yours: if you need two days, ask for the feedback in writing, schedule the follow-up conversation for the third day, and tell people you will answer then. You get the information without pretending to be someone else.$$

[[story]]
Grace Groner was born in 1909 in a farming community in Lake County, Illinois. She and her twin sister, Gladys, graduated from Lake Forest College in 1931, and according to published accounts a man named George Anderson paid for their education. Groner later worked as a secretary at Abbott Laboratories, and she stayed in that job for 43 years.

In 1935 she bought three Abbott shares at $60 each, $180 in all. Over the following years the shares split many times. A split raises the number of shares you hold. Groner also turned the dividends back into shares every time, which means she left the earnings inside the investment instead of putting them in her pocket. Those three shares, together with the new ones they produced, grew for decades.

Her way of life looked nothing like the size of the pile. She lived in a small cottage that had been left to her, and she bought her clothes at rummage sales. She also travelled widely and gave help to people in need without telling anyone. Nothing in her daily life showed how much she had.

Groner died in January 2010, at the age of 100. Reports describe her estate as "in excess of seven million dollars", and Business Insider puts it at $7.2 million. In 2008 she had set up a foundation to receive it, and the foundation serves students at Lake Forest College, her own alma mater. The income is meant to go to independent study, internships, study abroad and service projects. Her former home, "Grace's Cottage", houses two senior students each year.

Seven million dollars is roughly 38,000 times $180. Three things together produced that result: time, share splits and reinvested dividends. A caution is needed as well. The outcome depended on Abbott's stock gaining value over decades. The same behaviour in another company might not have given the same result, so this is an example rather than a guarantee.

Clason's book names making your money grow through investment as its third cure. Groner's story comes from real life and is nowhere in the book, yet it carries the same logic: a small sum, put in early, not sold, with its earnings reinvested. For seventy-five years the only thing she had to do was hold back: she kept every share.

What stands out is how small the first step was. Three shares, $180 and a very long patience.

[[lessons]]
$$**Put a small sum in early, reinvest what it earns, and keep your hands off it.** Groner's $180 did not need genius to grow, only years and the discipline of not touching it. If you set aside a small amount at the end of each month into a steady investment and make "don't withdraw the gains" a rule from the start, time begins working for you, and you do not have to decide again every single month. You do not have to wait for a large capital before you begin, a small start counts, and not tying everything to a single company belongs to the same patience, as long as the decision is made on the first day.$$

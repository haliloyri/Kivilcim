A wheel of fortune spins and stops on a number. Everyone watching knows the number is random. It has nothing to do with the question they're about to answer. And yet, a few seconds later, it quietly pulls their answer toward it.

[[story]]
In *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, Daniel Kahneman describes an experiment he ran with his long-time collaborator Amos Tversky. They built a wheel of fortune marked from 0 to 100. But they secretly rigged it so it could only stop at two numbers: 10 or 65.

Students at the University of Oregon came in one at a time. Each spun the wheel and wrote down the number it landed on. Then they were asked two questions. First: is the percentage of African nations among the members of the United Nations larger or smaller than the number you just wrote? Second: what is your best guess of the actual percentage?

Everyone knew the wheel was just a wheel. The number it showed could not possibly carry any information about African nations.

##Students who saw 10 guessed, on average, 25 percent. Students who saw 65 guessed 45 percent.##

Kahneman calls this the **anchoring effect**. When we have to estimate an unknown quantity, a number we've just seen, even an obviously irrelevant one, becomes a starting point. We adjust away from it, but not far enough. Our final answer stays tied to the anchor.

You might think this only happens with students guessing about something they don't care about. Kahneman shows otherwise.

In one study, experienced German judges read the description of a woman caught shoplifting. Then they rolled a pair of dice, which, unknown to them, were rigged to show either 3 or 9. They were asked whether they would sentence her to more or fewer months than the number on the dice, and then what sentence they would actually give. Judges who rolled a 9 said, on average, 8 months. Judges who rolled a 3 said 5 months.

In another study, real-estate agents were asked to value a house. They were given a listing price, which was either higher or lower than the actual one. Their valuations moved toward the price they'd been shown. When asked, the agents insisted the listing price hadn't influenced them at all.

This is what makes anchoring so powerful. It works quietly, even on experts, and even on people who believe they're immune.

Kahneman offers practical advice for negotiations, where anchors matter enormously. If the other side opens with an outrageous number, don't treat it as a starting point for bargaining. Say clearly that it isn't acceptable as a basis for discussion, and focus your own mind on the arguments against it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Irrelevant numbers still steer us.** The students knew the wheel was random, and still moved their estimates by twenty percentage points. Anchoring doesn't require that we believe the number. It only requires that we see it. That's what makes it so hard to notice in everyday decisions.$$

$$**Expertise isn't a shield.** Experienced judges changed prison sentences after rolling dice. Real-estate agents shifted valuations based on a listing price they swore they ignored. If professionals are pulled by anchors in their own field, it's reasonable to assume that you are too, especially when you feel sure you aren't.$$

$$**Whoever sets the first number shapes the deal.** In a negotiation, the opening number becomes the anchor for everything after it. Kahneman's advice is to refuse to bargain from an outrageous anchor and to decide your own number, with reasons, before you hear theirs.$$

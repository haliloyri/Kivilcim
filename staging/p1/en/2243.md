[[open]]
A doctor says he wants a word with a mother in private, switches on a radio and leaves the room. The two adults stand in the doorway and watch the girl they came about. As the music plays, she moves, and the problem they walked in with begins to look like something else.

[[story]]
Sir Ken Robinson told this story at TED in February 2006, in a talk titled "Do schools kill creativity?" TED's own summary says he makes a case for an education system that nurtures creativity rather than undermining it. He skipped the statistics and argued it with one child, leaving the audience to draw the conclusion.

As he told it, the girl was struggling at school and could not sit still. Her mother took her to a doctor. The doctor asked to speak with the mother alone, turned on a radio and left the room with her. From the doorway they watched the child dance to the music. The doctor's suggestion was that she be sent to a dance school, which is a very different prescription from the one a struggling pupil might expect.

The girl was Gillian Lynne, born in 1926. She became a dramatic ballerina with the Royal Ballet, and later choreographed *Cats*, which won her an Olivier Award in 1981, and worked as choreographer and musical stager on *The Phantom of the Opera* in 1986. The childhood scene rests on Robinson's telling rather than on her own words, but her career is a matter of record.

The force of the story lies in what the doctor actually did. He diagnosed nothing and ran a small test instead: change the setting and see whether the behaviour changes. When the radio came on, it did. That gave the mother another answer to why her daughter could not sit still. The same restlessness could count as a problem in a classroom and as a talent in a dance studio, and nothing about the child had changed except the room.

The talk went on to become one of the most-watched in TED's history. TED's page shows more than 80 million plays, and it has been translated into dozens of languages. One reason is that nearly everyone has a school memory. The scene is short, visual and easy to carry into one's own life: one doctor, one radio, one child. Listeners recognised the fidgety child in their own classroom, or themselves, and Robinson's claim leaned on that recognition: the shape of a gift does not always match what a school knows how to measure.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you correct someone's "problem" behaviour, ask in which setting it might be a strength.** Someone you manage can look scattered in meetings and brilliant in the field, and a child can fidget at a desk and be fully absorbed on a stage. Trying a different setting is often the cheapest way to find the right question, because it costs a few minutes and no verdict. The doctor did exactly that: he switched on a radio and watched. When you do the same, you stop fitting the person to the measure and start questioning the measure itself, and it spares the other person from being judged in advance.$$

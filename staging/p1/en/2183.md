[[open]]
By the dining-room window of a small brick house stands a twelve-sided walnut table, hardly big enough for a tea tray. Family tradition says a woman wrote at it, and the novels that came from this one corner have been read for more than two hundred years.

[[story]]
Jane Austen moved into the cottage at Chawton, in Hampshire, on 7 July 1809. She came with her mother and her sister Cassandra, and a longtime family friend, Martha Lloyd, lived with them too. The house stood on the estate of her brother Edward, who provided it, and before them the estate's bailiff had lived there until his death in 1808.

She stayed until May 1817, almost eight years. When she arrived she had published nothing. She did, however, bring drafts with her: *Sense and Sensibility* and *Pride and Prejudice* were already written in an earlier form, and at Chawton she revised them before they went to the printer.

Then the books began to appear. *Sense and Sensibility* came out in 1811. *Pride and Prejudice* followed in 1813, and a letter from January of that year records the arrival of a copy. *Mansfield Park* was published in 1814 and *Emma* in 1815. Besides revising her early manuscripts, she wrote *Mansfield Park*, *Emma* and *Persuasion* in this house.

Look at the arithmetic of it. Four novels reached readers in her lifetime within five years, and every one of them was shaped under the same roof, in the same routine of a household of women in a small village. She did not travel to a writing retreat. The household was her setting, and the table, by family tradition, was her desk.

What made the difference was the stability. For years there was one place, one set of people and one habit of returning to the same pages. Nothing in the record suggests she raced. It suggests she revised for a long time what she had already begun, and then wrote new work on the same steady footing.

Consider what that routine left out. There was no move between cities during those years, no change of household and no second career to juggle. The list of things competing for her attention stayed short, and so the work that remained on it could be finished and then polished. Only after the early books had reached print did the later ones follow in quick order.

None of this proves that quiet alone produced the books; the talent was hers. But it shows the shape of her output: few projects at once, long periods with each, and a base so settled that the work could simply continue from day to day.

[[lessons]]
$$**Give your best work one settled place and a steady routine, and keep returning to the same few projects instead of starting new ones.** Austen revised drafts she had brought with her before she wrote the next books, so old work was not abandoned for new. You can copy that on a small scale: pick one desk or corner, one regular hour and a short list of projects, and return to them every day. The results of a modest routine kept up for years tend to outweigh a heroic week that is never repeated, because the work simply keeps moving.$$

A boat twenty-seven and a half metres long noses up to the landing stage of a river town in the Amazon region. Inside is a shop of about a hundred square metres, and every shelf carries products from a single company. The town gets its supermarket by water.

[[story]]
Chris van Tulleken's *Ultra-Processed People* is about how ultra-processed food reaches people. This story is a real scene from that world: the floating supermarket Nestlé sent along Brazilian rivers in 2010.

The project was called "Nestlé Até Você a Bordo". The boat was twenty-seven and a half metres long and travelled to small river towns in the Amazon region. In about three weeks it called at eighteen small cities. For these towns the river was the road, and the boat was a shop floating along it. A boat needs no road and no permanent building, and when one town has been served it simply moves on to the next. That is a flexible way to sell, and it explains why a river became a sales route.

Inside there was roughly a hundred square metres of retail space. The shelves held more than three hundred products, among them Ninho milk powder, Maggi products and Nescafé. A crew of eleven worked on board, running the counter, the shelves and the till of a shop that moved from town to town.

The company counted about 800,000 potential customers along the route. Set against a crew of eleven, that is a striking ratio, and it shows how a few people and one boat can aim at a very large audience.

Critics called the project junk food for the poor. The boat was not the only channel either. In the same year Bloomberg reported that about 7,000 women in Brazil sold Nestlé products door to door. The company's routes into people's lives included the river and the doorstep.

There is no data here linking the boat to anyone's health, so no such link is drawn. What can be seen is simpler: the road a product travels to reach a person. In a town with no shop nearby, the one store that ties up at the landing stage is that town's whole choice for the week. Read that as an inference rather than a finding, since nothing verifies it. Even so, a product finding you is not the same as you choosing it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Look at the road a product took to reach you.** When a brand keeps appearing in the same neighbourhood, outside the same school or in the same small town, effort may well have gone into making it easy to buy there. Someone who wonders why a new branch opened on their own street can start to tell advertising apart from real need while shopping. Once you notice how a product came to you, you can meet it on your own terms, and pause for a moment before putting something in the basket that was never on your list.$$

A high-school teacher puts a thick book on a boy's desk. Its title is about guided missiles, and the boy is a coal miner's son in a town with one employer. She does not explain it, and she does not offer to read it with him. She says one sentence, and the boy takes the book home.

[[story]]
In Hickam's account, the teacher is Miss Riley, and the sentence she said is among the best-known lines in *Rocket Boys*. She taught chemistry at Big Creek High School in McDowell County, West Virginia. According to the West Virginia Encyclopedia, her full name was Freida Joy Riley, and she urged the boys to dive into advanced math and physics.

The book was *Principles of Guided Missile Design*. Hickam's own discussion guide for the memoir quotes what Miss Riley said when she gave it to Sonny: "All I've done is give you a book. You have to have the courage to learn what's inside it."

Look at what that one sentence contains. She names what she has done, which is to give a book, and what she has left undone, which is the reading. There is no lesson plan in it, no promise that the boys will follow the text, and no offer to do the hard part for them. Sonny and his friends were building rockets on a coal dump, and the book gave their experiments a language to be measured in.

They used it. A reviewer in the Christian Science Monitor records that the boys taught themselves trigonometry to measure the range of their launches. That is a remarkable detail for teenagers with no classroom assignment behind it. No grade depended on the arithmetic, and the rocket did.

Miss Riley kept showing up for them in other ways. Hickam's guide says she stood up for the group against the school's feared principal, Mr. Turner. Later she was the one who wanted the boys to enter science fairs, and Hickam says they entered only near graduation because Miss Riley wanted them to try. Asked about her in an interview, Hickam said: "I had a huge crush on her."

What she gave them was small and cheap, a book and a challenge. What made it work was that the boys had already started building and wanted answers. A teacher cannot hand over curiosity, but she can recognise it and put the right difficult thing in front of it. That is why her sentence still gets quoted. Everything after that, the sums, the failed launches and the next design, was theirs.

[[lessons]]
$$**Hand someone the hard material, say plainly that the effort is theirs, and then step back.** Help that arrives with a promise to do the difficult part for them teaches the learner to wait, while a good book plus a trusting remark tells them you expect them to cope. This works best when they have already started something and have a question of their own, because then the difficulty has a purpose. Next time you teach or mentor, pick the one resource that is slightly too hard, give it over, and resist explaining it for the first week, so that the first question is theirs.$$

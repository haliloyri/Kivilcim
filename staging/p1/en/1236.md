Think of almost any great story. A hero with a problem. And somewhere along the way, a wise guide who helps them: a mentor, a teacher, an old master. Donald Miller asks a simple question that makes many business owners uncomfortable: in your story, which one are you trying to be?

[[story]]
In *Building a StoryBrand*, Donald Miller argues that the most powerful tool for communicating a message is the same one humans have used for thousands of years: story. And in stories, roles matter.

Every person, Miller says, sees themselves as the hero of their own story. They wake up with problems they want solved and goals they want to reach. When they come across a business, they are, often without realizing it, asking one question: can this help me win?

The mistake many businesses make is casting themselves as the hero. Their websites open with their history, their achievements, their awards, their vision. Their ads talk about how great the company is. The message, in effect, is: *look at us.*

##But when a brand plays the hero, it competes with the customer for the same role, and the customer stops listening.##

People aren't looking for another hero. They already have one: themselves. What they're looking for is a **guide**.

Miller points to the classic structure of stories. Think of the wise old mentor who trains a young hero, like Yoda guiding Luke Skywalker. The guide isn't the star. The guide has already been through the struggle, has the wisdom and tools the hero needs, and uses them to help the hero succeed. The story is about the hero's victory, not the guide's.

That, Miller argues, is exactly the role a business should play. The customer is the hero. The business is the guide.

He identifies two qualities a good guide must show. The first is **empathy**: the guide understands the hero's struggle and cares about it. A business shows empathy by speaking directly to the customer's problems and feelings: *we know how frustrating this is.* The second is **authority**: the guide is competent to help. A business shows authority through evidence: testimonials, results, experience, credentials, but used to reassure the customer, not to boast.

Empathy without authority feels kind but unhelpful. Authority without empathy feels arrogant. Together, they tell the customer: *we understand you, and we can help you win.*

Once a business shifts from hero to guide, Miller says, its whole message changes. It stops talking about itself and starts talking about the customer's problem, the customer's success and how it can help them get there.

[[lessons]]
$$**Your customer is the hero.** People see their own lives as a story in which they are the main character. Messages that center the company, its history, awards and pride, compete with that. Miller's point is that customers pay attention when they see themselves, and their problem, at the center.$$

$$**Be the guide, not the star.** The wise mentor in every classic story helps the hero win and then steps aside. A business that takes that role is far more compelling than one demanding attention for itself. It's a humbler position, and a much more persuasive one.$$

$$**Show empathy and authority together.** Empathy says "we understand your struggle"; authority says "we can help you solve it." One without the other falls flat. Good guides, in business or in life, combine genuine understanding with real competence, and use both in service of the other person.$$

[[reflect]]
- In your pitch, website or profile, who is the hero: you or the person you serve?
- How do you show empathy for your customers' or colleagues' problems?
- How do you show authority and competence without turning it into boasting?

&&How would your message change if your customer became the hero?&&

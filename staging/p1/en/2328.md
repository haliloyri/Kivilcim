A woman sits at a microscope with a thin slice of tissue on a glass slide. It came from a monkey that died in quarantine. What she sees does not look like any ordinary illness, and it troubles her because she knows exactly what it resembles.

[[story]]
Nancy Jaax was an Army veterinary pathologist working at USAMRIID in Maryland when the Reston monkeys arrived, an episode that sits at the centre of Richard Preston's *The Hot Zone*. Her job was to examine the tissue of the dying animals under a microscope. By her own account to National Geographic, the slides showed features consistent with Ebola, and she went on to perform post-mortem examinations on the sick animals.

The picture was not a single disease. The monkeys from the Philippines carried two at once. One was simian hemorrhagic fever, which is not contagious to people. The other was the Ebola relative that would later be called Reston virus. A harmless illness could have explained the deaths on its own, and the explanation sounded reasonable enough. The slides showed that it was incomplete.

At first the team assumed the virus was the Zaire strain, which is often fatal, so how dangerous the Reston virus was to humans remained unknown. Jaax says her greatest fear was that the virus would leave the building, above all through a person who had been exposed and passed it on. The quarantined monkeys had no chance of surviving, and euthanising them was, in her words, the compassionate choice, though a hard one.

Of the roughly 47 or 48 people involved when the animals were cleared out, none developed antibodies. Four or five of the animal caretakers had been exposed to the virus, but none had fallen ill. Jaax says the safety procedures worked and became a model for later responses.

Nothing about this story is glamorous. There is a glass slide, a microscope and one person's insistence on what she saw. A ready-made explanation for the monkeys' deaths was sitting there, and settling for it would have been comfortable and plausible, because once an explanation feels satisfying, people tend to stop looking.

Jaax looked at the slide itself. A second disease was present, and the person who made it visible was the one who did not believe the first explanation was enough. Large outcomes often start from something this small: looking at the raw material once more with your own eyes, and being willing to say that something else is going on here.

Notice also how the work itself was done. Jaax's job was not to hope for a finding but to describe what she saw on the glass exactly as it was. What she saw pointed to something no one expected, and she left that evidence standing instead of bending it to fit the second explanation.

[[lessons]]
$$**When you have found one known cause for a problem, check whether a second one is hiding behind it.** The Reston monkeys already carried a harmless illness that could have closed the question, yet the slides showed something more. Blaming falling sales on the season, or a recurring problem at home on one person, offers the same comfort. After you find the first explanation, make one more pass and look at the raw material yourself rather than at a summary of it. That habit catches the jobs that look finished but are only half done; one extra check before you decide costs far less than a missed cause.$$

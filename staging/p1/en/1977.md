On a spring day in Lucerne, a philosopher is alone with a young woman and asks her to marry him. She says no. The plan to share a home with her and a friend, three people with books, carries on all the same, for months to come.

[[story]]
Friedrich Nietzsche gets the chapter on difficulties in Alain de Botton's *The Consolations of Philosophy*, and one review says the book follows his struggle to find a wife. The best-documented chapter of that struggle began in Rome in April 1882. Nietzsche, then in his late thirties, met a young woman named Lou Salomé, introduced through the writer Paul Rée.

He is believed to have fallen for her almost at once. At first he asked Rée to propose on his behalf, and she refused. In May, in Lucerne, Nietzsche asked her himself, and she declined again. By her later account he asked three times in all. It is an odd detail that after the second refusal he stayed committed to the shared-household plan, and the plan lasted from spring into autumn.

Salomé had her own plan, and Nietzsche stayed loyal to it. She, Rée and Nietzsche would live and study together as an academic commune, like a household of siblings. The three travelled through Switzerland and Italy together with her mother, looking for a place, an abandoned monastery in mind. They found no suitable site, and the idea was dropped.

By October 1882 it was over. In Leipzig, Salomé and Rée left, and Nietzsche was in a state of mental anguish. His sister Elisabeth had written to the families to undermine the plan, and he blamed her, Rée and Salomé in turn. He went afterwards to Rapallo, in near-isolation and ill, and there, biographers report, he wrote the first part of *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* in about ten days.

When the plan for three fell apart, two paths lay in front of him: sit and wait, or write. The record shows the second.

Be careful with that sequence. Nobody can prove the book exists because of the rejection, and Nietzsche was a writer long before Lou Salomé. What the record supports is more modest and more useful: a man lost the person and the household he had planned around, and soon afterwards he sat down to a piece of work entirely his own.

The rejection did not make the work. But the work gave the rejection somewhere to go, and that may be all a person can ask of a bad season.

[[lessons]]
$$**After a rejection, start a piece of work that belongs only to you.** Someone whose long relationship has just ended, or whose proposal at the office was turned down, often waits for the feeling to pass before doing anything. The days that follow tend to be empty, and an empty day gives the loss all the space it wants. A project of your own, with a daily task you can finish by evening, will not undo the loss, but it gives your hands and your attention something to do, and it lets the evening end with a finished task rather than another round of replaying.$$

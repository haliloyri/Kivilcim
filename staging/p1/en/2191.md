Shortly after two in the morning, in a cluster of sod buildings on the Texas Panhandle, the ridgepole of a saloon cracks with a sound that carries through the whole settlement. The men inside are asleep, and most of them will not think much of it. Dawn will give them a reason.

[[story]]
In S. C. Gwynne's *Empire of the Summer Moon*, the trading post of Adobe Walls belongs to the final stretch of the Comanche story. The story starts weeks before the fight, at a Sun Dance in the spring of 1874. A Comanche medicine man named Isa-tai promised the warriors who attacked the buffalo hunters both victory and immunity from bullets. According to published accounts, he and Quanah Parker then led Comanche, Cheyenne and Kiowa warriors toward the post.

On the morning of June 27, 1874, there were about twenty-eight men and one woman inside. Among them were the saloon owner James Hanrahan, a twenty-year-old Bat Masterson and the hunter Billy Dixon. Published accounts say the ridgepole cracked at about two o'clock, and that Hanrahan, who suspected an attack, may have fired a shot to rouse the camp. At dawn, a force estimated at more than seven hundred charged.

The hunters answered from behind thick adobe walls with long-range rifles. Two defenders died in the first charge and one more in later ones, and a fourth was killed when his own gun went off by accident. The siege lasted four or five days. On the third, Dixon shot a warrior who was watching from a bluff. Those present put the distance at about a thousand yards; an Army survey later measured 1,538.

The numbers were lopsided in one direction and the losses in the other. A few dozen people in a few buildings faced hundreds of riders, yet the defenders lost four and held the post.

Estimates put the attackers' losses at about thirty dead, and Quanah himself was wounded. The warriors finally withdrew, and a rescue party arrived after they had gone. The fight opened the Red River War of 1874 and 1875, which ended with the Southern Plains peoples settled on reservations in what is now Oklahoma.

Look at the kind of promise that sent the warriors forward. Isa-tai did not forecast the outcome of a risky attack. He guaranteed there would be no cost. A guarantee like that cannot be checked in advance, only in the field, and in the field the answer was written in the casualties on both sides: four hunters, and roughly thirty of the men who had been told bullets would not touch them.

[[lessons]]
$$**Treat every "no risk" guarantee as a claim to test before you rely on it.** A salesperson who says "this investment can't lose", a manager who says "nothing will go wrong in the migration" or a friend who says "there's no downside" is asking you to trust confidence rather than evidence. When you hear one, ask: what sign would show us we are wrong, and what do we do then? That question does not insult anyone. It makes you a co-owner of the plan, and a promise with no answer to it is only hope wearing the costume of a plan.$$

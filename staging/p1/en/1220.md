Most New Year's resolutions are about getting something: losing weight, earning more, finally running that marathon. Janice Kaplan made a stranger one. For one year, she decided, she would be grateful. She expected it to be a pleasant experiment. She didn't expect how much it would change.

[[story]]
Janice Kaplan was a journalist, and she approached her resolution like a reporter. In *The Gratitude Diaries*, she describes spending a year deliberately trying to live gratefully, keeping a diary of what she was thankful for, and interviewing scientists who study gratitude to understand what might be happening.

She organized the year by focusing on different parts of her life in turn. She started with her marriage, then moved through family, money, work and health.

The marriage month was revealing. Kaplan realized she tended to notice what her husband did wrong, a forgotten task, an irritating habit, far more readily than what he did right. So she began paying attention to the small good things instead: the coffee he made, the way he handled a problem, his support. And she started telling him. The marriage hadn't changed much. Her attention had. And that changed how she felt, and how she treated him, which in turn changed how he responded.

To understand why this might work, she turned to researchers. One of the most important was psychologist Robert Emmons, a leading figure in the science of gratitude. In a series of studies with his colleague Michael McCullough, participants were asked to write down things they were grateful for, once a week. Compared with people who wrote about hassles or neutral events, those who wrote gratitude lists felt better about their lives overall and were more optimistic about the week ahead. In one study, they even exercised more and reported fewer physical complaints.

##Nothing in their circumstances had changed. What changed was what they were paying attention to.##

As her year went on, Kaplan came to see gratitude not as a feeling that simply arrives when good things happen, but as a practice, a choice about where to look. Life offered plenty of irritations and disappointments. It also offered many good things that were easy to overlook because they were ordinary.

By the end of the year, she felt noticeably happier. Her job, her income and her family were largely the same. But her experience of them had shifted. Writing things down, she found, made a real difference. It forced her to be specific, and specific gratitude, she discovered, is much more powerful than a vague sense that things are fine.

[[lessons]]
$$**Gratitude can be practiced.** Kaplan treated gratitude as a year-long project, not a mood. By deliberately focusing on what went right, area by area, she felt happier without her circumstances changing much. That suggests gratitude is less about waiting for good things than about learning to notice them.$$

$$**Writing it down strengthens it.** In Emmons and McCullough's studies, people who wrote weekly gratitude lists felt better and more optimistic than comparison groups. Writing forces you to be specific. "Things are fine" fades quickly; "my colleague stayed late to help me" tends to stay with you.$$

$$**Gratitude changes relationships.** Kaplan's marriage improved when she started noticing, and saying, what her husband did right. Appreciation spoken out loud changes how people feel around you, and how they treat you back. Often, the relationship that most needs gratitude is the one you take for granted.$$

- Whom in your life do you take most for granted, day after day?

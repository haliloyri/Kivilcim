A boy in Scotland has a pair of rabbits, and suddenly a whole litter of babies with nothing to eat. He has no money to buy food. But he has an idea, and it's one he'll use again, decades later, to win one of the biggest steel contracts in America.

[[story]]
In *How to Win Friends and Influence People*, Dale Carnegie tells several stories about Andrew Carnegie, the famous steel industrialist. (They weren't related, despite the name.) One quality Dale Carnegie admired in him was a deep understanding of how much people care about their own names.

It started when Andrew was a boy in Scotland. He had a mother rabbit that produced a litter of baby rabbits, and he had nothing to feed them. So he made the other boys in the neighbourhood an offer. If they would go out and gather enough clover and dandelions to feed the rabbits, he would name the baby rabbits after them.

The plan worked beautifully. The boys went out and gathered food. The chance to have a rabbit named after them was worth more to them than any payment.

##Andrew Carnegie never forgot what that taught him about people.##

Years later, as a businessman, he wanted to sell steel rails to the Pennsylvania Railroad. Its president was a man named J. Edgar Thomson. Carnegie built a huge new steel mill in Pittsburgh, and gave it a name: the Edgar Thomson Steel Works.

When the Pennsylvania Railroad needed steel rails, Dale Carnegie writes, it's not hard to guess where J. Edgar Thomson bought them.

Dale Carnegie gives other examples of people who understood the power of a name. One was Jim Farley, a political organizer who was said to know the first names of around 50,000 people. When he met someone, he took the trouble to learn their full name and some details about their family and work. The next time he saw them, even a year later, he could greet them by name and ask about their family. People remembered that.

From all of this, Carnegie draws one of the most famous principles in his book: remember that a person's name is to that person the sweetest and most important sound in any language.

The point isn't to manipulate people with flattery. It's that using someone's name, remembering it, honoring it, tells them they matter to you as an individual. Most people spend much of their lives feeling like one face in a crowd. Being recognized by name, especially by someone busy or important, is a small moment that says: *I see you.*

[[lessons]]
$$**People care deeply about their names.** The neighbourhood boys worked for free because a rabbit would carry their name. Dale Carnegie's principle is that a name is the sweetest sound to its owner. Recognizing that isn't a trick. It's understanding a basic human need to be seen as an individual.$$

$$**Honoring a name builds goodwill.** Naming a steel mill after the railroad president was a grand gesture, but the principle scales down. Crediting a colleague by name, remembering a client's children's names, or thanking someone specifically all say "you matter." People remember how that made them feel.$$

$$**Remembering names is a discipline.** Jim Farley's 50,000 names didn't come from a magic memory. He deliberately took time to learn names and details. Most of us forget names because we don't make the effort to fix them in our minds when we first hear them.$$

[[reflect]]
- Whose name could you honor, use or credit more often?
- How do you feel when someone very busy remembers your name?
- Whose name do you keep forgetting, and what would actually help you remember it?

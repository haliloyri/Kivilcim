A pastor and marriage counselor in North Carolina clears his desk and pulls out the session notes he has been keeping for years. He is not hoping for a new idea. He wants to know what keeps coming back, the complaint he has heard in his office so often that it has started to blur.

[[story]]
Gary Chapman's *The 5 Love Languages* came out in 1992, but it began as a reading project. Chapman kept seeing the same scene in his counseling office: one partner said "I don't feel loved," and the other insisted they were doing everything they could. To work out why, he told Psychotherapy Networker, he sat down and read through 12 years of his own session notes.

The notes held nothing new. They held the same thing, over and over, and no single page gave it away. Only the repetition across years made it visible. People who felt unloved described their complaints in different words, yet the complaints sorted into five groups. Chapman named them the five love languages: words of affirmation, quality time, receiving gifts, acts of service and physical touch.

The logic behind the five is simple. "You don't love me" does not mean the same thing in every home. For one person it means "you never say anything kind to me." For another it means "we never spend time together," and for a third, "you don't help around the house." The love was there, but it was not being spoken in a language the other person could hear.

Chapman did not rush to print. First he asked couples to work out each other's love language, and one of them told him, "Gary, this has changed everything." Then he used the idea in small groups and saw similar results.

About five years after he began working with couples, he decided to write a book. He wanted to reach couples who would never sit in his office, so he wrote in plain language, without psychological jargon. By the time the Networker article appeared in 2016, the book had sold more than 10 million copies and been translated into 50 languages.

What stands out is where the evidence was kept. It sat in Chapman's own notes, in his own office, for years. Each session showed one couple's trouble, and only the whole stack showed the pattern.

There is a second detail in the order of events. The idea went through couples and small groups before it went into print, so by the time it reached paper, other people's marriages had already tested it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you hunt for a new answer, reread your own records from start to finish.** A customer complaint, an argument that keeps recurring in meetings or the same conversation at home looks like a separate event each time it happens. Read a year of your emails, notes or journal in one sitting, and you may find one sentence wearing different clothes. That lets you stop gathering new information and start working on what you already hold, and you can try the pattern on a small scale first, with one team or one person, before you build a whole plan around it.$$

Inside the bank, the three deals had code names: Magnolia, Maximus and Catalyze. On paper they were bond sales for a Malaysian state fund. Years later the bank admitted that its own control staff had known one thing about them all along: any deal involving a certain man carried significant risk.

[[story]]
Tom Wright and Bradley Hope's *Billion Dollar Whale* describes how Goldman Sachs came to sell bonds for 1MDB, and this story is about what the bank itself admitted in October 2020. That month The Goldman Sachs Group entered a deferred prosecution agreement with the US Justice Department, and its Malaysian subsidiary, Goldman Sachs (Malaysia), pleaded guilty to conspiring to violate the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act.

The deals ran between 2012 and 2013. Inside Goldman they were called Project Magnolia, Project Maximus and Project Catalyze, and together the three bond sales were worth about $6.5 billion. The bank earned approximately $606 million in fees and revenue. Goldman's own statement of facts said it had also increased its stature and presence in Southeast Asia.

One man connected the bank to the fund: Low Taek Jho, known as Jho Low, who held no formal position at 1MDB. Goldman admitted that, through bankers including Tim Leissner and Roger Ng, it used Low's connections to advance the scheme, and that this helped it win a role on all three deals. According to the department, more than $2.7 billion was diverted from the bond offerings, and Goldman admitted that its people conspired with Low to promise and pay over $1.6 billion in bribes to officials in Malaysia and Abu Dhabi. Low was indicted and, when the settlement was announced, remained a fugitive.

The part that matters for the rest of us is the paperwork inside the bank. Goldman admitted that employees in its control functions knew any transaction involving Low posed a significant risk, and that they were on notice he was involved. They did not take reasonable steps to make sure he was not. Red flags raised during due diligence and afterwards, including Low's involvement, were ignored or only nominally addressed so that the transactions would be approved and the bank could continue to do business with 1MDB.

So the warnings were not missing. People had seen them, named them and written them down. What failed was the step after: no one had the power, or the incentive, to turn a warning into a stop. A flag that can only be noted, never acted on, works as a receipt for the decision that was going to happen anyway.

[[lessons]]
$$**Decide before the deal what a red flag is allowed to stop.** A person who warns you only matters if the warning can change the outcome, and that has to be agreed while nothing is yet at stake. Suppose a small design studio's bookkeeper notices that a big new client keeps paying through an unfamiliar company. If the studio has already decided that unexplained payers pause the contract until someone answers, her remark is a lever, and if not, it is one more thing everyone nods at before signing. A rule written down in advance protects both the person who raises the concern and the people who would rather not hear it.$$

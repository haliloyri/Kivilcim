Two managers sit down together to staff a computer project. They could go after veterans from other companies. Instead they hire people who have barely left school, and and the team that results gets nicknames that sound like they belong to a children's mystery series.

[[story]]
Tom West and Carl Alsing built the Eagle team in an unusual way. According to Wired's retrospective on Tracy Kidder's *The Soul of a New Machine*, they gathered a group of managers and then hired about two dozen young engineers, most of them straight out of college. The hardware designers became known as the Hardy Boys. The microcode engineers, the people who wrote the lowest layer of instructions inside the machine, became the Microkids.

West had a name for what he wanted from them. He called it "signing up". By his account, the phrase meant giving yourself over so completely to the machine that your life shrank around it. Nobody on the team was drafted into that. The point of the word was that each person chose it.

Jonathan Blau, one of the engineers, later described the project in a single phrase: it had "that boot camp quality that can't really be repeated." Boot camp is a fitting picture. It is short, it is demanding, and the people who go through it come out knowing something that outsiders cannot quite follow.

Kidder's book became one of the best-known accounts of that way of working, and it won the 1982 Pulitzer Prize for General Nonfiction. Its title points at the same thing West was after, the soul of the machine, which comes from the hours that people choose to put into it.

The staffing choice and the phrase belong together. A team of newcomers, many of them in a first job, has no habits to protect and no previous employer's way of doing things to defend, and a leader who says "sign up" is asking for a decision, not obedience. Because each engineer made the decision for themselves, and could see what it demanded, the long hours did not have to be extracted from them one at a time.

The same logic has a cost, and the story does not hide it. A commitment that total can only be borrowed for a while, which is what Blau meant when he said it could not really be repeated. Eagle's engineers later scattered to different companies and, in Blau's case, out of high technology altogether.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask people to choose a hard commitment instead of assigning it to them.** A volunteer coordinator who tells a group exactly how many weekends the cleanup will take, and then asks who is in, ends up with fewer people but a crew that turns up. A task handed down gets done to the letter, while a task someone has said yes to gets their own ideas as well. Say plainly what the commitment costs and for how long, before anyone answers, so that the yes really means something and the end date is just as clear to everyone as the start.$$

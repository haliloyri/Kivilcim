Picture Seattle in 1968. A teenager sits at a computer terminal at his high school, typing, losing track of time. Almost no teenager anywhere could do what he's doing. He doesn't know that. He just knows he loves it. Now picture the kid sitting beside him, every bit as sharp and every bit as hooked. Hold on to both of them, because only one gets the ending you'd expect.

[[story]]
The first kid is Bill Gates, and the school is Lakeside School in Seattle. Morgan Housel opens the "Luck & Risk" chapter of *The Psychology of Money* here, and he starts with a detail that's easy to skip: Lakeside had a computer.

In 1968 that was extraordinary. Very few high schools in the world had access to one. For most teenagers, a computer was something you might read about, not something you could sit down at after class. Lakeside's students could. Gates and a handful of friends, Paul Allen among them, took full advantage. They spent enormous amounts of time on the machine, and that time turned out to be decisive for what later became Microsoft.

Gates has never pretended otherwise. Decades later, in 2005, he spoke to Lakeside's graduating class and put it plainly:

> "If there had been no Lakeside, there would have been no Microsoft."

Housel is careful here, and you should be too. He isn't saying Gates got lucky and nothing else. Gates had exceptional talent and relentless drive. The point is that those qualities met an opportunity so rare that almost nobody his age was ever offered it. Talent met the right room at the right moment.

Then Housel tells you about the second kid.

His name was Kent Evans. He was Gates's close friend at Lakeside and his early partner in computing. By Gates's own account, Evans was just as smart and just as driven. They were the kind of pair who finish each other's projects, and Gates believed they would have kept working together. If you were placing bets on which Lakeside students would build something world-changing, you'd have put Evans's name right next to Gates's.

Kent Evans never graduated from high school. He died in a mountaineering accident before he got the chance.

##Same school, same rare computer, same brilliance, same hunger. One became one of the richest people in history, and the other died on a mountain as a teenager.##

Housel sets the two stories side by side on purpose. Gates's story, he says, is luck: a force outside his control that pushed him forward. Evans's story is the flip side of luck, which is risk: a force outside his control that took everything away. Neither boy earned his outcome in the way we usually mean when we say someone "earned" something. Both worked hard. Both were gifted. What separated them was something neither could choose.

That's why Housel draws a conclusion that sounds almost too simple. Success is not as good as it seems, and failure is not as bad as it seems. Be careful whom you praise and admire. Be careful whom you look down on. And when you want to learn how things work, look at broad patterns across many people instead of the one extreme case everyone talks about, because the extreme cases are exactly where luck and risk do the most work.

Most people know the name Bill Gates. Almost nobody knows the name Kent Evans. The talent was the same, but only one of them got to show it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Luck and risk are one force pointing two ways.** Gates got a seat at one of the rarest computers any teenager could reach. Evans got caught by a fatal accident. Housel treats these as mirror images: things beyond effort that shaped the outcome as strongly as effort did. If you only notice luck when it lifts someone, you'll miss risk when it quietly knocks someone down.$$

$$**Extreme success needs talent and improbable circumstances together.** Gates's own verdict, "no Lakeside, no Microsoft," comes from the person with the most reason to take full credit. He was brilliant and driven, and that still wasn't enough by itself. The opportunity wasn't enough by itself either. The outlier result came from the two meeting, and that combination is exactly what nobody can plan for.$$

$$**Judge carefully in both directions, and learn from patterns.** Evans was as capable as Gates, yet history barely remembers him. So a great outcome doesn't prove great decisions, and a bad one doesn't prove bad ones. Housel's warning follows from this: don't copy the playbook of a single outlier, because the most extreme stories are the ones luck shaped most. Habits shared by many successful people are a safer guide.$$

[[reflect]]
- List two advantages you have that you never chose: a school, a city, a parent's contact, the year you entered your field.
- Think of someone whose career or business stalled. What risks did they face that you never saw?
- Name a success story you've been quietly modeling your plans on. Which parts of it depended on timing or access you don't have?
- Picture your "Kent Evans," someone as capable as you whose path broke for reasons outside their control.

&&When you tell the story of your proudest achievement, which parts do you leave out because they happened to you rather than because of you?&&

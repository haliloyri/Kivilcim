October 1962. On the president's desk sit reconnaissance photographs of Soviet nuclear missile sites being built in Cuba. Around him, advisers with serious credentials are saying the same thing: strike now, before the sites are finished. John F. Kennedy has every reason to act fast. What he does instead is the reason Ryan Holiday opens a whole book with this moment.

[[story]]
Ryan Holiday starts *Stillness Is the Key* not in a monastery or on a mountain, but in the White House during the Cuban Missile Crisis.

The facts arrived as pictures. U.S. reconnaissance flights had photographed Soviet nuclear missile sites under construction in Cuba. The discovery raised the stakes about as high as they can go, and the room reacted accordingly.

Many of Kennedy's military and political advisers pressed for an immediate response. An air strike to take out the sites. Or an invasion. The case for both was easy to make: the threat was real, the clock was running, and moving first looked like strength. Waiting, by contrast, looked like hesitation.

Kennedy did something that, in that atmosphere, was harder than giving an order. He deliberately slowed the process down.

He gathered a group of advisers and let them debate the options over several days. He did not react on the first impulse, even though the first impulse was the one most people in the room were pushing for.

Holiday points to one piece of Kennedy's background that helps explain the restraint. Kennedy had read Barbara Tuchman's *The Guns of August*, a history of how the great powers blundered into the First World War. Nobody in that story set out to start a catastrophe, yet they arrived at one. Kennedy was wary of repeating that kind of escalation.

The days of deliberation did something that a quick decision could not have done. They made room for an option that sat between attacking and doing nothing.

##Kennedy chose a naval blockade, which was called a "quarantine," over an attack, and that choice gave both sides time.##

The quarantine did not end the crisis on the spot. It bought time instead of forcing the next step. The crisis ended with the Soviets agreeing to remove the missiles.

Holiday uses the episode as the doorway into his central argument. What he calls stillness is the ability to be steady and to think clearly under pressure. In his account, that is not a nice-to-have quality for calm moments. It is what makes good decisions possible in exactly the moments when everyone is shouting for speed.

The detail worth holding onto is simple. The blockade was not the first thing on the table. It came out of the pause, after days of argument that the first recommendation would have cut short.

[[lessons]]
$$**The loudest option comes first.** When the photographs landed, the options that surfaced fastest were the most forceful ones: an air strike or an invasion. That is typical under pressure. The first idea tends to be the one with the most energy behind it, and energy is easy to confuse with correctness. Kennedy's advisers were not wrong to raise those options. The mistake would have been treating the first idea as the only one.$$

$$**A pause can create options.** The blockade came out of several days of debate. Urgency narrows the field to "act now" or "do nothing," while time lets a third path appear. Slowing down in a crisis is not the same as ignoring it. Kennedy kept working the problem. He simply refused to let the deadline in his gut set the deadline for the decision.$$

$$**Other people's mistakes are a map.** Kennedy had read how the great powers blundered into the First World War. That history gave him a picture of what escalation looks like from the inside, one reasonable-seeming step after another. Knowing how others went wrong under pressure is a way of recognizing the pattern before you are halfway down it.$$

[[reflect]]
- When you feel pushed to decide quickly, who or what is actually doing the pushing?
- Think of a decision that turned out better because you slept on it. What showed up overnight that wasn't there before?
- Is there a choice in front of you right now where you've only considered the most obvious option?

&&Before your next big decision, what is one option besides the obvious one that you could put on the table first?&&

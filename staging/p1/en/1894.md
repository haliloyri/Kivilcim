A morsel of food hangs on a string inside a man's stomach. Every so often a surgeon pulls the string up, looks at what is left of the food and notes it down. The man is a paid servant in the surgeon's house, and the opening in his side has never closed.

[[story]]
On 6 June 1822 a shotgun went off at Fort Mackinac, in what was then Michigan Territory, and its full charge hit Alexis St. Martin, a French Canadian trapper of nineteen, in the left side. The army surgeon William Beaumont was called. He did not expect the young man to live.

St. Martin lived. Beaumont dressed the wound with flour and charcoal poultices, cleaned it, removed debris and bled him against the fever, and after about ten months it had healed, mostly. What remained was a permanent opening, nearly an inch wide, that led straight into the stomach. Beaumont could look in.

He did, for years. Beginning in 1825 he lowered food into the stomach on a string, took it out at timed intervals and weighed what was left. Over about ten years, with St. Martin's cooperation coming and going, he ran more than 250 experiments at several army posts. He saw that gastric juice was absent when the stomach was empty, that liquids left it quickly, and that a man's mood changed how much juice the stomach made. Chemists who analysed samples confirmed free hydrochloric acid in it.

The conclusion, set out in his 1833 book, *Experiments and Observations on the Gastric Juice and the Physiology of Digestion*, was that digestion is a chemical process, not merely a grinding one. It was one of the first long, careful looks at a working human stomach, and it was possible only because one body had a window in it.

The other half of the account is less comfortable. St. Martin lived and worked in Beaumont's household in exchange for room, board and pay, and four contracts signed by both men explicitly allowed the experiments. Yet the experiments were often painful, and Beaumont was St. Martin's only source of food, housing and income. Historians writing for the Becker Medical Library at Washington University argue that he could hardly be called a free volunteer, and they call the whole arrangement something that could not be considered ethical. Why the wound was never closed is, they note, a matter of speculation.

St. Martin returned to Canada for good in 1834. Beaumont left the army around 1839 and set up in general practice in St. Louis.

The paperwork is the instructive part. A contract can record that two people agreed and still leave open whether both were free to say no. When one side supplies the meals, the roof and the wages, a signature tells you less than it appears to.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask who can say no to you before you decide that someone has agreed.** A shop owner whose assistant is always willing to stay late may be hearing a yes that fear of losing the job has shaped, not a free choice. When one person controls another's pay, housing or reputation, enthusiasm and silence both need a second reading, because refusing costs the weaker side far more. Make it easy to decline, ask in a way that leaves a real exit, and treat a quick yes from someone who depends on you as a reason to double-check that it was freely given.$$

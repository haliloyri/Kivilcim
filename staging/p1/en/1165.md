In 1968, a group of mothers at a private school in Seattle held a rummage sale. With the money, they bought something almost no school had: time on a computer. One of the students who wandered in to see it was a thirteen-year-old named Bill Gates.

[[story]]
Malcolm Gladwell tells this story in *Outliers* to make a point about success that many people find uncomfortable. We love to credit extraordinary people with extraordinary talent. Gladwell wants us to also look at their extraordinary opportunities.

The school was Lakeside, in Seattle. Its Mothers' Club used the proceeds from their sale to pay for a computer terminal connected by phone line to a mainframe downtown. It sounds modest, but in 1968 it was remarkable.

At the time, most programming was done with punch cards. You typed your program onto cards, handed them in, and waited, sometimes for hours or a day, to discover whether it worked. A single mistake could cost you an entire day.

The Lakeside terminal used a newer system called time-sharing. You typed a command and saw the result almost immediately. Mistakes cost seconds instead of days. You could try, fail and try again, over and over, in a single afternoon.

##Bill Gates was in eighth grade. From the moment he saw that terminal, he could hardly be pulled away from it.##

The money soon ran out. But Gates and his friends didn't stop. They found a company in Seattle called C-Cubed that let them use its computers for free, in exchange for finding bugs in its software. Later, another company, ISI, gave them computer time in exchange for writing a payroll program. Gates also discovered that a computer at the University of Washington sat unused between about three and six in the morning. So he would slip out of his house at night and walk to the university to program while everyone else slept.

Gladwell adds up this unusual chain of luck: a school with a terminal, companies willing to trade access for work, a university computer free in the middle of the night. By the time Gates dropped out of Harvard to start Microsoft, Gladwell estimates, he had been programming practically nonstop for seven years, far beyond ten thousand hours.

How many teenagers in the world had that kind of access in the late 1960s? Gladwell's answer: very few.

But there's a second half to the story that's easy to overlook. The opportunities didn't simply fall into Gates's lap one after another. After the first one, he went looking for them. He traded his skills for access. He got up at three in the morning. The luck was real. So was what he did with it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Every success story has an opportunity in it.** Gates was brilliant, but he also stood in front of a time-sharing terminal in 1968, something most universities didn't have. Gladwell's point isn't that talent doesn't matter. It's that we should notice the doors that were opened, for ourselves and for others, instead of pretending they weren't there.$$

$$**Fast feedback speeds up learning.** Punch cards meant waiting a day to learn you'd made a mistake. Time-sharing meant seconds. That difference let a teenager do in a week what used to take months. Wherever you're learning something, shortening the loop between trying and finding out is one of the most powerful changes you can make.$$

$$**Access given, access pursued.** The mothers gave Gates his first chance. After that, he chased the rest: bug-hunting at C-Cubed, a payroll program, a 3 a.m. university computer. Luck opened the first door. Persistence found the next ones. Both halves of the story matter.$$

[[reflect]]
- What opportunity have you received in life that you rarely give credit for?
- Where are you waiting for access when you could be chasing it?
- How long is the feedback loop in the skill you're trying to learn, and could it be shorter?

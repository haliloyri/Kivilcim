A mission hospital in northern Zaire. A feverish patient comes to the outpatient clinic and is given an injection against suspected malaria. It is an ordinary treatment on an ordinary day, and no one there imagines that this needle will turn out to be the first link in a chain.

[[story]]
Of the 17 staff at the Yambuku Mission Hospital, 11 died in the autumn of 1976. That figure comes from the report of an international commission, published by the World Health Organization in 1978, and it explains why the story of Ebola's discovery is also a story about how a routine can carry a disease. Richard Preston's *The Hot Zone* traces Ebola back to this outbreak in what is now the northern Democratic Republic of the Congo.

According to the report, the first patient began to feel ill on 1 September 1976, five days after receiving a chloroquine injection at the hospital's outpatient clinic for suspected malaria. Within a week, others who had been given injections at the same hospital fell ill as well. Nearly all later cases had either been injected there or had close contact with a sick person.

The scale was heavy. Between 1 September and 24 October 1976, 318 people developed a haemorrhagic fever, most of them within 70 kilometres of Yambuku, and 280 died. The hospital closed after about four weeks. Only 38 people were confirmed by blood tests to have survived the infection.

The CDC describes the spread as running through contaminated needles and syringes at hospitals and clinics, and through close contact with the sick. Nobody in that chain set out to do harm, and nobody made a dramatic mistake. At the start stood an ordinary medical act, an injection, and that act became an invisible route of infection.

What makes the account hard to forget is how ordinary the link was. A routine that is repeated every day is rarely questioned. Each injection carried the disease through a normal piece of health care to the next person in line. The commission's most important finding was less about where the illness began than about which everyday procedure had spread it.

The logic of such an outbreak is that a problem often travels inside the most familiar movements. The more often a procedure is done, the more people the risk inside it can reach, and the fewer eyes notice it.

The practical lesson is to look for an outbreak in the ordinary movements that carry it, as well as in the illness itself. To stop its spread you first have to notice the movement, and that happens only when you look again at what everyone calls routine.

[[lessons]]
$$**To find a risk that spreads inside a routine, ask at every step: what does this pass on to the next one?** At Yambuku an injection was an everyday step that carried the disease from one person to another, and for that reason nobody looked at it with suspicion. A form everyone signs at a company, an item shared in a household or a regular handover can carry a problem along just as silently. Put frequently repeated tasks under the lens from time to time, and write down what each one hands to the next step. That view ties the problem to the process more than to a person.$$

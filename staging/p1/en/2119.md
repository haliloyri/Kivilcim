In a Berlin lecture room, a scientist finishes his talk to a hall of doctors and nobody applauds. Nobody argues either, which is stranger. A young physician in the audience goes home and spends the whole night working out a new way to stain what he has just heard described.

[[story]]
On 24 March 1882 Robert Koch stood before the Physiological Society of Berlin and told its members what causes tuberculosis. According to the paper he published afterwards, about one person in seven died of the disease. It was a common killer, and nobody had yet shown what caused it.

The idea that TB was infectious was already old, and researchers had shown it could be passed to animals. But whether a micro-organism was responsible was contested, even by very distinguished investigators. Koch, who worked at the new Health Department in Berlin, had taken tuberculosis on himself, and his lecture settled it in what the Nobel committee later described as scarcely two pages of print.

He had found a way to see the bacillus. In his method an alkaline methylene blue solution stained the bacteria blue, while a brown dye called vesuvin coloured the surrounding tissue, so the organism stood out against its background. He found it every time he looked at tuberculous tissue, in people and in animals.

Then he grew it. He raised pure cultures of the bacillus, and when he inoculated animals with them, they developed the disease. The loop was closed: the organism seen in the sick tissue, grown on its own, gave tuberculosis to a healthy animal. He added that the bacillus lives off the living body, so that infection comes mainly from the coughed-up sputum of people with consumption.

The room, by accounts of the evening, was stunned, too aware of hearing something historic to applaud or object as was the custom. Paul Ehrlich, then in his twenties, later called it the most gripping experience of his scientific life. He went home and spent the night developing a better stain for the bacillus.

Within a month Koch published the lecture, on 10 April 1882, in the Berliner klinische Wochenschrift. In 1905 he received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for his work on tuberculosis.

A cause is not a cure, and the cure took decades more. But before anyone could fight the bacillus, someone had to make it visible and show, step by step, that it was the culprit. Koch's lecture handed his colleagues the stain, the culture and the animal test, so that anyone with a microscope could check it for themselves.

[[lessons]]
$$**Hand over the procedure together with the conclusion.** A claim that comes with its method can be tested by anyone, and the people who test it often improve it, as the young physician did that very night. Someone who has perfected a bread recipe, for instance, gets more out of writing down the temperatures, the timings and the flour than out of announcing that the loaf is the best in town, because other bakers can then repeat it and tell her where it fails. A conclusion shared this way recruits helpers, while a bare one only asks for trust and leaves you to defend it alone.$$

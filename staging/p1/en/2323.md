On a barren hillside in Haiti's Central Plateau, families who once farmed the valley floor now squat on other people's slope. Below them lies a lake that was not there a generation before. Years later, a two-room clinic will open among them, at the edge of the water that took their land.

[[story]]
Tracy Kidder gives a whole part of *Mountains Beyond Mountains*, "The Tin Roofs of Cange," to the village where Paul Farmer built his life's work. To understand why the clinic is there, start with the water.

In the mid-1950s, according to published accounts, Haiti's largest river was dammed as part of an international development project. The reservoir flooded the fertile valley and the village of Cange with it. The residents were subsistence farmers, and by one account they received little or no compensation for their homes and land. They climbed the barren hillside above the lake and settled there as squatters.

Farmer later called the project costly and, in the end, a failure. Villagers told him the dam had brought them neither electricity nor water. A plan made far away had given the country a reservoir and left the people beside it with less than they had before.

Farmer came to Cange as a young man, after graduating from Duke, and volunteered at a hospital there. In 1985 he and his colleagues opened the Clinique Bon Sauveur, a two-room clinic. At about the same time, health workers in Cange identified the community's first cases of AIDS. A clinic built for ordinary illness already had a second disease standing at its door.

The two rooms grew. The small clinic that began treating patients in 1985 is today the Zanmi Lasante sociomedical complex, a 104-bed hospital, and its site is among the people displaced by the flooded valley. Zanmi Lasante is Haitian Creole for Partners In Health. Over the years the organization also put tin roofs on village houses, which is where Kidder's chapter title comes from.

Consider how that sequence reads. An outside project took land from farmers for the sake of a benefit that, in their account, never reached them. An outside project then came back, this time to the same hillside, and built what the earlier one had not provided. Nothing in that account needs bad intent; a plan can fail the people beside it simply because they were never the ones it was drawn for. The difference between the two was not the foreign money. It was who the work was for, and whether the people who lived beside it could see it and use it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you judge a plan, ask who paid its cost and whether they ever saw its benefit.** A dam can look like progress on a map and still leave the farmers beside it poorer. The same test works on a reorganization, a new tool or a change of schedule, because the people who bear the cost are rarely in the room where it is approved. Ask them early, in their own words, what they lose and what they gain. If nobody can say what they gain, the plan is not finished yet, and asking before approval costs an afternoon while repairing a decision afterwards can cost years.$$

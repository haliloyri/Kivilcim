[[open]]
Picture the first day of a photography class. Before you've touched a camera, the professor tells you how you'll be graded. Half the room will be judged on a single photograph. The other half will be judged only on how many photographs they take. Which half would you pick? Hold on to your answer, because by the end of the term the choice turns out to matter a lot more than it seems.

[[story]]
James Clear tells this story in *Atomic Habits*, in a chapter titled "Walk Slowly, but Never Backward." It centres on Jerry Uelsmann, who taught film photography at the University of Florida.

On the first day of class, Uelsmann split his students into two groups.

The first was the "quantity" group. The only thing that counted for their grade was how many photographs they produced. Take more photos, get a higher grade. Nobody would judge whether any single picture was good.

The second was the "quality" group. They had a much lighter load on paper: one photograph, due at the end of the term. But that one photo had to be close to perfect if they wanted an A.

You can guess what most of us would expect. The quality group had the whole term to focus on a single image. They could think it through, plan it carefully and get it right. The quantity group was just churning out volume.

##At the end of the term, the best photos all came from the quantity group.##

Clear explains why. The quantity group spent the term actually taking pictures. They shot constantly. They experimented with composition and lighting, tried different methods and got things wrong. Then they learned from what went wrong and shot again. Every roll of film was another rep, and the reps added up to skill.

The quality group spent much of the term doing something else. They theorised about what the perfect photo would be. They speculated. They talked about ideas they never tested. When the term ended, they had little to show for all that thinking beyond unverified ideas and mediocre work.

Clear uses the story to draw a line between two things that are easy to confuse. The first is **motion**, which means planning, strategising and researching. It feels like work, and in a sense it is. The second is **action**, the behaviour that actually produces a result. The quality group had plenty of motion. The quantity group had action.

Clear points out why motion is so tempting. When you're planning, nobody can criticise the outcome, because there isn't one yet. Action exposes you. A photo you actually take can be bad, and you'll know it. Staying in motion lets you feel productive while quietly putting off the chance of failing.

His conclusion is simple. Habits and skills form through repetition. If you want to get good at something, the place to start is repetition, not perfection. The students who were told to aim lower and do more ended up producing the work that was actually better.

[[lessons]]
$$**Planning is motion, not progress.** The quality group was busy all term. They thought hard about the perfect photograph and talked through their ideas. But none of that produced a good picture. Clear's point is that motion can look exactly like work from the inside. The test is whether it produces a result. The quality group's term of theorising left them with ideas nobody had tested and work that was ordinary.$$

$$**The reps are where the skill comes from.** The quantity group didn't get better because someone taught them the right theory first. They got better because they kept shooting, tried different lighting and compositions, and learned from their mistakes. Each photo was a small experiment with a real result. Quality turned out to be a side effect of quantity, which is why the best photos came from the group that was never graded on quality at all.$$

$$**Motion is a safe way to delay failure.** Clear notes that we often stay in planning mode because action exposes us to criticism. A plan can't be bad yet. A photograph can. The quality group's setup made every attempt feel high-stakes, so the safest move was to keep thinking. The quantity group had no such shelter. They had to produce, so they risked bad results every day, and that risk is what taught them.$$

[[reflect]]
- Think of a project you've been "preparing" for a while. How much of that preparation is research, and how much is a way of not starting yet?
- What would one rep of it look like today, even a clumsy one? That might be one sketch, one paragraph or one short run.
- Which rules are you grading yourself by right now: the single perfect photo, or the count?
- Where has fear of a bad result quietly turned into more planning?

&&If you were graded only on how many times you did it, not how well, what would you start doing tomorrow?&&

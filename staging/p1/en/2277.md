At a door in south London, a man fills a small glass phial from the tap, writes the address on its cover and carries it home. Tenants cannot tell him which water company supplies them, because their landlord pays the bill. So he will let the water itself say, with a few drops of silver nitrate.

[[story]]
The man is John Snow, and by the 1850s London already had a respected explanation for why some districts suffered from cholera more than others. William Farr, who compiled the statistics at the General Register Office, had noticed a pattern in the epidemic of 1849: the lower the ground, the higher the death toll. Farr was inclined to think the level of the soil itself had some direct influence. The numbers were real, and they fitted the old belief that bad air settled in low places.

Snow had an answer to hand. High towns such as Wolverhampton and Merthyr Tydfil had suffered badly on several occasions, while Bethlehem Hospital and the Queen's Prison stood on very low ground, drew water from deep wells on their own premises, and nearly escaped. Brixton, fifty-six feet above high-water mark, lost 55 in every 10,000, more than districts north of the Thames at less than half its height. Elevation was a stand-in for something else, he argued: how contaminated the water was. Steven Johnson's *The Ghost Map* follows Snow's search for the cause of cholera, and this comparison is one of the central pieces of his work.

To prove it he needed a case where everything stayed equal except the water. London gave him one. Two companies, the Southwark and Vauxhall and the Lambeth, laid their pipes down the same streets, and in many cases a single house had a different supply from its neighbours on both sides. One company drew from the Thames where it carried London's sewage; the other had moved its intake up to Thames Ditton. No fewer than three hundred thousand people, in Snow's words, had been divided into two groups without their choice and mostly without their knowledge.

He began in Kennington. Of forty-four deaths up to 12 August, thirty-eight were in houses served by the Southwark and Vauxhall, four by the Lambeth and two by private wells. He took the result to Farr, who was, in Snow's words, much struck by it. At Farr's suggestion, the registrars of the south districts were asked to record the water supply of each house in which a death occurred.

Where tenants could not say, Snow tested. The Lambeth water gave 2.8 grains of silver chloride per gallon with silver nitrate, the Southwark and Vauxhall water 91. Over the first seven weeks, he found, the Southwark and Vauxhall's customers died at 315 per 10,000 houses, the Lambeth's at 37, and the rest of London at 59.

Farr's elevation pattern was a clue that pointed the right way for the wrong reason. Snow's two companies removed the doubt, because the same street and the same mix of rich and poor were split only by a pipe.

[[lessons]]
$$**Look for a case where only one factor changes, then compare.** A pattern such as Farr's elevation finding can be perfectly accurate and still point to the wrong cause, because many things travel together on the same hillside. Snow stopped arguing about hills and went looking for houses that differed in one respect only, the water. You can borrow the move at work: when two teams, two stores or two months show different results, find the pair that differs in just one thing before you decide what caused the gap. A single clean contrast persuades people that a long argument cannot.$$

[[open]]
Visitors to a refectory in Milan are let in a small group at a time, and each group gets about fifteen minutes in front of one wall. Nobody touches it. The air is managed. The wall carries one of the most famous paintings in the world, and it is fragile, because the painter tried something new on it.

[[story]]
Walter Isaacson's *Leonardo da Vinci* follows the painter's experiments, and the Last Supper is one of the most expensive of them. Leonardo painted it in the 1490s on the wall of the refectory of Santa Maria delle Grazie in Milan, for Ludovico Sforza. The wall is still there. The paint has had a hard time.

For a wall, the standard method was buon fresco. The painter lays colour on wet plaster, and as the plaster dries the pigment becomes part of the wall. It is durable, but it forces speed. Each section has to be finished before the plaster sets, and there is little room to change anything afterwards.

Leonardo chose differently. He worked with tempera and oil on dry plaster, a method that let him work slowly and rework passages, as he could on a panel. Fresco does not allow that. On a wall, though, it carries a price. The paint does not bond into the plaster; it sits on top of it.

That price came due early. Published accounts say the paint began to flake during Leonardo's own lifetime, and over the following centuries the surface was damaged, repaired and repainted many times. A long restoration, led by Pinin Brambilla Barcilon, lasted about twenty years and ended in 1999. Its goal was to stop further decay and to uncover as much of Leonardo's own painting as could be recovered, rather than to add new paint.

Fresco is normally painted in daily patches, each only as large as the painter can finish before the plaster dries. A painter who wants to step back, look at the whole table and adjust a face has little room for that. Leonardo's choice removed the clock. A wall, unlike a panel, cannot be carried to a workshop and replaced if it fails.

Even now the wall needs protection. That is why the groups are small and the visit is short.

It is tempting to read this as a story about a failed experiment, but the better reading is about timing. The method fitted the painter's wish to take his time and perfect the picture, and it did not fit the job, which was a wall that had to last for centuries. Each choice made sense on its own terms. What was missing was a test of the method against the lifespan the work would need.

[[lessons]]
$$**Test a method against how long the result has to last.** A shortcut that makes the work easier today can cost you years later, and the bill often arrives after you have moved on to other things. Leonardo's technique gave him freedom while he painted and left the wall weak for everyone after him. Before you adopt a clever process for a report, a system or a house repair, ask how it will look in five years and who will have to maintain it. If you cannot say, try it on something small first, and let it age a little before you commit.$$

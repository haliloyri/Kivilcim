Early in the morning, in a camp far from Rome, a man sits down with his notebook and lists the people who will annoy him before dinner. He writes them out one by one: the busybody, the ungrateful, the slanderer, the crafty, the envious. Then he gives himself a reason not to be angry at any of them.

[[story]]
The note above the passage reads, in the old English translation, "In the country of the Quadi at Granua." The Quadi were a Germanic tribe north of the Danube, and the Granua is one of that river's tributaries. Marcus Aurelius, by then emperor, was away from Rome on a frontier campaign, and his notebook, which we call the *Meditations*, carries notes like this one about where parts of it were written.

The entry reads like a rehearsal. In the morning, he tells himself, you will meet an idle, curious man, an ungrateful one, a railer, a crafty, false or envious man, an unsociable man with no charity. They behave this way, Marcus writes, out of ignorance of what is truly good and truly bad. He understands the difference. He also knows that each of these people is his kinsman, not by blood and seed but by sharing the same reason and the same divine spark.

From that he draws two conclusions. None of them can hurt him, because none can make him do anything truly shameful. And he cannot be angry with someone so close to him by nature. People are born to work together, he says, like feet, hands and eyelids, like the rows of upper and lower teeth. To be in opposition is to go against nature, and to chafe at someone or turn away from them is to be in opposition.

The same notebook opens with a different kind of list. In the first book, Marcus thanks the people who shaped him, one at a time, and names what each gave him. From Alexander the Grammarian he learned that when someone makes a slip of language, you do not point it out. You reply by saying the phrase correctly. From Catulus he learned not to brush off a friend's complaint, even an unfair one, but to try to bring him back to his usual self. Alexander the Platonist taught him not to say or write "I am not at leisure" too often as an excuse for neglecting duties to friends.

One list looks back at people who helped him, and the other looks ahead at people who will not. Both are written in the same practical tone. Neither flatters anyone, and each shows a tired emperor deciding in advance how he will treat people.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before a difficult day, write down the awkward people you will probably meet and what you owe each of them.** A shift manager who knows a regular customer will argue about the bill can prepare a calm sentence at the start of the shift, and the argument has less hold on her when it comes. Marcus rehearsed, in a camp far from home, the usual kinds of irritation and reminded himself that people act out of mistaken ideas of what is good. Writing it down before the day begins gives you one calm minute you can rely on later.$$

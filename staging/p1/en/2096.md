On the day his company announced a deal that would end hundreds of jobs, its chief executive stayed in a Sunnyvale office instead of flying to New York for the press event. He spent the day helping staff carry their belongings out to their cars. An adviser had told him that is where he had to be.

[[story]]
Ben Horowitz was running Loudcloud in 2002, when the dot-com crash had left the company close to bankruptcy. The way out was a deal with EDS. According to Fortune's 2008 profile of Bill Campbell, about a third of the staff would be laid off, a third would transfer to EDS, and a third would stay to run what became Opsware.

Horowitz had planned to go to New York to announce it. Campbell, a close friend, told him to skip the trip. As Fortune quotes him, "The second you announce that thing, nobody in the company's going to care about anything but where they stand." Everyone in the building would want to know which of the three groups they were in, and only the chief executive could be there to say so.

Horowitz took the advice. He stayed in Sunnyvale all day and, in the words of the Fortune account, helped staff carry their things to their cars. Later he wrote a long piece on how to lay people off, and one line in it comes straight from that day: "The message is for the people who are staying." He also called Campbell's help the thing that saved the company.

It had not gone well the first time. By his own account Horowitz mishandled the first layoff at Loudcloud and fixed his approach before the next two. In the end there were three rounds, about four hundred people in all. Yet the company that stayed together was Opsware, and in 2007 Hewlett-Packard bought it for $1.6 billion.

The logic of Campbell's advice is easy to miss. A chief executive's natural pull on announcement day is toward the audience outside, the press and the investors. Campbell pointed him at the audience inside, where fear was highest and one visible person could answer the question everyone was actually asking.

Campbell's involvement went beyond that one piece of advice. Andreessen and Horowitz persuaded him to join the board, and he attended the Friday engineering meetings, which Andreessen called a form of applied coaching. He also pushed the company to keep building software through the downturn, on the grounds that without its own technology it had nothing to sell.

[[lessons]]
$$**On the day hard news lands, be physically where the people it affects are.** The audience that matters most is the one that has to live with the decision, and presence answers questions no memo can. Before you announce a reorganisation, a cut or a change of plan, ask who will be most anxious and put yourself in front of them that same day. A clear message carries the facts, while your being in the building shows the people behind those facts that someone is still looking after them, and that you will not hide behind the announcement or the press release.$$

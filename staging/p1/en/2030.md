At the Atlanta airport there is a new hangar, and twenty-six aircraft sit in it. The owner is not an airline. It is a company that makes Oreos, Ritz crackers and Winston cigarettes, and it keeps the fleet to move its own executives.

[[story]]
The central figure of Bryan Burrough and John Helyar's *Barbarians at the Gate* is F. Ross Johnson. Born in Winnipeg, he was president of Standard Brands when it merged with Nabisco in 1981, sold Nabisco to R.J. Reynolds for $4.9 billion in 1985, and became chief executive of RJR Nabisco in 1987. Time reported that he moved the company's headquarters from Winston-Salem to Atlanta, sold Heublein and cut the corporate staff from 1,000 to 400. The same magazine described a man who liked fine living and counted athletes such as Bobby Orr and Frank Gifford as friends.

This story is about his spending, in small details. The Christian Science Monitor wrote in 1990 that the company had put more than $12 million into a hangar at the Atlanta airport for a fleet of 26 planes. After his death, obituaries recalled that he was known for flying his pet dog on corporate jets.

Those were small items beside a $17 billion offer. At that scale the hangar was close to a rounding error, and the dog's flights were an anecdote. Still, they were concrete examples of how the man at the top spent other people's money, and the same man later proposed to buy that company from its shareholders.

In Time's issue of 5 December 1988, a piece examining Johnson's offer quoted John Swearingen, a former chairman of Standard Oil of Indiana: "There is a limit to what managers ought to be paid for managing other people's money." The same article reported that Johnson and seven top executives would put in $20 million for a stake of about 8.5 percent, scheduled to grow to 18.5 percent.

According to published accounts, the board's unease grew once details of Johnson's unusual severance arrangements came out. Johnson left the company in February 1989.

There is no need to weigh the hangar against the dog. What stands out is how clues about the way an executive treats other people's money form a backdrop to any large request he makes later. The sums may be small, but when every item points the same way, the person across the table can see that direction for himself.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you ask others for their money or their trust on something big, look at your small, visible spending as an outsider would.** A person who runs a team budget and keeps meals, travel and entertainment plain will be listened to more easily when a large investment request follows. Most people cannot judge a big figure, but everyone understands a small line item and draws a conclusion about how its owner treats other people's money. Everyday spending, visible to others, works like the opening sentence of the big request that comes later, written long before you ask.$$

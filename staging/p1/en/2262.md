[[open]]
Around a table, one question gets four different numbers: 40, 60, 80, 95. The person who has to decide must turn them into a yes or a no, and soon, with no answer key to check against. He has been given every figure except the one he can act on.

[[story]]
In spring 2011, intelligence analysts and officials in Washington debated whether Osama bin Laden was living in a compound in Abbottabad, Pakistan. According to Jeffrey Friedman and Richard Zeckhauser, who later analysed the episode in the journal *Intelligence and National Security*, the question ran through roughly forty intelligence reviews and several meetings between President Obama and his senior officials. It is also the kind of problem *Superforecasting* is about: how probabilities travel from the analyst to the person who decides.

At a March meeting, as Mark Bowden reported, the CIA team leader on the hunt put the chances as high as 95 percent. Michael Morell, the deputy director of intelligence, offered 60. Most people placed their confidence around 80, though some were as low as 40 or even 30. Other accounts of the meeting agree that the president heard a spread like this, and the paper lists point estimates of 40, 60, 80 and 95 percent among those he received.

He is reported to have complained that the discussion gave him not more certainty but more confusion, and that the numbers disguised uncertainty instead of giving him useful information. In the end he settled on odds of about fifty-fifty, and he is reported to have told his advisers that he could not base the decision on any greater certainty than that.

Friedman and Zeckhauser draw a point of method from this. A range of estimates always implies a single estimate, because if you find four numbers equally plausible, the natural move is to average them, and 40, 60, 80 and 95 average to about 69. An analyst who hands over a spread and leaves the combining to the president has not avoided choosing a number. The choice is simply made by someone who knows less about where each figure came from.

That is why they argue the combining belongs to the analysts. They know which colleague was working from the freshest information, whose number rested on the thinnest evidence, and which two people were actually drawing on the same source. Left to a busy president, all four figures look like opinions of equal weight.

The compound was raided in early May 2011, and bin Laden was there. The decision to go had rested on odds that nobody around that table could pin down.

[[lessons]]
$$**When you hand over a range, say which single number you would bet on.** A builder who tells a couple the extension will cost somewhere between a modest sum and a painful one leaves them to pick a figure with none of the builder's knowledge. If the builder says "I would plan for this amount, and here is what could push it up," the couple can act and still see the risk. Giving your own best number forces you to do the combining yourself, and it shows the listener where you are unsure. It also makes your later accuracy visible, which a range never does.$$

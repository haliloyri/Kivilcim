A note goes up on the bulletin board by the door of a private daycare. Parents who arrive more than ten minutes after closing will pay ten shekels for each child. The money goes to the owner, and the teacher who stays behind with the last children gets none of it. Parents look at this board every day.

[[story]]
Two economists, Uri Gneezy and Aldo Rustichini, wanted to test a plain prediction: put a penalty on a behavior, change nothing else, and you will see less of it. Steven Levitt and Stephen Dubner use their experiment in *Freakonomics* as a lesson about incentives. The researchers watched ten private daycare centers in Haifa from January to June 1998. Each contract promised that the center would run from half past seven to four o'clock, and none said a word about parents who arrived late.

For the first four weeks the researchers only counted the late ones. At the start of the fifth week, six of the ten centers, picked at random, put up the note. A parent more than ten minutes late would pay ten shekels per child, added to the monthly fee of 1,400 shekels. The other four centers heard nothing and became the comparison group. Ten shekels was small but not trivial, since an illegal-parking ticket in Israel cost 75 at the time.

Lateness did not fall. It climbed week after week for two or three weeks, then settled at almost twice the level of the first four weeks. In the four centers without a fine, nothing noticeable changed.

At the start of the seventeenth week the fine was taken away. A notice went up on the board with no explanation, and if parents asked, the principals were told to say it had been a trial for a limited time. Over the next four weeks the number of late parents stayed as high as it had been under the fine. The old habit did not come back.

The authors offered two readings and said the data could not rule out either one. In the first, the fine told parents something new, a signal that lateness was less serious than they had assumed. In the second, the fine turned a favor into a purchase. Once ten shekels bought the extra time, the guilt that used to come with asking for it was paid for, and removing the price did not bring the guilt back.

They did not claim that every fine backfires. A very large one, they noted, would probably have emptied the late line. Their point was narrower: a penalty is announced into a relationship that already has unwritten terms, and "everything else stays the same" may be impossible to keep true.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask what a penalty tells people before you attach one.** A book club that starts charging a small fee for skipping a meeting without notice may find that members begin to treat absence as something they can buy, and the apology that used to come with it disappears. A price tells people how much a behavior costs, and a low price can say it matters very little. Before you add a consequence to something that was handled by goodwill, ask what the people on the receiving end will read in it, and whether you could still ask for the old courtesy afterwards.$$

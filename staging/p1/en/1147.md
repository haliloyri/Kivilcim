One person tells a stranger on the train their whole life story. Another hasn't told their closest friend about a problem they've carried for a year. They seem like opposites. Nedra Glover Tawwab would say they're struggling with the same thing, from opposite ends.

[[story]]
In *Set Boundaries, Find Peace*, Nedra Glover Tawwab offers a simple way to see where your boundaries stand. She describes three levels, and most of us recognize ourselves in more than one of them.

The first is **porous** boundaries. These are weak or loosely defined. People with porous boundaries overshare, often with people they barely know. They find it hard to say no. They take on other people's problems as their own, worry constantly about disappointing anyone and bend over backwards to avoid rejection. Everyone can come in, and not much is kept safe.

The second is **rigid** boundaries. Here, the walls are up. People with rigid boundaries rarely share anything personal. They don't ask for help, even when they badly need it. They keep people at a distance and hold strict rules about what they will and won't allow. Often, Tawwab notes, rigid boundaries are a form of protection, a response to having been hurt before. They do keep some pain out. They also keep out closeness and support.

##Porous boundaries let everyone in. Rigid ones let no one in. Neither is really a choice.##

The third level is the one Tawwab calls **healthy** boundaries. Healthy doesn't mean somewhere vaguely in the middle. It means you're clear about your own values. You listen to your own opinions instead of automatically adopting other people's. You share personal information with the right people, in the right amount, at the right time. You can say no, and you can also accept hearing no from others without taking it as rejection.

What makes this framework useful is that it isn't a personality label. Tawwab points out that most of us mix the levels. You might have porous boundaries with your parents, giving in to every request, and rigid ones at work, where you never admit you're struggling. You might be healthy with friends and porous with your partner.

She also names different types of boundaries: physical, sexual, intellectual, emotional, material, and time. You can be clear about your time and completely unclear about lending money.

The hardest part of all this isn't learning a rule. It's asking yourself, in each relationship, a better question. *How much of myself do I want to share here? And can I say yes to this without losing something important?*

[[lessons]]
$$**Boundaries can fail in both directions.** Tawwab's framework shows that oversharing and never sharing are two versions of the same problem. The porous person has no filter and the rigid person has no door. Both are responding automatically rather than deciding, which is why both can end up feeling unsafe or alone.$$

$$**Healthy means chosen.** A healthy boundary isn't a fixed setting. It's a decision made with your values in mind: sharing with the right people, in the right amount, and being able to say no and hear no. That takes more thought than a blanket rule, but it's what lets closeness and self-respect exist together.$$

$$**Check each relationship separately.** Most of us are porous in some places and rigid in others. Looking relationship by relationship, and type by type, such as time, money or emotions, shows exactly where the work is instead of leaving you with a vague sense that you're "bad at boundaries."$$

[[reflect]]
- In which relationship are your boundaries porous, with too much given or shared?
- Where have you built a wall that might be keeping out help or closeness?
- Which type of boundary, like time, money, emotions or physical space, is hardest for you?

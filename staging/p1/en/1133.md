Try a small experiment before you read on. Notice where your mind was a moment ago. Most likely it was somewhere else: replaying something from earlier today, or rehearsing something that hasn't happened yet. Eckhart Tolle would ask how many seconds today you actually spent right here.

[[story]]
The idea at the heart of *The Power of Now* is so simple it's easy to dismiss. Tolle states it plainly: the present moment is all you ever have.

It sounds like a slogan until you look at it closely. Think of your happiest memory. When it happened, it happened in a present moment. Now think of something you're dreading next month. If it comes, it will arrive as a present moment too. Tolle puts it like this:

> Nothing ever happened in the past; it happened in the Now. Nothing will ever happen in the future; it will happen in the Now.

The past, in other words, exists only as memory, a thought you're having now. The future exists only as anticipation, another thought you're having now. Yet most of us live almost entirely in those two places. We relive old conversations and rewrite old decisions. We imagine meetings, arguments and disasters that may never happen. Meanwhile the one moment where life is actually taking place slides by with hardly any attention.

Tolle isn't saying you should never think about time. He draws a useful distinction. There's **clock time**: making an appointment, planning a project, learning from a mistake so you don't repeat it. That's practical, and you need it. Then there's what he calls **psychological time**: the habit of identifying with the past and living for the future, as though your real life is always somewhere else. That's where, he argues, most anxiety, regret and worry come from.

##Worry needs a future to live in. Regret needs a past.##

He offers a test you can run at any moment. Ask yourself: *do I have a problem right now?* Not tomorrow, not in ten minutes, not in the version of next week your mind is busy building. Right now, this moment.

Most of the time, the honest answer is no. There may be a situation that needs attention later, and you can plan for it using clock time. But in this moment, you're sitting, breathing, reading. The "problem" is a thought about a moment that hasn't arrived.

Tolle's claim is that coming back to that moment, over and over, changes how you feel. The present is always available. You just have to choose to go there.

[[lessons]]
$$**Everything happens now.** Tolle's line, "nothing ever happened in the past; it happened in the Now," isn't wordplay. It points out that every experience you've had or will have takes place in a present moment. Memories and plans are thoughts you're having now. Seeing this makes it harder to believe that your "real" life is waiting somewhere else.$$

$$**Plan in clock time, but don't live there.** Tolle doesn't ask you to ignore the calendar. Clock time is for appointments, projects and learning from mistakes. The trouble starts when you move into psychological time and let past and future run your mood all day.$$

$$**Most problems aren't happening this second.** The question "do I have a problem right now?" is a small tool with a big effect. It separates a real situation you can act on from the story your mind is telling about it. Usually, in this exact moment, you're fine.$$

[[reflect]]
- Where did your mind spend the last five minutes: in the past, the future, or here?
- Which worry that's been on your mind today would disappear if you looked only at this moment?
- What is one daily activity, like making coffee or walking, where you could practice being fully present?

&&Right now, this second, is there actually a problem, or only a thought about one?&&

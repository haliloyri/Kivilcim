A woman in one country and a man in the next live under different paperwork rules about what happens to their organs after death. One of them has to take a step to be counted as a donor. The other has to take a step to be left out.

[[story]]
In the Netherlands in 1998, a national donor register was set up. It came with an extensive educational campaign and a mass mailing of more than 12 million letters in a country of 15.8 million people. According to Eric Johnson and Daniel Goldstein, the effort failed to change the share of Dutch people who were effectively registered as donors.

Johnson and Goldstein reported this in a short paper in Science in November 2003, titled "Do Defaults Save Lives?" Thaler and Sunstein also cover organ donation in *Nudge*. The authors had noticed that donation rates across European Union countries differ by nearly a factor of ten, and that the gaps stay stable from year to year.

The countries fell into two groups. In one, a person had to register to become a donor. In the other, everyone counted as a donor unless they registered to opt out. The authors filled gaps in the published data by contacting central registries and calculated an "effective consent rate": the share of people who were donors on paper.

The gap was enormous. In Germany the effective consent rate was 12 percent. In neighboring Austria it was 99.98 percent. No country that required registration reached the level of any country that presumed consent, so the two distributions did not overlap, and about 60 percentage points separated them.

Controlling for transplant infrastructure, economic and educational status and religion left large differences in place. The authors also ran an online experiment, and the gap there was smaller. In real countries, they note, leaving the default costs more: forms to fill in, phone calls to make, letters to send.

They read the pattern this way: if people held firm preferences about organ donation, the default would matter little. It mattered a great deal, which suggested that many people had no settled answer and went along with whatever the system assumed. A register number could say more about how hard registration was than about what people truly intended.

One limit belongs here. The paper measured registered consent, not organs actually transplanted, and later researchers argued that the real difference in donations between countries is smaller than the chart suggests. Even so, the two rows of bars side by side show how much the starting position of a form can shape what it appears to record.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you read a blank as a "no", check what saying yes would have cost.** A parent who did not write their name on the sign-up sheet for the school evening may be perfectly willing to come and simply never got round to writing. If joining takes a signature and not joining takes nothing, the empty lines mostly record effort and not wishes. When you see few yes answers on a list, a form or a survey, look at how much work a yes required before you decide people do not want it, and ask the question directly at least once.$$

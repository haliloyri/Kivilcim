A trader at a Wall Street bank types in an order to buy 10,000 shares. The price is on his screen, so the order should be simple. Instead only part of it fills, and by the time he reaches for the rest, the price has already moved away from him.

[[story]]
The trader was Brad Katsuyama, the central figure of Michael Lewis's *Flash Boys*. He had started at the Royal Bank of Canada's New York office in 2002 and, according to a profile in the Globe and Mail, took over its electronic trading desk in 2009. Buying shares on screen had become unreliable. A purchase of 10,000 shares would fill in part. Prices would rise on other exchanges while he tried to complete it.

His team's reading was that high-frequency traders were responding to the first part of the order before the rest could arrive. Large orders have to be split across several exchanges, and each exchange sits at a different distance from the sender. Sub-orders sent at the same instant therefore land at different times, and a fast trader who sees the first one can reach the others ahead of it. Critics dispute how much money this earned, but the RBC team took it as the working explanation.

Their answer was a program called Thor, short for Tactical Hybrid Order Router. It did not try to outrun anyone, and it did not need to. It delayed the separate buy requests inside a single order so that they reached the various exchanges at the same moment, down to the microsecond. The requests bound for the nearest exchanges waited, and the farthest went first. The distances that had worked against RBC became a timetable that worked for it. With nothing left to see early, RBC's traders could buy at the prices on their screens again.

From the summer of 2009 Katsuyama also took his findings to investors on the buy side, among them Doug Schrank of Southeastern Asset Management. The diagnosis led him further. He left RBC to start his own exchange, IEX, which held its first trading day on October 25, 2013.

RBC declined to invest in it, to protect IEX's independence, but RBC executed the exchange's first-ever trade.

Thor is worth a second look because of how little it asked of the market. It needed no new law, no cooperation from the exchanges and no race. It changed one thing under RBC's control, the moment its own messages left, and that was enough to change what the order met when it arrived.

[[lessons]]
$$**When something keeps costing you, change the part of the exchange that you control.** Suppose a small team always gets its requests to a busy supplier at the same time as everyone else, and always ends up last in the queue. Rather than waiting for the supplier to change its rules, the team can alter when and how it sends its own requests, for instance by bundling them or sending the urgent one first. The change is small, sits entirely in your hands and does not need anyone's permission, and the results show you whether your reading of the problem was right.$$

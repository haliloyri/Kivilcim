A day into a flight to the Moon, one astronaut raises a problem that has nothing to do with rockets. His income tax return is due in a few days, and it has not been filed. He says it is serious. The commander answers with a joke about where the money will go.

[[story]]
Jack Swigert was not supposed to be on Apollo 13 at all. Ken Mattingly, the prime command module pilot, had been exposed to German measles shortly before launch and was taken off the crew. Swigert, the backup, took his seat only days before liftoff on 11 April 1970. Jim Lovell and Jeffrey Kluger's *Lost Moon* tells what followed, and this small moment belongs to the quiet first day, before the oxygen tank failed.

About 24 hours into the flight, the transcript records the exchange. Lovell asked the ground: "How do I apply for an extension?" Swigert took it up: "I didn't get mine filed. And this is serious." He explained that "things kind of happened real fast down there." Then he added: "I may be spending time in another quarantine besides the one that they are planning for me."

A little later Lovell came back on the radio. "Houston, this is 13. Is it true that Jack's income tax return was going to be used to buy the ascent fuel for the LM?" Capcom Joe Kerwin, who was on the console, answered: "Well, considering that he's a bachelor and hasn't got that deduction to take, yes."

Swigert did not laugh it off. "Hey, Joe. I'm glad you brought that up, because I was really serious about that." Kerwin gave him a real answer: "Okay, Jack. We'll take care of it. Tom Stafford says he'll get an extension for you."

The tax return never entered the mission plan, and it never needed to. What the exchange shows is how a late arrival lands on a crew. Swigert had been moved into the seat in a hurry, so his private affairs had been left half done. He said so in front of everyone, in plain words, and the ground treated it as a real item while still allowing the joke. The commander teased him, and the capcom promised action.

Nobody pretended the problem was trivial, and nobody made it heavy, and the whole exchange took only a couple of minutes of radio time. Something that was a worry for one man became an item on a list that others were now carrying. Within a day and a half the crew would need exactly that habit, saying a problem aloud and letting others take a piece of it.

[[lessons]]
$$**When you join a team late, say your loose ends out loud and early.** Swigert's open admission, "I didn't get mine filed," cost him a little embarrassment and bought him a promise from the ground within minutes. A hidden loose end tends to grow in private and arrive at the worst moment, while a spoken one can be shared and fixed by someone who has the standing to fix it. This holds when you start a new job, when you step into a project that is already moving, and when you cover for someone on leave. Name the unfinished thing, name the date it matters, and ask who can take it.$$

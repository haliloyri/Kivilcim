A doctor writes his investors a letter and puts one number in it: 1.687 billion dollars. That is the size of his bet against the housing market. Some of the people reading it begin to ask for their money back. He does not move, and he has even written down what he stands to lose.

[[story]]
Michael Lewis's *The Big Short* follows the people who bet against the American housing market before the crisis of 2008. This story is about one of them, Michael Burry. Burry is a physician with an MD from Vanderbilt, awarded in 1997, but he earned his living running an investment fund instead of working in a hospital. He called it Scion Capital, a name taken from a fantasy novel by Terry Brooks.

By the middle of the 2000s Burry believed that subprime mortgage bonds would lose value. He expressed that view through credit default swaps, or CDS. A CDS works like an insurance policy on a bond: if the bond falls in value you are paid, and in exchange you hand over a regular premium. As long as the market keeps rising, paying that premium means watching money drain away.

In November 2006 Burry wrote his investors a letter titled "A Primer on Scion Capital's Subprime Mortgage Short". According to that letter, the fund held subprime CDS with a notional value of 1.687 billion dollars. He also wrote down the worst case. If the bet failed, the fund would pay about 6 percent a year in carrying costs. What he could lose was known, and it was in print.

While the market was still climbing, that picture made investors uneasy, and some of them asked to withdraw. Then mortgage loans began to go bad, and the bet paid off. By reported figures Burry made around 100 million dollars personally and his investors more than 700 million. The fund closed in 2008.

What stands out is less that he was right and more how he handled the long stretch before he was proven right. Burry carried his view as a position with a stated cost and a stated risk, not as a feeling. His investors knew what they were waiting for, how long they might have to wait and what they could lose in the worst case. That openness did not persuade everyone, and some left. But it left no need to dress up the decision afterwards, because the number was on the table and only the market could say whether he was right.

That is the hard part of holding an unpopular view. When a crowd thinks you are wrong, the easy options are to quit quietly or to dig in without explaining. Burry took a third route: he priced the wait, he told people the price, and he turned patience into a written plan.

[[lessons]]
$$**If you want to defend a view, write down the cost of waiting in numbers before you start.** When you believe in a project or a new path and everyone around you is doubtful, the real argument is rarely "am I right?" and usually "what am I paying until I am proven right?". By stating his yearly cost of about 6 percent upfront, Burry made his investors' waiting measurable. Write down your own possible loss, your time limit and the point at which you will quit. That keeps both the sceptics and you honest, because when the deadline comes you compare a number and not a mood.$$

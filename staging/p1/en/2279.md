A physician reads down a list of the dead and notices a trade that is missing from it. There is a brewery in Broad Street, near the pump, and no brewer's man appears in the register. The physician goes to ask the owner what the men drink.

[[story]]
The physician is John Snow, and the register is the record of cholera deaths in Soho in 1854. Steven Johnson's *The Ghost Map* is about this outbreak. This story is about people who should have fallen ill and stayed well, and two who fell ill though they lived far away.

He called on the brewery's owner, Mr Huggins, who told him that more than seventy men worked there and none had suffered from cholera in a severe form. Only two had been indisposed, and only mildly. The men were allowed a certain quantity of malt liquor, Mr Huggins believed they did not drink water at all, and he was certain they never took any from the street pump. The brewery had a deep well of its own.

The Poland Street workhouse told a similar tale. It was more than three-fourths surrounded by houses in which people had died of cholera, yet out of 535 inmates only five died, and the other deaths were of people admitted after they had already fallen ill. The workhouse had its own pump-well, and its inmates never sent to Broad Street for water. Snow calculated that if the workhouse had suffered like the streets on three sides of it, upwards of a hundred people would have died.

The opposite case came from a percussion-cap factory at 37 Broad Street. About two hundred people worked there, two tubs of pump water stood on the premises for anyone who wanted a drink, and eighteen of the workers died, sixteen men and two women, at their own homes.

Then came a detail that Dr Fraser pointed out to him, and which Snow called perhaps the most conclusive of all. The weekly return recorded a death in Hampstead on 2 September: the 59-year-old widow of a percussion-cap maker. Her son told Snow she had not been near Broad Street for months. A cart travelled daily from Broad Street to Hampstead, and it was the custom to carry out a large bottle of the pump water because she preferred it. She drank it on Thursday evening and on Friday, was seized that night, and died on Saturday. A niece who visited her drank some too, went home to Islington, and died as well. There was no cholera in either neighbourhood at the time.

Snow noted that many people drank the pump water without falling ill, and he promised to explain why later. What mattered to him was the pattern: the sickness followed the water wherever it travelled, and the people spared were the ones who had another source.

[[lessons]]
$$**Study the people the problem skipped as carefully as the people it hit.** Snow's most persuasive evidence came from a brewery, a workhouse and a house in Hampstead, places where the pattern should have held and did not, or held where nobody expected it. When something goes wrong at work, ask who was exposed and escaped, and what they did differently. The answer often hides in the quiet group that never shows up in the complaints, and it can point to a cause that the loud cases alone would never reveal. Make a habit of asking for that group's account before you settle on an explanation.$$

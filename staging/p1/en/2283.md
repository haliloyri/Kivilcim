[[open]]
At a Justice Department press conference, an official summed up a Hollywood movie in a single line: a corrupt stockbroker who tried to hide his illicit profits in a perceived foreign safe haven. He was not reviewing it. According to the allegations he then read out, the movie had been paid for with money stolen from a Malaysian state fund.

[[story]]
Tom Wright and Bradley Hope's *Billion Dollar Whale* follows 1MDB money into some unlikely places, and this story is about one of the most visible: the 2013 film *The Wolf of Wall Street*. When the Justice Department announced its civil forfeiture complaints on 20 July 2016, the film was on the list of assets the department alleged had been bought with misappropriated 1MDB funds.

"According to the allegations in the complaints, this is a case where life imitated art," said Assistant Attorney General Leslie Caldwell. The associates of corrupt 1MDB officials, she said, were alleged to have used some of the illicit proceeds to fund the production of a movie about a corrupt stockbroker. Caldwell added that whether corrupt officials try to hide stolen assets across international borders or behind the silver screen, the department was committed to ensuring there was no safe haven.

The studio behind the film was Red Granite Pictures, co-founded by Riza Aziz, the stepson of Malaysia's then prime minister, Najib Razak. Najib was not the subject of the lawsuits, and he and Riza have consistently denied wrongdoing. In March 2018 Red Granite agreed to pay the US government $60 million to settle the civil case: $30 million within 30 days, $20 million within the next 180 days and a final $10 million within 180 days after that.

The settlement also covered claims against its interests in two other films, *Daddy's Home* and *Dumb and Dumber To*. Paramount Pictures, which had held profits from *Daddy's Home* while the case was pending, was to release those funds to a government-controlled account. The agreement said the payment was not an admission of wrongdoing or liability by Red Granite, and Riza would draw no salary from the company during the payment period beyond what was needed for health insurance.

The same lawsuits sought a $3.2 million Picasso painting that, they alleged, had been bought with 1MDB funds and given to the film's star, Leonardo DiCaprio. DiCaprio turned the painting over to US authorities.

Part of the force of this episode is how ordinary the channel looks. A film is a normal thing for an investor to back. Nothing about a shooting schedule, a poster or a premiere signals that the money behind it is under question, and the film's own plot, about money that needs somewhere to hide, only adds to the irony.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask where an unusually generous backer got the money.** A rich offer feels like a stroke of luck, and the pleasant surprise makes it awkward to ask questions that would sound ungrateful. Imagine a village football club offered a very large sponsorship by a company nobody has heard of. The committee can ask in writing for the firm's accounts and the source of the funds before the logo goes on the shirts, and a real sponsor will not mind answering. Asking early costs a little goodwill, and answers that never come cost far more later, once the contract is signed and the shirts are printed.$$

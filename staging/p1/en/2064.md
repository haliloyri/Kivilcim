One in 73 million. A professor gives the jury that figure, and then a picture to go with it: an outsider at eighty to one winning the Grand National four years running. Everyone in the courtroom hears it. The trial concerns the deaths of two babies, and the number sounds like an answer.

[[story]]
Sally Clark, a solicitor from Wilmslow in Cheshire, lost her first son, Christopher, on 13 December 1996, when he was eleven weeks old. Her second son, Harry, died on 26 January 1998, aged eight weeks. On 9 November 1999 the Crown Court at Chester convicted her, by a majority of ten to two, of murdering both. Her first appeal was dismissed on 2 October 2000. Ben Goldacre covers the case in the "Bad Stats" chapter of *Bad Science*, as an example of statistics going wrong in a courtroom.

The figure came from Professor Roy Meadow. He told the jury that the chance of two sudden infant deaths (SIDS) in one such family was about 1 in 73 million. A table in a government-funded report gave 1 in 8,543 for a single death in a family like the Clarks, with none of the risk factors the table listed, such as a smoker in the house, and Meadow squared it. Professor Berry, an editor of that report and a witness for the defence, called squaring the number an illegitimate oversimplification. The report's own text said a second death should be investigated thoroughly, though it would be inappropriate to assume maltreatment was always the cause, and that text was not put to the jury. The trial judge cautioned the jury that people are not convicted on statistics.

Goldacre, writing in 2006, separates two problems. The first is the figure itself. Squaring it treats the two deaths as independent, when environmental or genetic factors could be shared by one family. The second is subtler, and has a name, the prosecutor's fallacy. Two babies dying in one family is very rare. But once it has happened, the jury has two explanations in front of it, double SIDS or double murder, and double murder is rare too. Without knowing which is rarer, 1 in 73 million says nothing about who is innocent.

The first appeal court accepted part of this: looking back, the figure was no help and was merely a distraction. It still upheld the convictions on the evidence it had.

Then microbiological test results from Harry's post mortem came to light. They had never been before the trial, because the lawyers on both sides did not know they existed. *Staphylococcus aureus* had been found in several samples, including the cerebrospinal fluid. The experts disagreed about what it meant. In January 2003 the Court of Appeal held the convictions unsafe and quashed both.

[[lessons]]
$$**When a rare event has happened, compare how rare each competing explanation is.** A shopkeeper who finds the till short twice in one week may feel it cannot be chance. The better question is whether, in a shop like hers, till errors or theft happen more often. That comparison makes you stop before you jump to the easy conclusion or the easy culprit, and it reminds you that rarity alone explains nothing. The comparison costs a minute and accuses no one, so you can ask the same question about a breakdown, a complaint or a headline with a very small number in it.$$

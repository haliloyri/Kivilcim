Daniel Lieberman of Harvard looks at the muscles in a runner's backside. When people walk, the biggest of them hardly switches on. When the same people jog, it works hard. That single measurement raises a question about what the human body was shaped to do.

[[story]]
A study that points the same way as the case Christopher McDougall makes in *Born to Run* appeared in *Nature* in 2004, by Dennis Bramble of the University of Utah and Daniel Lieberman of Harvard. They argued that the human body was shaped by endurance running. According to Nature's news report, early humans may have started running long distances around 2 million years ago, once their ancestors had begun walking upright on the African savannah. Lieberman put it simply: humans are quite good at endurance running.

On their theory, natural selection would have favoured traits such as wide, sturdy knee joints. The list also includes springy Achilles tendons, stout leg joints, hairlessness and sweating, the last two of which help the body shed heat. Fred Spoor of University College London added that unusually large semicircular canals in the inner ear, found in modern humans and in *Homo erectus*, may have helped runners keep their balance.

The measurement behind the sharpest example concerns the gluteus maximus, the largest muscle in the backside. It barely activates during walking and works strongly during jogging, and Lieberman suggested that running is the main reason people have prominent buttocks. The article also set out speeds. The fastest human sprinters reach about 10 metres a second and cheetahs about 30, while a horse galloping a long distance averages about 6 metres a second, which is slower than a top-class human runner.

It also offered two reasons early humans might have run. One was scavenging, racing hyenas, which are also good long-distance runners, to a carcass. The other was persistence hunting, chasing an animal until it is exhausted.

The report itself does not treat the theory as settled. Spoor said it was not yet clear how the idea would be received. The report also quotes a view that human locomotion is not special and that only brain size sets people apart from other apes. So what we have is not a proven result but an explanation backed by measurements, one that the field was still arguing about when the report was written. Every item on the list, from the tendon to the sweat, is offered as part of one answer to a single question: why can people run so far?

[[lessons]]
$$**Hold a strong explanation as a candidate and ask what would count against it.** A café owner may believe that customers flock at lunchtime because of the soup, and she may be right. If she also counts the days without soup, she learns what her explanation really predicts. When an expert like Spoor says it is not yet clear how a theory will be received, that is not a flaw in it, it points to where the next measurement should come from. You can keep a good story about your work next to the question that would test it, and your next decision rests on a checked idea.$$

A man is caught breaking the same rule twice, by the same policeman. The first time, he got a warning. This time, he can see a fine coming. What he says in the next ten seconds turns an angry officer into a sympathetic one. Dale Carnegie tells this story on himself.

[[story]]
In *How to Win Friends and Influence People*, Dale Carnegie describes his walks in Forest Park, a wild, wooded area near where he lived. He usually took his little Boston bull dog, Rex, a friendly dog that wouldn't hurt anyone. They rarely met other people there, so Carnegie often let Rex run free, without a leash or a muzzle.

One day, a mounted policeman stopped him. He was stern. Didn't Carnegie know it was against the law to let a dog run loose in the park without a muzzle? Next time, the officer said, he'd be explaining himself to a judge.

Carnegie promised to comply, and for a while he did. But Rex didn't like the muzzle, and Carnegie didn't like making him wear it. So one afternoon he let Rex run again. Almost immediately, they ran into the same policeman on the same horse.

This time, Carnegie didn't wait for the lecture. He spoke first.

> Officer, you've caught me red-handed. I'm guilty. I have no alibis, no excuses. You warned me last week that if I brought the dog out here again without a muzzle you would fine me.

##The policeman's tone changed completely.##

He said he knew how tempting it was to let a little dog like that have a run when nobody was around. Carnegie agreed it was tempting, but still against the law. The officer said a small dog like Rex wasn't going to hurt anybody. Carnegie said it might kill a squirrel. By now, the officer was almost defending him. In the end, he suggested Carnegie simply let the dog run somewhere over the hill, out of sight, and forget about it.

Carnegie explains what happened. The policeman, like anyone, wanted to feel important. When Carnegie defended himself, the officer had to assert his authority. When Carnegie condemned himself first, there was nothing left to prove. The only way the officer could still feel important was by being generous.

Carnegie turns this into a principle: **if you are wrong, admit it quickly and emphatically.** Say the critical things about yourself before the other person can say them. It's far easier to hear criticism from yourself than from someone else. And very often, the other person responds by forgiving you and minimizing your mistake.

Any fool, Carnegie adds, can try to defend his mistakes. It takes a certain kind of strength to admit them.

[[lessons]]
$$**Admitting it first takes the fight away.** When Carnegie made excuses, the officer lectured. When he confessed first, fully and without hedging, the officer softened. Owning a mistake before the other person raises it removes their need to prove you wrong, and often turns an opponent into an ally.$$

$$**Let the other person be generous.** Carnegie's insight is that people want to feel important. Once you've already condemned yourself, the only way left for them to feel important is to show mercy. Giving someone that chance is far more effective than forcing them to win an argument against you.$$

$$**Excuses invite attack.** Defending a clear mistake makes the other person push harder, because now they have to establish that you were wrong. A quick, plain admission ends that contest. It isn't weakness. Carnegie treats it as a sign of real self-confidence.$$

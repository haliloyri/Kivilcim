A bank trader gives an inquiry set up after the crisis a number: at least fifty. Fifty hedge funds, he says, used him to bet on the housing market collapsing. What looked like the insight of a handful of geniuses was a fairly crowded table.

[[story]]
Read *The Big Short* and the people who bet against housing can look like loners, contrarians, almost one of a kind. What Greg Lippmann told the Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission, the FCIC, adds something important to that picture. Lippmann was a Deutsche Bank trader, the man credited with getting rival banks to a dinner in early 2005 to agree a standard contract. He told the commission that in 2006 and 2007 he brokered such bets for at least 50 hedge funds.

The instruments were credit default swaps written on low-rated securities, contracts that pay out if mortgage-linked products lose value. His number was reported in a Bloomberg item dated 27 January 2011. The commission's own 545-page report said the figure might have reached as high as 100.

Put the two figures side by side and what stands out is the uncertainty. The gap between "at least fifty" and "possibly a hundred" shows that nobody knew exactly how many funds were involved. In either case it is clear that the bet did not belong to a few people. Many institutions, each working on its own, had seen the same crack, and at least fifty of them placed their trades through the same intermediary. A shared channel and a shared contract make it easier for separate firms to act on the same idea through the same door.

None of this says anything about whether the view was right. It says a lot about where the people holding it actually stood. To believe your idea is the one nobody else sees misleads you in two directions: you feel either more special than you are or more alone than you are. Yet the same idea can ripen at the same time at the desks of people who have never met.

Lippmann's number is therefore a measuring stick, not a boast. An idea that looks contrarian is often one that few people say aloud, not one that few people hold. Counting the quiet ones around you is the first step towards knowing where your view really stands.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you defend an unusual view, count how many others have seen the same thing.** When you notice a risk in a project or a flaw in a process, do not assume you are alone just because nobody around you is speaking. Lippmann's figure of at least fifty funds shows that an unusual view can be far more crowded than it feels. Test yours by talking quietly with a few people; colleagues or people from other departments may be looking in the same direction. That checks your idea against trustworthy sources and lets you speak up knowing you are not alone.$$

[[open]]
Picture a hotel bathroom. On the towel rack, a small card asks you to hang your towel up and use it again tomorrow. Now picture a second card asking the exact same thing in slightly different words. One of those two cards gets noticeably more people to go along. The one that wins doesn't give you a better reason. It hardly gives you a reason at all.

[[story]]
Researchers working with a hotel wanted to test something simple. Guests found a card in the bathroom asking them to reuse their towels. The standard card did what most of these cards do and appealed to protecting the environment. The reason was sound, and most guests would have agreed with it.

Then the researchers tried an alternative. Instead of explaining *why*, the new card told guests what other people did.

> Most guests at this hotel reuse their towels.

That was the whole change. The card offered no new argument and no reward, only a fact about the crowd.

The social-norm card clearly won. Published accounts put it at roughly 26% more towel reuse than the environmental appeal. Then came a twist. A version that referred to guests who had stayed in the same room did even better. These weren't people in general. They had slept in the same bed and stood at the same sink. The more the "others" resembled you, the harder their example pulled.

##A good cause, stated plainly, barely moved people. One sentence about what other guests did moved them far more.##

The towel study was published long after the 1984 original of *Influence*. It appears in the book's later, expanded editions. It is still a near-perfect illustration of what Robert Cialdini calls social proof. When we're uncertain about the right thing to do, we look at what people like us are doing and take that as the answer. Nobody checks into a hotel knowing the towel etiquette, so guests look for a signal. Tell them the crowd reuses, and the question is settled.

Cialdini's original examples were just as ordinary. Canned laughter on TV works even though everyone knows it's fake. Bartenders "salt" their tip jars with a few bills so tipping looks like what people do here. In the darkest case, bystanders in an emergency notice that nobody else is acting and conclude that nothing needs to be done.

That last example leads to his warning. Social proof doesn't push toward what's good. It pushes toward whatever looks common. A message that stresses how many people are doing the wrong thing can backfire, because the part people absorb is "everyone does it."

Then there's the uncomfortable part. The guests didn't need convincing that the environment mattered, and most probably agreed already. What changed their behavior was hearing what everyone else did. We like to believe our values steer our choices. The towel cards suggest the crowd often gets there first.

[[lessons]]
$$**Other people's behavior counts as evidence.** The environmental card gave guests a good reason. The winning card gave them a fact about the crowd: most guests reuse their towels. In an unfamiliar hotel, nobody knows the local norm, so that fact reads as the correct answer. When people are unsure, showing them what others do often works better than telling them what's right.$$

$$**The closer the crowd, the stronger the pull.** "Guests at this hotel" worked well. "Guests who stayed in this room" worked even better. Nothing about the argument changed, only how similar the reference group was to the reader. If you want your example to land, pick peers who look like the person you're talking to and who share their place or situation.$$

$$**Advertising a bad habit can spread it.** Cialdini warns that messages stressing how many people do the wrong thing can make that behavior seem normal. The bystanders who stay still because no one else moves show how it works. Complaining that "everyone ignores this" may tell your audience that ignoring it is the norm. Point to the people doing it right.$$

[[reflect]]
- Think of the last time you picked something mainly because lots of other people had picked it: a restaurant, an app, an opinion.
- Is there an "everyone does it" habit around you that's actually a minority behavior that simply gets talked about a lot?
- When you ask someone to change, do you lead with principle, or do you show them peers who have already changed?
- Where might your own complaints about "nobody ever does this" be making the problem seem normal?

&&What are you doing only because everyone around you does it, and would you still choose it if no one else did?&&

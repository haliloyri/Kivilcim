A man steps out of a lift on the ninth floor of a Hong Kong hotel and goes to his room. Other guests come and go along the same floor. Nothing about the scene looks like the start of anything, and a few weeks later it will be the address that investigators in several countries keep coming back to.

[[story]]
On 21 February 2003 a medical professor from Guangzhou checked into a room on the ninth floor of the Metropole Hotel in Kowloon, Hong Kong. David Quammen's *Spillover* follows SARS from southern China to Hong Kong, and this episode, as the published epidemiological account tells it, shows how one short stay carried the virus out into the world.

At least seven other guests and visitors on that floor were infected. Three of them were from Singapore, one from Vietnam, two from Canada, and one was a Hong Kong resident. They came from four different places, and what they shared was a floor.

Then they went home. According to the published analysis, the index cases of the outbreaks in Hong Kong, Toronto, Singapore and Hanoi were all linked to the hotel. Singapore, for instance, reported three patients on 8 March who had stayed at the hotel. Four cities on two continents had a starting point that fitted inside one building. Later that month Hong Kong's Department of Health announced that at least seven cases there were related to the hotel, and the ninth floor was cleared to resume business once it had been dealt with.

On 12 March 2003 the World Health Organization issued a global alert, and on 15 March it released its first emergency travel advisory naming SARS. By the end of 2003, the WHO's tally stood at 8,096 probable cases and 774 deaths.

What makes the Metropole worth telling is how ordinary the setting was. Nobody had planned a gathering, there was no market and no hospital ward, only a hotel where strangers slept on the same corridor. A virus that needs close contact does not need many people, only a place where unrelated people come close enough for long enough.

That is also why the hotel became a clue. Cases that looked unrelated, scattered across four cities, turned out to share an address and a few days in late February. Tracing where people's paths had crossed explained the pattern more quickly than studying each patient on their own.

[[lessons]]
$$**Find the place where separate paths cross.** A school secretary who notices that three unrelated families all caught the same cold has no reason to question each family separately, because one shared hallway, one shared bus or one shared event explains it faster. A shared location links cases that seem to have nothing in common, and it points to where a fix will work best. When several separate problems appear at once, list where each of them passed, and look for the place that appears in every list. A hallway or a waiting room is easy to overlook because it is nobody's problem, yet it often explains more than a careful study of each separate case.$$

A young man tells a philosopher about a friend who hasn't left his room in years. The reason is obvious, he says: the friend suffers from terrible anxiety about going outside. The philosopher listens, then turns the explanation completely upside down.

[[story]]
In *The Courage to Be Disliked*, Ichiro Kishimi and Fumitake Koga present the ideas of the psychologist Alfred Adler through a long conversation between a philosopher and a skeptical young man.

Early on, the young man describes a friend who has shut himself away in his room for years. He wants to go out, the young man insists, but he can't. He's too anxious. The anxiety is the cause; staying inside is the effect.

This is the way most of us naturally explain behavior. The philosopher calls it **etiology**: looking for causes in the past. Something happened, it created a feeling or a wound, and that explains how we act now. It's the approach associated with Freud, and it feels intuitive.

Adler, the philosopher says, looked at it differently. He called his approach **teleology**: explaining behavior by the goals it serves right now.

##The friend, the philosopher suggests, doesn't stay in because he's anxious. He creates the anxiety so that he can stay in.##

The young man is outraged. It sounds like blaming someone who's suffering. But the philosopher explains what he means. Going out into the world carries risks: being judged, rejected, compared, hurt. Staying inside protects the friend from all of that. The anxiety, then, is not simply something that happened to him. It serves a purpose. It gives him a reason not to face those risks.

The philosopher then quotes Adler directly. No experience is in itself a cause of our success or failure. We don't suffer from the shock of our experiences, but make out of them whatever suits our purposes. We are not determined by our experiences, but by the meaning we give them.

This doesn't mean the philosopher denies that painful things happen. Trauma, loss and hardship are real, and they hurt. His point is narrower, and in a way more hopeful. If the past fully determined who we are, we'd be trapped by it forever. If instead we are shaped by the meaning we give to the past, then that meaning can change, and so can we.

The young man finds this idea hard to accept. It asks more of him than the comfortable belief that his past explains everything.

[[lessons]]
$$**Behavior often serves a present goal.** The idea that the friend creates anxiety in order to stay in sounds harsh, but it asks a useful question: what does this behavior protect me from? Often, hesitation or avoidance isn't just caused by the past. It helps us avoid a risk we'd rather not face now.$$

$$**Meaning shapes us more than experience.** Adler's line, that we are determined by the meaning we give our experiences, not the experiences themselves, puts some power back in our hands. Two people with the same painful past can build very different lives, depending on what they decide that past means.$$

$$**The past is real, but not a sentence.** The philosopher doesn't deny that painful events happen or matter. He rejects the idea that they must decide the future. Taking the past seriously while refusing to be ruled by it is the difficult balance this idea asks for.$$

[[reflect]]
- What do you tell yourself your past prevents you from doing today?
- What might your hesitation or avoidance be protecting you from right now?
- Which past experience could you give a different, more useful meaning to?

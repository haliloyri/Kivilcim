A light appears on a screen. Your only job is to press a button when you see it. It's about as simple as a test can get, and yet a group of healthy adults kept missing the light, more and more often, day after day. When researchers asked how they were doing, they said they were doing fine.

[[story]]
Matthew Walker tells this story in *Why We Sleep*, drawing on a study by the sleep researcher David Dinges. Dinges took healthy adults and set their nightly sleep for roughly two weeks.

There were several groups. One got a full eight hours a night. One got six hours. One got four. And one was kept awake entirely.

Every day, their concentration was measured with that button-and-light task. The researchers counted the lapses: the moments when the light came on and nobody pressed the button in time. It's a blunt measure, but it catches the small gaps in attention that everyone has when they're tired.

The result Walker dwells on belongs to the six-hour group, because six hours is what many people consider a normal working night.

After about ten days on six hours a night, the six-hour group was as impaired as people who had gone a full 24 hours without any sleep at all. No single night looked dramatic. The loss simply piled up.

Then came the part that makes this study hard to forget. When the researchers asked the restricted groups how impaired they felt, they consistently underestimated it. They believed they had adapted. Their performance said the opposite, and it kept declining while they reported coping.

##The sense of feeling fine was not evidence that they were fine; it was part of the impairment itself.##

Walker adds one more finding. Performance did not bounce back fully, even after several nights of recovery sleep. The debt was not cleared by a single lie-in, or even a few.

He then carries the point into places where a missed light is not a lab statistic. He writes about doctors working long shifts and about drowsy drivers, jobs and moments where a lapse can cost a life. In both cases, the person most likely to say they're alert enough is the one whose judgment on that question has been worn down.

What makes the study useful is its plainness. Nobody in it was sleeping two hours a night or pulling reckless all-nighters. They were getting six hours, the kind of night many people treat as a sensible compromise. Across ten days, that compromise added up to a whole night without sleep, and the people living it didn't notice.

[[lessons]]
$$**Your own gauge is the first thing to go.** The restricted sleepers told researchers they had adapted while their lapses kept climbing. After a run of short nights, asking yourself whether you're sharp enough is a question your tired brain answers badly. An outside check is more reliable than a feeling.$$

$$**Moderate short sleep adds up to a big loss.** Six hours doesn't sound extreme, and no single night of it looked dramatic. But ten of them in a row left people as impaired as a full 24 hours awake. The danger sits in the repetition, not the individual night.$$

$$**One long sleep doesn't wipe the slate.** In the study, several nights of recovery sleep still didn't fully restore performance. That changes the usual plan of running short all week and catching up on Saturday. The weekend lie-in helps, but the debt lingers longer than it feels like it does.$$

[[reflect]]
- When have you insisted you were fine after a short night, then later found a mistake you'd made?
- Who in your life works long shifts where a lapse would really matter?
- How many of your last ten nights were closer to six hours than eight?

&&After your next short night, before one important decision or drive, will you ask someone to check it or wait until you've rested, instead of trusting how you feel?&&

When people decide to achieve more, the first thing to go is usually rest. Lunch moves to the desk. Evenings fill up. The weekend becomes a chance to catch up. It feels like commitment. Robin Sharma argues it is closer to a design flaw, because it throws away half of what performance actually needs.

[[story]]
In *The 5AM Club*, published in 2018, Sharma describes an idea he calls the **Twin Cycles of Elite Performance**. The name sounds grand, but the model is simple. It has two parts, and they are meant to take turns.

The first cycle is high excellence. This is intense, focused work: the stretch where you give a task your full attention and push hard at it.

The second cycle is deep recovery. This is genuine rest, a real break from output, not a slightly quieter version of work.

Most people who want to do more only recognise the first cycle. Effort is what counts, so rest looks like a pause in the real business, or even a small failure of discipline. Sharma turns that around. He argues that constant output without recovery leads to exhaustion, and that recovery is part of performance, not a break from it.

##Recovery, in this model, is where capacity is rebuilt, not where progress stops.##

That changes what a break is for. If rest is only a gap in the work, it is the obvious thing to cut when time is short. If rest is the half of the cycle in which you are restored, then cutting it is like skipping the part of the process that makes the next stretch of hard work possible.

Sharma draws the idea from the way high performers in different fields alternate hard effort with rest. It is worth being clear about its status. He presents it as a principle, drawn from that pattern, not as the result of a measured study. The book is making an argument about how to work, not reporting an experiment.

The practical consequence he stresses is timing. Many people do rest eventually, but only once they are already worn out, when a break is no longer a choice. The book suggests the opposite: build recovery into the day and the week deliberately, in advance, as part of the plan. You do not wait for exhaustion to schedule it for you.

So the two cycles are not rivals. The intense work and the deep recovery belong to the same rhythm. People who keep only the first half, and treat the second as optional, are not getting further. By Sharma's account, they are gradually wearing themselves down.

[[lessons]]
$$**Effort and rest take turns.** The Twin Cycles model says sustained performance comes from alternating intense, focused work with deep recovery, not from constant output. If you only ever run the first cycle, Sharma argues, the result is exhaustion rather than more progress. The recovery half is not a reward for finishing; it is part of what lets you keep going, and the quality of the next focused block depends on it.$$

$$**Plan recovery before you need it.** Most people rest only once they are already worn out, when a break is forced on them. Sharma's advice is to put recovery periods into the day and week on purpose, in advance. A break you have already scheduled is much easier to protect than one you have to justify in the middle of a busy week, and it means rest arrives before exhaustion, not after it.$$

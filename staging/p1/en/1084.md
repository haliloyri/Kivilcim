Picture a research team hunting for the secret behind companies that made a lasting leap from good to great. They expect a bold vision, a brilliant strategy unveiled on day one. What they find looks almost like procrastination. The leaders who built companies that endured kept putting off the strategy question. They were busy with something else first, and that something turned out to matter more than any plan.

[[story]]
For *Good to Great*, Jim Collins and his research team set out to learn what separated companies that made a lasting leap from comparison companies that didn't. They went in with a reasonable guess. Surely the leaders of the great companies began by setting a new direction, with a fresh vision and a sharp strategy, and then brought people in to carry it out.

The evidence didn't show that.

The leaders who took their companies from good to great started somewhere else. Collins describes it with an image that stuck: a bus. First they got the right people on the bus. They got the wrong people off. They made sure the right people were in the right seats. Only after that did they decide where to drive.

Collins sums up their attitude in words roughly like these. The line is his own description of how these leaders thought, not a quote from any single executive:

> "I don't know where we should take this bus, but if we get the right people on it, we'll figure out how to take it someplace great."

It sounds almost careless, a leader admitting he doesn't know the destination. But there's hard logic underneath. The book puts it bluntly: people are not your most important asset. The *right* people are.

The right people also make later changes possible. If someone got on the bus because of where it was headed, a new destination feels like a broken promise. If they got on because of who else was riding, they adapt when the route has to change. ##A team chosen for who they are can survive a plan that turns out to be wrong.##

The comparison companies often worked the other way around. Collins calls their pattern the "genius with a thousand helpers." One strong leader set the vision and then hired helpers to execute it. That could work for a while. But the vision lived in one person's head, and when that leader left, the company often faltered. The helpers had been hired to follow, not to figure things out.

The sharpest case in the book involves two banks. At Wells Fargo, first under Dick Cooley and then under Carl Reichardt, the bank kept hiring outstanding people even when it had no specific job for them. From the outside, that could look like overstocking. Then banking deregulation arrived and shook up the whole industry. Wells Fargo had a bench of talent ready for the shift, and it handled deregulation well.

Bank of America took a different approach, which Collins describes as "weak generals, strong lieutenants." It faced the same upheaval and struggled.

Collins insists none of this justifies mass layoffs. His phrase is "rigorous, not ruthless." The goal is disciplined, deliberate decisions about people, instead of letting those decisions drift. He turns that into a few working rules:

> When in doubt, don't hire. Keep looking.

> When you know a people change is needed, act.

> Put your best people on your biggest opportunities, not your biggest problems.

[[lessons]]
$$**Pick the riders before the route.** The bus comes in a fixed order: right people on, wrong people off, right seats, and only then the destination. A strong team can find a direction and change it when it has to. A weak team can't carry out even an excellent plan. If you settle the "what" first, you end up hiring people to fit a map that may already be wrong.$$

$$**A vision in one head leaves with that head.** The "genius with a thousand helpers" companies could look impressive while the genius was in charge. Their weakness showed up at the handoff. Helpers hired to execute someone else's idea have never had to think for themselves. When the leader left, nobody else could steer.$$

$$**Build the bench before the storm, and aim it at opportunity.** Wells Fargo hired outstanding people before it had jobs for them, and those people were ready when deregulation hit. Bank of America's "weak generals, strong lieutenants" had no such cushion. The same discipline shows up in Collins's rules. Don't let doubt sneak someone onto the bus, don't let delay keep the wrong person in a seat, and send your best people toward the biggest opening rather than the biggest fire.$$

[[reflect]]
- Think about your current project. Did you lock in the plan first and then look for people to carry it?
- Go through your team one by one. Who would you eagerly rehire today, and who would you hesitate over?
- Where are your strongest people right now: on your biggest opportunity, or putting out your biggest fire?
- Is there a people decision you already know you need to make but keep postponing?

&&If you left tomorrow, would your people keep going because they believe in the work, or only because they were following you?&&

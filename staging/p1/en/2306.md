[[open]]
A village curate sits at a window in the English Midlands, watching women knit by hand, stitch after slow stitch. He has built a machine that could do the same work many times faster. He carries it to the most powerful woman in the country, expecting praise, and leaves with something quite different.

[[story]]
Acemoglu and Robinson tell the story of William Lee in *Why Nations Fail* to show how a ruler can see a new technology as a threat. Lee, according to the tradition they draw on, was a curate in the village of Calverton, in Nottinghamshire, who invented a mechanical stocking frame around the end of the sixteenth century. Hand-knitting was a livelihood for many poor families, and his machine promised to produce stockings far faster.

He went to Queen Elizabeth I to ask for a patent, which would have given him protection and a monopoly. She refused. The best-known version of her answer comes from an account published in 1831 by the historian Henson, who drew on the memories of old stocking knitters: she is said to have replied that she had too much love for her poor people, who earned their bread by knitting. That wording was recorded more than two centuries after the event, so treat it as tradition rather than a transcript.

Lee did not stop. The same accounts say that after Elizabeth's death James I also refused to reward him, and that Lee then took his invention to France, where the minister Sully, serving Henry IV, offered him favourable terms. He settled in Rouen with his workers and later met the king. Henry was assassinated soon afterwards, support faded, and Lee is said to have died in 1610, before his brother arrived to join him.

The authors do not read Elizabeth's refusal as kindness. Their reading is political: a ruler fears the creative destruction that new technology brings, because those whose work disappears may turn into a restless, angry group that threatens her own position. The machine itself was never the problem. What mattered was who would lose, and whether those losers could shake the throne.

The detail that stays with you is that the invention survived. It was refined by others and spread over the following centuries. A refusal can delay a new thing and cost its inventor everything, yet it cannot make the underlying idea go away, only move it somewhere else.

There is also a quieter point in how the story is told. The tradition that survives is the one that explains the refusal in terms of the queen's love for her people, and the authors ask us to look past that stated reason to the interest underneath it. A decision-maker rarely announces that she fears losing control. She names a concern that sounds generous, and the real calculation stays out of view.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you launch an improvement, name who loses from it and what they can do about it.** A new tool, process or reorganisation usually helps most people a little and costs a few people a great deal, and those few tend to be exactly the ones who can slow or sink it. If you pretend they do not exist, they will show up later as quiet resistance, delays and rumours. Ask early who is affected, what they fear, and whether there is a way to share the gain or soften the loss, so the change survives its first year.$$

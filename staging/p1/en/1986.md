Take a couple who are both much taller than most people. Their grown children are tall too, taller than average, yet on the whole a little shorter than their parents. Take a couple who are both unusually short, and the grown children turn out a little taller than they are. It happens so reliably that someone had to ask why.

[[story]]
The man who asked was Francis Galton, and Peter Bernstein follows his answer in *Against the Gods* as one of the ideas that taught people to read patterns in numbers. Galton wanted real families, not guesses, and he got them in an unusual way: he offered prizes for family records, and, in his own words, the offer was largely responded to. He took care not to mention this particular question in his prospectus, for fear that it would bias the returns.

His results appeared in 1886 in the Journal of the Anthropological Institute, under the title "Regression towards mediocrity in hereditary stature". His data were the heights of 930 adult children and of their parentages, 205 in number. To compare mothers with fathers, he multiplied each woman's height by 1.08, which in his words amounted to adding a little less than one-twelfth. He then averaged the two parents into a single figure, the mid-parent.

The pattern was steady. In the population he studied, the level of mediocrity, his word for the average, stood at 68¼ inches. Galton found that the height-deviate of the children, on average, was two-thirds of the height-deviate of the mid-parent. By that rule, a mid-parent four inches above the average had children who stood about 2⅔ inches above it. Below the average, the same rule pulled the children up toward the middle. It was a pattern in the group, and no single family had to follow it.

He chose the word regression for this drift back toward the middle, and sometimes used the older word reversion for it, and he had seen something like it a decade earlier. According to accounts of his sweet-pea experiments in the mid-1870s, he sent friends packets of sweet-pea seeds, each packet of one size, and compared the seeds they grew with the seeds that were planted. The offspring of the largest seeds were large. On average they were not as large as their parents.

The drift runs from both ends, from the tall side and from the short side. It keeps a population from fanning out into a tribe of giants and a tribe of dwarfs with each generation, and the numbers show the pull working in both directions at once.

[[lessons]]
$$**Expect an extreme result to be followed by a milder one, and do not credit the change to whatever you did in between.** A café has a record Saturday, so the owner rewrites the menu, and the next Saturday is quieter. The quiet day is partly the ordinary pull back toward the usual level, and the new menu may deserve less of the blame than it seems. When you see an unusually good or bad result, ask what the normal range looks like before you decide that something caused the change, and keep a note of the ordinary weeks around it.$$

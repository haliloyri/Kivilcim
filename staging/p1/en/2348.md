[[story]]
Tara Westover's memoir *Educated* is an account of growing up in a survivalist Mormon family in Idaho. In it she describes abuse by an older brother, whom she calls "Shawn," a pseudonym. She says she is now estranged from half of her family. The memoir became a bestseller, and soon the people it describes began to answer.

Her parents have disputed parts of the book, speaking through an attorney. According to the Salt Lake Tribune, he said that the recollections "are not what she told them at the time." The Deseret News later reported more detail on where the two sides diverge. Westover writes that her father called her a name over her clothing. Her mother, asked about it, said no. Her father said he had no recollection but that he is not perfect.

There is also a disagreement about the setting. The memoir describes a remote mountain home, while her mother's family says it stands on the main road through Clifton. On her father's health, Westover has suggested a diagnosis, and her mother rejects what she calls an armchair diagnosis. On the brother's head injuries, Westover says they made his volatility worse, while her mother prefers to think they softened him. These are matters on which the two accounts simply sit side by side.

The family is not of one mind, though. The Deseret News reported that one of Westover's brothers wrote that there was "no doubt there was abuse, neglect and other awful choices." The brother she calls Shawn declined to be interviewed.

So here is what can be said. A memoir is one person's account, checked against documents where they exist, and some of it can never be checked by outsiders. Her parents' objections are on the record, and so is the support from one sibling. A careful reader holds each claim at its true weight: what Westover wrote, what her parents say through a lawyer, what a brother wrote, and what no one has shown.

That discipline is harder than it sounds. The temptation is to pick a side and then treat every uncertain detail as settled. A reader who stays with the record, and not with a hunch, can say "I do not know" about a scene and still take the whole account seriously.

[[lessons]]
$$**When two people disagree about the past, write down who said what before you decide what happened.** A disputed memoir is a clean example, because each claim has an owner: the author, the parents, a sibling. In a team conflict about who promised what, the same step stops a hunch from becoming a verdict. List the claims, mark which ones have a document or a witness behind them, and say plainly which are still one person's word against another's. You may still decide in the end, but you will know how much weight your decision can carry and what you might still need to ask.$$

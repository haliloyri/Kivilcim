In a botanical garden in the Bronx, a nineteen-year-old who dreads talking to women gives himself a task. Over the course of a month he will talk to a hundred of them. He is forcing himself straight into the thing he fears, again and again, to see what really happens.

[[story]]
He did not get a date. By published accounts, that is how Albert Ellis's experiment ended, and yet he later described it as a success. The young Ellis, who would go on to become one of the founders of cognitive-behavioral therapy, had grown up in the Bronx with an exaggerated fear of public speaking and, in his teens, a deep shyness around women.

At nineteen he forced himself to speak to about a hundred women in the Bronx Botanical Garden over a month. What he reported afterward was that he had desensitized himself to his fear of rejection. Whatever he had hoped for in each conversation, the lasting result was that the catastrophe he feared never arrived.

The number is worth a closer look. A hundred conversations in a month comes to roughly three a day. A single brave moment would not have been enough, because fear does not give up after one defeat. But meeting the same fear day after day makes it more familiar each time, and a little more ordinary. The desensitization Ellis described fits that kind of build-up.

Years later, in 1955, Ellis presented what he first called rational therapy. In it the therapist helps a client see that their own beliefs feed their emotional pain, and urges the client to act on that insight. Ellis's method was never only talk. Trying a new behavior also tested how solid the old belief really was.

Oliver Burkeman's *The Antidote* argues against positive-thinking slogans and for a closer look at the thing you dread, a tradition with ancient roots. Ellis himself said he drew on the Stoics. A saying attributed to the Stoic philosopher Epictetus captures the idea: people are disturbed not by things, but by the views they take of them.

In practice that means much of a fear lives in the forecast made before the encounter. "If I'm rejected, I won't be able to bear it" cannot be tested without being rejected. The garden walks ran that test again and again, and each conversation returned a small piece of evidence about how large the nightmare really was.

One caution about the story itself: the figure of a hundred women comes from published accounts, and other tellings may give the details a little differently. The core stays the same. Ellis gave himself small, repeated doses of what frightened him, and he judged the result from his own experience.

[[lessons]]
$$**Take what you fear in small, repeated doses, and write down what actually happens each time.** If speaking up in a meeting or approaching a stranger makes your stomach tighten, it is usually your forecast that frightens you, not the event. This week, try it three times in a low-stakes setting and note the outcome in a line each. After a few rounds you hold real data in place of a prediction, and the fear finally has something concrete to be measured against. Each small trial makes the next one lighter, so the fear shrinks to its actual size, and you can keep going without waiting for courage to arrive first.$$

A medical paper describes twelve children. There is no comparison group, only twelve case histories, which is the entire sample. Within a few years that single paper is being quoted in newspaper columns and television discussions as if it settled a question about every child in the country.

[[story]]
The paper appeared in the Lancet in 1998, written by Andrew Wakefield and colleagues. Its twelve patients were children, eleven of them boys, with an average age of six, who had been referred to a paediatric gastroenterology unit with bowel problems and a loss of skills they had previously acquired. According to the paper's own summary, in eight of the twelve the parents linked the start of the behavioural symptoms to the MMR vaccine.

That is the material behind the scare that Ben Goldacre examines in the last chapter of *Bad Science*. His point about the size of the study is blunt. Even if it had been immaculately conducted, a case series of twelve children's clinical anecdotes could never have justified the conclusion that MMR causes autism, because the numbers were far too small.

The newspapers, he writes, repeated one man's concerns again and again, mostly without describing the methods. Studies finding no link, published in full in peer-reviewed journals, were ignored. When Tony Blair declined to say whether his son had been given the vaccine, the commentators arrived: Goldacre names Carol Vorderman and Fiona Phillips among those who shared their worries with the nation. In 2002, he says, fewer than a third of broadsheet reports on MMR mentioned that the overwhelming evidence showed no link.

The formal verdicts came later. On 28 January 2010 the General Medical Council found Wakefield "misleading", "dishonest" and "irresponsible" in how he described where the children in the paper came from, implying they were routine clinic referrals. The children had undergone colonoscopy, lumbar puncture and barium studies, tests carried out for research rather than in their own clinical interest and without ethics committee approval. The Lancet retracted the paper that February, and in May his name came off the medical register.

Goldacre's column that day accepted all of this. He noted that inquiries in medicine usually look for failures in the system rather than one person to blame, and he still refused to leave the blame with one man. In his view the media were equally guilty. He saw the scare end because Wakefield's own conduct broke the emotional story newspapers had built, not because anyone had learned a lesson, and so he predicted another MMR.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you pass on a health story, ask how many people were studied.** If a friend tells you that a small group who tried a new gadget were all delighted, that shows where an idea might begin and says nothing yet about whether it works. A small number does not disprove a claim, but it also does not earn the loud, repeated coverage it sometimes gets. Asking for the number takes about ten seconds and slows you down before you forward a headline, and it makes you notice when one source keeps saying the same thing, which is not the same as many sources agreeing.$$

[[open]]
Walk into any meeting and ask yourself who the leader is. You'll probably look at whoever has the biggest title. Now picture the moment a deadline slips and the room goes tense. Someone speaks up and says part of it is on them. Watch where everyone's attention goes next. Often it moves away from the title and toward that person. Simon Sinek built a whole argument around that shift.

[[story]]
In his 2014 book *Leaders Eat Last*, Simon Sinek starts from a simple claim that runs against how most organizations are drawn on paper. The org chart tells you who has authority. It doesn't tell you who leads.

> Leadership is a choice, not a rank.

That's the heart of it. Sinek argues that someone can sit in a senior role for years without ever leading anyone. Someone with no formal authority at all can lead every day. Rank is something you're given. Leadership is something you choose to do, again and again.

So what does the choice actually look like? For Sinek, real leaders give up their own comfort, and sometimes their own interests, so that the people in their care feel safe and can grow. He sets this against a different kind of person, one who uses a position mainly to protect themselves. When things get hard, that person lets others absorb the risk so their own advancement stays intact. Both might hold the same job title. What they do with it runs in opposite directions.

The book's title comes from a custom in the US Marine Corps. When Marines line up for food, the most junior go first and the senior officers eat last. Nobody makes a speech about it, and it isn't written up as a policy about values. It's a small, visible habit that happens every day, and it tells everyone in line where they stand. The people with the most rank take on the discomfort of waiting, and maybe of getting whatever is left. ##The most powerful people in the line are the ones who wait.##

Sinek connects this to what he calls the Circle of Safety. When people trust that their leader will protect them, something changes in how they behave. They stop spending energy guarding themselves against each other: covering their backs, hoarding information, avoiding blame. That energy goes into cooperating and taking initiative instead. The danger outside the group doesn't disappear. But inside the circle, people no longer have to treat each other as threats.

He also brings the idea into business. Sinek describes a manufacturing company that hit an economic downturn. The obvious move would have been layoffs. Its leadership chose shared sacrifice instead, spreading unpaid time off across everyone so that no one had to lose their job. Sinek uses it as an example of putting people before numbers. It wasn't a vague statement of care. It was a decision people could see, and it cost something.

Put those pieces together and the opening question gets harder to answer. The person with the highest title is in charge. But the person people actually follow is often someone else. It's whoever steps up, takes the blame, or covers a colleague when doing so costs them.

[[lessons]]
$$**Leadership happens in moments, not promotions.** Sinek's line that leadership is a choice, not a rank, moves leadership out of the org chart and into specific moments. You don't become a leader on the day your title changes. You act as one in the moments when you decide whether to step forward or stay safe. Two people with identical titles can make opposite choices in the same moment, and the people around them will notice.$$

$$**Leaders absorb the cost so others feel safe.** Marine officers eating last is a small thing, and that's the point. The leader takes the discomfort so the juniors don't have to. Sinek's contrast is sharp. A leader gives up personal comfort so others can grow. A position-holder gives up other people's security so they can advance. The test is who ends up paying when things get hard.$$

$$**People commit only when they feel protected.** The Circle of Safety explains why this matters beyond decency. When people trust their leader to cover them, they stop defending themselves against each other and start cooperating. The company that chose shared sacrifice over layoffs made that protection visible. Everyone could see that the people inside the circle came before the numbers.$$

[[reflect]]
- Think of someone you'd follow without being told to. What did they actually do, apart from any title they held?
- Recall the last time someone needed cover and you chose to protect your own comfort instead. What would it have cost you to step in?
- In your team, who makes it feel safe to speak up? Is it the person in charge, or someone else?
- Where in your work do people spend energy guarding themselves against each other rather than against the real problem?

[[open]]
A hospital room, an IV pole, and a computer a nurse has left switched on. The man in the patient's gown knows exactly how to read the images on the screen, because he has read many like them from the other side of the desk. This time the name at the top is his own.

[[story]]
Paul Kalanithi's memoir *When Breath Becomes Air* begins at that screen. In May 2013 he was 36 years old and in the last year of his neurosurgery residency at Stanford, with about fifteen months left before ten years of training would end. He had won awards and had job offers waiting. For six years he had been reading scans like this one, and now he sat in a hospital gown, attached to an IV pole, looking at his own CT.

A nurse had left the computer in the room. He went through the images the way he had been taught, lungs, then spine, then liver. The tumors in his lungs were too many to count. His spine was deformed. A whole lobe of his liver had been destroyed.

Nobody had to translate for him. The pictures told him how far the cancer had spread, and he understood it at once, in the same trained way he would have understood it for a stranger. That is a strange kind of knowledge to hold, because it arrives without any of the softening a patient usually gets from a doctor's careful words.

His wife, Lucy, was beside him. She was a doctor too, an internist, so she could read what was on the screen as well. Quietly, almost as if she were reading from a script, she asked whether there was any possibility that it was something else.

He answered with one word: "No."

Her question is the one almost anyone would ask in that room. His answer is the one only a trained eye could give so quickly. Between them sit two people who both know the medicine and who still have to say the thing out loud to each other.

The scene works as the opening of the book because it removes the distance he had spent a decade learning to keep. A surgeon-in-training is taught to look at pictures like these calmly and name what is there. On that day the calm was still available to him, and so was the knowledge, but the person in the picture was the one holding the mouse.

Kalanithi had been moving toward a career as a neurosurgeon and scientist. The scan did not ask him whether that plan was still reasonable. It simply showed what was there, and he had been trained, better than most people, to look at that without turning away.

[[lessons]]
$$**Read your own bad news the way you would read a stranger's: in order, one part at a time.** When the problem is yours, whether it is a failed audit, a lost contract or a test result, the mind jumps to the verdict before it has looked at the evidence. Going through the parts in a fixed order, as a clinician goes through a scan, gives you a list of what is actually there before you decide what it means. It also lets the people beside you ask their own questions and get a straight answer, instead of a reaction.$$

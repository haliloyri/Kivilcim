[[open]]
In the back of a car heading out of Manhattan toward Greenwich, a chief executive takes a call from the Treasury Secretary and the head of the New York Fed. He had counted on about a month to find a buyer for his firm. The voices on the phone give him two days, and the clock they name belongs to markets on the other side of the world.

[[story]]
Andrew Ross Sorkin's *Too Big to Fail* tells how Wall Street's giants came apart in 2008, and Bear Stearns was the first to go. This story is about its last weekend, 14 to 16 March. According to the Wall Street Journal's later reconstruction, Alan Schwartz, Bear's chief executive, had expected roughly a month to find a buyer until that Friday call from Henry Paulson and Timothy Geithner. Paulson told him: "You need to have a deal by Sunday night."

That Friday Bear's shares fell 47 percent, to $30. A loan backed by the Fed and routed through JPMorgan, guaranteed for up to 28 days, had let the firm open for business that morning. The chief financial officer, Samuel Molinaro, heard about the Sunday deadline while stopped for coffee. "I thought we had 28 days," he said.

The deadline had a reason. Asian markets would open around 6 p.m. New York time on Sunday, and the officials wanted a deal announced before then. JPMorgan, led by Jamie Dimon, first floated a price of $8 to $12 a share. After its people read Bear's books, the idea shrank to $4 or $5. Paulson, worried about being seen to bail out Wall Street, told Dimon that sounded high to him.

Then the number dropped again. JPMorgan's Doug Braunstein told Gary Parr, who was advising Bear: "The number's $2." Schwartz answered, "Two dollars is better than nothing." Bear's board approved the deal unanimously at about 6:30 p.m., shortly before Asia opened. The year before, a Bear share had touched $171.51.

Many of the shareholders who lost almost everything were Bear's own employees. Their anger moved the buyer. On 24 March JPMorgan raised its offer to $10 a share, and agreed to absorb the first $1 billion of losses on the assets the Fed was financing, which left the Fed with about $29 billion.

Look at what moved the price. It fell as the buyer read the books, and it fell again as the hours ran out. Schwartz had planned on a calendar of weeks, while the calendar that counted was set by a trading floor in Asia. Nobody on the Bear side could stop that clock, so the only thing left to negotiate was the number.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask early which clock the deal really runs on.** The date you were given is often not the date that governs the outcome, because the other side, a regulator or a market may be working to a different schedule. A shop owner renegotiating a supply contract may think the month-end is the deadline, while the supplier's accounting period closes earlier and its manager has to show a signed deal by then. Once you know the real clock, you can decide what to do before it, instead of discovering it on the last day, when the only variable left is the price.$$

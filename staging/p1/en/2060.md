[[open]]
A man is holding a certificate from a professional association. It cost $60 and says "certified member". The name on it is Henrietta, a cat who died about a year earlier. The association asked for no exam, no proof of qualifications, and apparently never checked whether she was alive.

[[story]]
Ben Goldacre gives a whole chapter of *Bad Science* to Gillian McKeith, and the starting point is her CV. McKeith, a nutritionist with a weekly Channel 4 programme, her own food range and bestselling books, billed herself as "Dr Gillian McKeith PhD". Under "Professional Associations" her CV said she was a certified member of the American Association of Nutritional Consultants, the AANC.

Goldacre tested that line in his Guardian column in 2004. He had paid the same association $60 and received a certificate made out to his cat, Henrietta, known as Hettie, who had died about a year before. In his account, membership needed a name, an address and the money. There was no exam, no check on qualifications and no assessment of anyone's practice.

Her doctorate raised the same question. Goldacre described it as a non-accredited correspondence-course PhD from an American college. In February 2007 a reader of his website, badscience.net, complained to the Advertising Standards Authority about her use of "Dr" on that basis. According to Goldacre, the ASA's draft adjudication concluded that the claim "Dr" was likely to mislead, and McKeith agreed, before it was published, to stop using the title in her advertising.

Goldacre said the title was not what troubled him most. He cared about the biology she presented as fact. She said chlorophyll would oxygenate your blood. Chlorophyll only makes oxygen when light is present, and the inside of your gut is dark. He called it the kind of mistake a 14-year-old could correct.

The footnotes followed the same pattern. McKeith's long documents carried neat superscript numbers pointing to trials, studies and papers. When Goldacre followed the numbers and checked the references, he reported that they often were not what the main text claimed. Some led to magazines such as *Health Store News* rather than to academic journals, and that was the source given for her claim that chlorophyll returned red-blood cell counts to normal within days in anaemic animals. On screen, Channel 4 had branded her a "clinical nutritionist", interpreting blood tests in a laboratory setting with all the authority a white coat could supply.

The detail worth keeping is what the certificate actually certified. It showed that a payment had gone through. Her CV lined up titles, memberships and degrees, and each one looked like a credential, yet behind none of them stood a test anyone could point to. Goldacre made that visible with a dead cat, because a document that a cat can collect cannot count as evidence of expertise.

[[lessons]]
$$**Judge a credential by what its issuer asked of the person who got it.** If a plumber hands you a card saying "certified master", you can ask which exam the card came from and who marked it. When payment alone was enough, the card shows that someone bought it and says nothing about whether the work is sound. Asking that one question lets you tell the paper around a person from the claim itself, and it keeps you checking the claim even when the paper looks impressive. You can put the same question to a course certificate, a trade badge or a line on a consultant's CV.$$

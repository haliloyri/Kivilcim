In a Johannesburg hospital, a doctor studies a newborn and remarks that he is a very light-skinned baby. No man stands beside the bed to claim him. When the question comes, the mother says the father is from Swaziland, and the doctors write that down and ask nothing more.

[[story]]
Under apartheid, a birth certificate recorded three things about a child: race, tribe and nationality. Trevor Noah's certificate, according to his own account, tells a story that is only partly true, and the choices behind it were made in the first hours of his life.

When he was born, the doctors noted how light his skin was, and there was no father present to explain it. His mother said the father came from Swaziland. Noah suspects that the doctors probably understood this was not the whole truth, but that they needed an explanation to write down, and this one would do.

She also said he had been born in KaNgwane, a semi-sovereign homeland set aside for the Swazi people. The two details fit together: a Swazi father and a birth in the Swazi homeland. As a result, Noah writes, his certificate does not label him Xhosa, which he technically was. His father's name does not appear on it either. The paper simply says he came from another country, and it contains nothing the government could not accept.

After the birth, his mother took him from the hospital to a new flat she had rented in Joubert Park, next to Hillbrow. His father had not wanted to be involved at first, but once the child existed he insisted on being part of his life. The three of them became an informal family, and Noah and his mother sometimes sneaked over to visit him.

What matters here is what the form recorded and what it did not. It recorded the child himself. As Noah tells it, nothing on the certificate pointed to the relationship between his parents, so the answers that were given left no visible trace of an illegal one. A rule that bans something still has to be enforced through paper, and paper only knows what has been written on it.

Noah describes himself as proof of his parents' "criminality" rather than of their love, and the title of his memoir builds on that idea. The detail from the birth certificate shows how heavily a single line on a form could weigh on a child's life, and how one sentence in a hospital room could turn into an official record that stayed with him.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you fill in a box, read line by line what the rule or form actually measures.** Most regulations and forms ask for less than we fear and check only certain boxes. Noah's mother knew which fields a birth certificate held and thought about her child's record within those fields, so she did not have to explain anything the paper never asked. In a job application, a contract or a company policy, you can check first what the text really says, and then whether the thing you are afraid of is actually written there. The answer is often narrower than expected.$$

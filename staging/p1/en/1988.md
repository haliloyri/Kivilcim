The shop in Newport paid five percent of every sale in rent and still outsold every other Ben Franklin store around it. Its owner had paid back his father-in-law in under three years. What he never got from the landlord was one short sentence about what happens when the lease runs out.

[[story]]
In 1969 a new store opened in Newport, Arkansas. It was Wal-Mart number 18, and its founder had last been in town as a variety-store owner who lost his shop. In *Made in America*, the 1992 memoir he wrote with John Huey, Sam Walton tells how that first shop slipped out of his hands.

He opened it on 1 September 1945: a Ben Franklin franchise of about 5,000 square feet, for which he paid Butler Brothers $25,000. Twenty thousand of that was a loan from his father-in-law. The rent was five percent of sales, so every dollar a customer spent at the counter put a few cents in the landlord's pocket as well.

The store had done about $72,000 in sales before Walton took it over, so he was not buying a proven machine. He worked the place hard. According to Harvard Business School's account of the numbers, sales were $105,000 in the first full year and $250,000 in the fifth, with profit of $30,000 to $40,000 a year. At that level the rent alone came to roughly $12,500 a year. He repaid the family loan within thirty months. He also picked up a second lease that Kroger had left empty and opened the Eagle Store, mostly to stop a rival, John Dunham, from expanding.

Then the lease ran out. It had no renewal clause. The landlord, P. K. Holmes, declined to renew and gave the store to his own son. Walton sold him the inventory and fixtures for $50,000, according to the same account. He kept the money and what he had learned. He did not keep the address.

Look at how the loss arrived. Walton had not failed; the store was doing well enough that someone else wanted it. A percentage rent meant the landlord could follow the growth year by year, and when the paper gave him the option, he took it. The weakness was a single missing sentence in a document signed five years earlier, when nobody had a reason to argue about endings.

Walton left Newport and moved the family to Bentonville in 1950. He had a repaid debt, five years of retail practice and no building. Nineteen years later he came back to the same town under his own company's name.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you pour years into something, find out who decides how it ends.** A rental, a supplier contract, a manager's verbal promise and a partnership handshake all come with an end date or an exit clause, and whoever holds that clause holds more of your future than you do. Success sharpens the question: the better a shop, an account or a project performs, the more tempting it becomes for the owner of the terms to take it over. Ask for the ending in writing at the start, when asking costs one slightly awkward minute and nobody has anything to lose yet.$$

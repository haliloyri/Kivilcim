*What important truth do very few people agree with you on?*

Take a moment and try to answer it. Not a clever answer, an honest one. If you find yourself reaching for something most people already believe, you're in good company. This is Peter Thiel's favorite interview question, and he finds that most people can't really answer it.

[[story]]
In *Zero to One*, written with Blake Masters and published in 2014, Thiel explains why he likes this question so much. It sounds simple. It turns out to be hard.

Answering it well takes two things at once. First, knowledge that isn't common: you have to actually know something most people don't. Second, courage: you have to be willing to say something unpopular to the person deciding whether to hire you. Most people fall back on repeating a conventional view.

Thiel's broader point is that the question points at something real. He argues that there are still "secrets" in the world: important truths that are unknown or unappreciated. And he argues that great companies are built on them.

He sorts these secrets into two kinds. Secrets of nature are truths about the physical world, waiting to be discovered. Secrets about people are things people don't know about themselves, or things they hide because they don't want others to know.

So why do so few people go looking? Thiel lists several reasons people stopped believing in secrets. Incrementalism teaches us to take small, safe steps. Risk aversion makes being wrong feel worse than never trying. Complacency lets comfortable people stop asking. And then there is what he calls "flatness": the belief that if something were possible, someone smarter would already have done it.

##The quiet assumption that someone smarter got there first is often what stops people from looking at all.##

His examples come from companies that saw what others missed. Airbnb looked at a situation where most people saw nothing and found untapped supply and unaddressed demand sitting side by side. Uber and Lyft built businesses on connecting riders with private drivers. In each case, the opportunity existed before anyone acted on it. It simply wasn't widely appreciated.

That is why the interview question matters to him. A candidate who can name a true thing few people accept is showing the same habit of mind that finds these openings.

[[lessons]]
$$**Opportunity hides in disagreement.** Airbnb found untapped supply and unaddressed demand in a place where others saw nothing. If an idea is one everyone already nods along to, the opportunity inside it has probably been taken. The more promising territory is a truth most people don't yet accept.$$

$$**"Someone would have done it" is an assumption.** Thiel calls this belief flatness, and he lists it alongside incrementalism, risk aversion and complacency as reasons people stopped believing in secrets. It feels like humility or realism. In practice it ends the search before it starts, without anyone checking whether it's true.$$

$$**Not every secret is technical.** Thiel's second category, secrets about people, covers what people don't know about themselves or choose to hide. You don't need a lab to find those. You need to notice what people want but don't say, which is often right in front of you.$$

[[reflect]]
- Have you ever held back an idea because you assumed someone must have already tried it?
- What is something you believe from direct experience that most people around you would doubt?
- When you last answered a hard question, did you say what you think or what sounded safe?

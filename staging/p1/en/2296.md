[[open]]
A grey ocean, thousands of metres of water beneath it, and a nuclear submarine somewhere down there that has stopped answering. The Navy has a rough idea of where it was heading and none of where it ended up. A mathematician asks a roomful of specialists to do something odd: each of them is to write down a guess.

[[story]]
In May 1968 the American nuclear submarine USS Scorpion was lost in the Atlantic with all 99 men aboard. The Navy could not simply look for the wreck, because nobody knew where to look. The ocean in question was huge and the water was deep. The question was not how to dive down to it but where to send the ships and sensors in the first place.

According to published accounts, which James Surowiecki draws on in *The Wisdom of Crowds*, a Navy scientist named John Craven took a different route from a single grand theory. He gathered a group of people with different kinds of expertise: submarine men, mathematicians, salvage specialists. He then asked each of them to say what they thought had happened to the boat and where it lay. Craven is described as having built a betting-style game around it, so that each person gave a best guess rather than arguing for a favourite theory.

These people had no way of working out the answer from the data alone, and none of them could say with confidence how the submarine had died. Each held one piece: how a boat of that class might fail, how fast it would sink, how currents and the sea bed would carry it. No single piece located the wreck. Craven combined their separate guesses into one estimate of the spot.

The ship search then went to that spot. About five months after the loss, in the autumn of 1968, the Navy found Scorpion. Reports say the wreck lay roughly 220 yards from the position the group's combined guess had pointed to. That is a tiny distance on the scale of an ocean.

A few details of the search are told differently by different writers, including exactly how Craven did his combining and how much credit belongs to which person, so treat the story as a good illustration rather than a clean experiment. The part that holds up is the logic. Nobody in the room knew the answer. Each person knew a sliver, and each guessed with some error. Because they came from different fields, their errors did not all lean the same way. When Craven put the guesses together, the errors cancelled and the shared signal was left standing.

The method asks for people who are different from each other and who give their guesses separately, not in a debate where one confident theory sweeps the room.

[[lessons]]
$$**When nobody can see the whole answer, ask people with different pieces for separate best guesses and combine them.** A real problem often lacks one person who knows everything, whether it is the cause of a failure, the reason a customer left or the date a project will really land. Pick people from different corners, ask each for a number or a location, not an argument, and put the answers together. The mix of backgrounds matters more than how impressive each person is. You then act on the pooled estimate and keep your faith in any single theory in proportion to the evidence.$$

Think back to your last real outburst. Maybe it was a raised voice, a cutting line, or a message you wish you could unsend. In the moment it felt completely justified. Afterwards you replayed it and wondered why on earth you said that. You weren't thinking clearly, and you knew it only later. Daniel Goleman argues that this isn't a flaw in your character. It's a feature of your wiring.

[[story]]
In his 1995 book *Emotional Intelligence*, Goleman opens his discussion of runaway emotion with a real case that is hard to read. In 1960s Manhattan, a burglar named Richard Robles broke into an apartment. He came to steal, but the break-in escalated, and it ended with two young women dead. Years later, trying to explain what had happened inside him, Robles said something that sounds almost childish next to the horror of it:

> "My head just went bananas."

Goleman takes that phrase seriously. He's not excusing anything. He thinks it describes something real about how the brain works. He calls it an **emotional hijacking**: the moment an emotion floods in and overwhelms rational thought.

The story he builds comes from the work of neuroscientist Joseph LeDoux. Deep in the brain sits the amygdala, which Goleman describes as a kind of sentinel or alarm, constantly scanning for threats. What LeDoux showed is that sensory signals can reach the amygdala by a fast shortcut, *before* the neocortex, the thinking brain, has finished making sense of them. So the alarm can go off and the body can start reacting while the reasoning part of you is still catching up.

Normally there's a manager in the building. The prefrontal cortex acts as the brain's regulator of emotion, the part that says *wait, is this actually what it looks like?* But during a hijacking, the amygdala's alarm can override that manager. For a stretch of time, the part of you that reacts fastest is running the show, and it's also the part least able to judge.

Goleman gives a second case that shows how badly the shortcut can misfire. A father came home, and his teenage daughter jumped out to startle him as a prank. In that split second he mistook her for an intruder, and he shot her.

##The alarm didn't wait for the facts; it fired on a shape and a sudden movement, and the thinking brain arrived too late.##

Why would we be built like this? In evolutionary terms, Goleman explains, the speed made sense. When the danger was physical, a creature that reacted instantly survived more often than one that paused to deliberate. The trouble is that the same alarm now fires at insults, frustrations and perceived slights: a sharp comment in a meeting, being cut off mid-sentence, an email with the wrong tone. There's no predator, but the body responds as if there were one.

Then Goleman turns to what we do once the alarm is ringing. Drawing on research into anger, including the work of Dolf Zillmann, he argues that anger builds on itself. Each angry thought adds fuel for the next. That leads to his most counterintuitive point. Venting, the classic advice to "let it all out", tends to *prolong* anger rather than release it. What actually helps is time away from the trigger. Leaving the situation, distracting yourself, and questioning the angry thoughts give the body's arousal a chance to subside. Cooling down takes real time, and there's no shortcut through it.

[[lessons]]
$$**You can react before you think.** LeDoux's shortcut is the heart of this story. Signals can hit the amygdala before the neocortex has processed them, which is how the father fired before he'd recognised his own daughter. When a reaction arrives with total certainty and no delay, that speed is itself a clue. It may have come from the alarm, and your judgment may not have had its turn yet.$$

$$**An ancient alarm is answering modern problems.** The sentinel evolved to keep us alive against physical danger, and it hasn't updated its settings. Criticism, frustration and a perceived slight can trip it just as hard as a real threat. Knowing this lets you treat the surge in your chest as information about your wiring, not proof that the other person is an enemy.$$

$$**Buy a gap, don't blow off steam.** The anger research Goleman cites, including Zillmann's, shows that venting and angry rumination feed the fire. The aim isn't to suppress the feeling. It's to create enough time and distance for the prefrontal manager to come back online. Stepping away can look like avoidance, but it's what lets the arousal fade so the thinking part of you can decide what you do next.$$

[[reflect]]
- Which situations most reliably set off your alarm: criticism, being interrupted, feeling ignored, time pressure?
- What physical signs tell you a hijacking is starting? Maybe heat in your face, a tight jaw, or your speech speeding up.
- The last time you vented, did it actually calm you down, or did you walk away angrier than you started?
- Think of one outburst you regret. At what exact moment could a pause have fit in?

&&Which trigger catches you most often, and what one step will you take the next time you feel it firing, before you say a word?&&

[[open]]
In a Cleveland laboratory, a monkey wakes after surgery, follows a person's movements with its eyes and snaps at a colleague's fingers. Everything about the head works. The body under it does not move at all. The team has done something new with a mammal brain, and everyone in the room can see which piece is missing.

[[story]]
Mary Roach's *Stiff* has a chapter on decapitation and head transplants. The best-known modern experiment in that line was carried out by Robert J. White, a neurosurgeon who taught at Case Western Reserve University for about 40 years and ran a brain research laboratory at Cleveland Metropolitan General Hospital, now MetroHealth.

White's road to the head began with brains. In the early 1960s his team isolated a mammalian brain and kept it alive under deep cooling. In one experiment they connected a dog's brain to the blood vessels in another dog's neck. According to a profile from the University of St. Thomas, the cooling work stretched the time surgeons had to operate on the brain from a few minutes to an hour or two, and methods used today to treat brain and spinal injuries stem largely from his research.

In 1970 his team transplanted the head of one rhesus monkey onto the body of another. The cranial nerves stayed intact and the head received blood from the new body, but the spinal cord was severed at the neck, so the animal was paralyzed from the neck down. When it woke, it could hear, smell, taste and follow objects with its eyes. It could not move its new body, because no technology existed to reconnect a severed spinal cord. Accounts give eight or nine days as the time before the immune system rejected the transplant and the monkey died. It was the first of about 30 such operations.

White said the aim was to study how the brain could be isolated and how slowly a body rejects a new one, and he hoped the technique might someday extend the lives of people paralyzed from the neck down. He preferred the term "total body transplant", because he considered the brain the home of the spirit. The work drew sharp criticism. The nerve-regrowth researcher Jerry Silver called it fairly barbaric, and animal rights activists called White names such as "Dr. Frankenstein". White acknowledged the ethical concerns but argued that the major advances in transplantation and surgery had come out of centuries of animal experiment.

What the experiment settled was narrow: a head could be kept alive and aware on another body for about a week. What it could not settle was the part that would have made it useful to a person, a spinal cord that connects.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you celebrate the part that works, name the piece that is still missing.** A baker who spends months perfecting a sourdough starter may find, on opening day, that she has no way to keep loaves warm on a delivery run. The starter is excellent and the business still stalls on the unglamorous piece. Writing down, early on, the one link without which the whole thing cannot help anyone tells you where the next month's effort should go. It also keeps a good demonstration from being mistaken for a finished product, which is a mistake teams make most easily when the demonstration is impressive and people want to hear good news.$$

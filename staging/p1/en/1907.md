A gas station attendant has just turned down a young man's suitcase, watch and ID as payment for a tank of gas. The young man walks back to his car, looks at the McDonald's uniform he is wearing, picks up his briefcase, and heads for the first front door on a quiet street.

[[story]]
The attendant said no, and that left Matthew Dicks, nineteen years old, with a problem. It was a Sunday in June 1990. He was driving home from Laconia, New Hampshire, in a 1978 Chevy Malibu, and a tire had blown out near the Massachusetts border. He had spent his last cash on a new tire, the gas gauge was close to empty, and home was about three hours away.

Dicks tells this story in *Storyworthy* as one of the moments from his own life that he turned into something worth telling. The plan he came up with was a bad one. He worked at McDonald's, so he put on his uniform, took his briefcase, and went door to door in a nearby neighborhood, telling people he was collecting for McDonald's Children's Charities.

It worked. The first house gave him three dollars. The second gave him twenty. At about a dollar a gallon, twenty-three dollars was enough to get him home.

If the story stopped there, it would be a funny confession about a stupid thing a teenager did. Dicks goes further. In the book he describes a man at one of those doors who told him his wife had died of cancer and that his children had not come to see him in more than five years. The same events, in the same order, were suddenly about something else: loneliness.

Dicks promised to pay the charity back. In a blog post from October 2009, he wrote that he drops a dollar into the McDonald's charity containers almost every morning before work, and that by then he had given $631 in single-dollar bills, not counting loose change. His own verdict, in the same post: "It doesn't make what I did right, but I like to think that it at least makes me even."

The shape of the story is worth noticing. The scheme itself is clever and a little absurd, and a listener would enjoy it for that alone. But the part that stays is small: one man, one sentence, a doorway. The tire, the gas gauge and the briefcase are only the road to that doorway, and the dollars he has dropped into the boxes since keep the episode going long after the drive home.

[[lessons]]
$$**The real subject of a story often hides in one short moment inside the plot.** Dicks's tire story turned into a story about loneliness at a single front door; the tire, the gas and the twenty-three dollars were simply the way to get there. When you sit down to prepare to tell something from your own life, a hard week, a dispute, a project that went wrong, look past the plain order of events for the moment that made you see something differently. If you reach that moment in the telling, your listener gets a change, not just an incident.$$

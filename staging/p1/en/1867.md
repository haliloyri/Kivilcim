[[open]]
On one newspaper page, job advertisements sit beside railway timetables. Above them runs a call with a single target: at least 110,000 people should come to Chicago. The page does more than report the news. It hands readers the pieces they need to plan a journey.

[[story]]
Isabel Wilkerson's *The Warmth of Other Suns* tells of the millions of Black Americans who left the South in the Great Migration. This story concerns one of the tools that fed that movement, a campaign run by the Chicago Defender in 1917. The Defender was a newspaper published in Chicago for Black readers.

On 15 May 1917 the paper declared a day it called the "Great Northern Drive". Its goal was plain: at least 110,000 Black Americans moving to Chicago. The call came in the years of the First World War, when factories were short of workers. Wages in the North could be more than twice what a job paid in the South.

The paper did more than say "come". Its pages also carried job listings and train schedules. A reader could find the reason to leave, the place to apply and the train to board on the same sheet. For someone thinking of going, the hardest step is often not the decision itself but turning it into a date and a ticket.

The power of the method lies in a very plain detail. A general appeal creates excitement, but a job advertisement and a timetable tie that excitement to the next concrete step. A reader who wonders what to do tomorrow, where to go and how to get there finds the answers printed in the same place. The promise of the North stops being a slogan and becomes a sequence one can follow: a listing, a timetable, a journey.

No single newspaper can account for everyone who went north. The shortage of workers in wartime, low wages in the South and discrimination all played a part. But the campaign shows what happens when information is put on a page in a form people can act on. For those readers the paper worked at once as an employment office, a travel guide and a call to move, all in one place.

A decision only moves when it arrives with a task small enough to do. The Defender tied its call to a date, a target, a job and a train, and that small set of details is the whole technique. Nothing in it needed a speech or a movement; it needed a printed page.

[[lessons]]
$$**When you ask someone to take a step, put the way to take it next to your request.** Most people do not oppose a goal, but they stay where they are because they cannot find the first concrete move, and that is often mistaken for reluctance. If you want a teammate to switch to a new process, saying "let's switch" is not enough. Add the day, the file and the person to start with, and your request becomes a plan that can be carried out, so the other person does more than agree and actually begins.$$

[[open]]
A purchasing agent places an order for a large quantity of metal. The buyer on the paperwork is a company set up for the purpose, and the country that finally ships the metal is the one whose skies the finished aircraft is meant to cross.

[[story]]
Ben Rich's account in *Skunk Works*, written with Leo Janos, takes up a problem Kelly Johnson's team met in building the A-12, the CIA's very fast, very high reconnaissance aircraft that later led to the SR-71. At speeds above Mach 3 the skin of an airplane gets far too hot for aluminum. The CIA's own history of the aircraft says Johnson chose a titanium alloy, which is nearly as strong as stainless steel, about half the weight, and able to take the heat.

The choice created a supply problem. Rich describes the main American producer, Titanium Metals Corporation, as having only limited reserves. So the CIA ran a worldwide search for the raw metal and bought it through third parties and dummy companies.

One of the world's leading exporters of titanium was the Soviet Union. According to Rich, the metal was bought from it without the Soviets knowing where it was going, and they never had an inkling that they were helping to build the aircraft meant to spy on their own country. The CIA's historian David Robarge also records that some of the titanium came covertly from the Soviet Union.

Other difficulties arrived with the metal. Robarge writes that Lockheed's alloy supplier struggled with quality at first, and that a large share of early material was rejected. The scarce metal, in other words, had to be both found and made good enough.

None of this appears in a flashy design review. It sits in the dull part of the program, the order forms and the quality checks, and yet the entire airplane depended on it. A brilliant airframe that could not be supplied with its skin would have been a drawing.

The decision to build with titanium was bold, and the covert buying was the price. Johnson's choice of material set the terms of the whole program, and a scarce input meant the people who wrote the purchase orders were as important to the result as the people who drew the wings.

[[lessons]]
$$**Find out where your scarcest input comes from before you commit to the design that needs it.** A small furniture maker who falls for a rare wood, or a team that builds its product around one supplier's component, takes on the supplier's risks along with the material. Asking early who else sells it, what it costs when quality slips, and what you would use instead turns a hidden dependency into a decision you can make on purpose. It also tells you, before any money is spent, whether the design you love is one you can actually build.$$

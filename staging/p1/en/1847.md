In Arizona, voters fill in the same ballot and say yes or no to the same tax proposal. Some line up in a church hall, others walk down a school corridor to the booth. The building that hosts the booth appears nowhere on the ballot.

[[story]]
One study that fits Robert Cialdini's pre-suasion idea is a piece of research by Jonah Berger, Marc Meredith and S. Christian Wheeler, published in *PNAS* in June 2008. Cialdini has discussed it in a PBS interview. The researchers asked whether the place where people vote changes how they vote.

The data came from Arizona's general election of 2000. Proposition 301 was on the ballot: it proposed raising the state sales tax from 5.0 to 5.6 percent and putting the money into education. The researchers coded what kind of building each polling place was. Churches accounted for about 40 percent of the votes cast and schools for 26 percent. The matched sample they compared contained 1,617 precincts.

The raw numbers were these: 56.02 percent of people who voted in schools supported the education tax, against 53.99 percent elsewhere. That gap is small, about two points. When the researchers accounted for demographics, votes on other measures and distance from a school, it shrank to somewhere between 0.4 and 0.8 of a point. Schools had no consistent effect on other ballot questions, and turnout was nearly the same at schools and at the places around them.

The team then checked whether the building itself could be the cause. In an online experiment with 327 people, participants were randomly assigned to rate either pictures of schools or pictures of ordinary buildings and churches. Afterwards, in what looked like an unrelated study, they voted on the same education tax. Among those who had seen schools, 63.6 percent supported it. In the control group, 56.3 percent did.

One detail came last. In the debriefing, none of the participants believed that the school pictures had raised their support. By their own account, the influence worked somewhere they were not looking.

There was no persuasive speech in any of this. The place where people voted, and what it brings to mind, most likely moved thoughts about education and schools a little closer to the front. That is what interests Cialdini: people often ask which words influenced them when the influence may have been where they were standing.

[[lessons]]
$$**Take the associations of the place into account when you make an important decision.** Deciding on a big purchase inside the shop, among shelves and bright displays, may use a different set of scales than deciding at the kitchen table next to the household bills. The room and the table where you decide can shift which yardsticks come to mind first. The effect may be small, but you can choose where you decide, and an important decision made in a quiet place unrelated to its subject makes it easier to see which yardstick has moved to the front.$$

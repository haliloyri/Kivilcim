A pipeline company asks the government's accountants for permission to book a profit on the day a contract is signed, years before the last cubic foot of gas is delivered. The staff reads the request and starts asking questions. The company answers them one by one, for months.

[[story]]
Bethany McLean and Peter Elkind chronicle Enron's rise and fall in *The Smartest Guys in the Room*, and the company's accounting choices sit at the centre of that story. This one begins with a letter dated 11 June 1991, sent to the SEC's Office of Chief Accountant. Enron had just formed a natural gas trading subsidiary, Enron Gas Services, and wanted permission to record its contracts a new way.

The method was called mark-to-market. Under ordinary accounting, revenue from a long contract is spread across its life. Under mark-to-market, the company books the present value of all the contract's future profit on the day it is signed, then revalues it every quarter. A rise or fall in value flows straight into revenue. Profit shows up on signing day.

At the time, it was unusual for a pipeline company to do this. Enron argued that its new unit was really a commodity trading business, and that such businesses commonly marked their positions to market. It attached a letter from Arthur Andersen calling the method preferable, and one from Ernst & Young saying it fit accepted accounting principles. Over the following months the two sides exchanged at least eight letters, and Enron's representatives, Jeffrey Skilling among them, met SEC staff twice.

The staff asked how comparable businesses kept their books and how the figures would be calculated. At one point it suggested Enron publish the old-style numbers alongside the new ones until the measurements had proved reliable. Enron declined. The earnings, it said, would rest on "known spreads and balanced positions" and would not depend significantly on subjective elements.

On 30 January 1992 the Chief Accountant, Walter Schuetze, sent a no-objection letter covering the first quarter of 1992 onward. Twelve days later Enron wrote back that, on further review, the right starting point was the beginning of 1991, a year earlier than the staff had approved, and that the effect on 1991 earnings was not material. According to a Senate committee staff report, the SEC apparently did not answer, and Enron reported its 1991 gas trading results the new way.

##The same report adds that long contracts have no market price to read off, so Enron had to estimate them with models built on assumptions.## Experts told the committee that mark-to-market is often a good method when applied correctly, because it shows what assets are worth now. The staff's own conclusion was that Enron at a minimum overestimated the values it marked, and very possibly manipulated them.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask who can check a figure before you count it as earned.** A number that rests only on its author's own assumptions is a forecast, and it deserves a different label from a number that came in as cash. A cafe owner who enters a signed catering contract as this month's profit will start spending money that has not arrived, and nobody else can tell her the estimate was generous. Naming an outside person who can test the assumptions, before the figure goes into a report or a plan, lets you keep the optimistic estimate without betting the budget on it.$$

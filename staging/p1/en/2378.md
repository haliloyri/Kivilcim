The chief executive of IBM sat across from a departing manager and asked him a question that had nothing to do with the exit paperwork. He wanted to know why software projects were so much harder to manage than hardware projects.

[[story]]
According to published accounts, that was how Thomas J. Watson Jr. of IBM handled Fred Brooks's exit interview. Brooks had managed the development of OS/360, the operating system for IBM's System/360 line of computers, which was announced in April 1964. The first release of OS/360 is dated 31 March 1966, almost two years later.

Brooks answered Watson's question in a book, *The Mythical Man-Month*, first published in 1975. It grew out of what he had seen on OS/360, where he had added programmers to a project that was already behind. He later concluded that the extra people had delayed it further. He compressed this into one sentence that other engineers soon named Brooks's law: adding manpower to a late software project makes it later.

The arithmetic behind the sentence is simple enough to do on paper. Every pair of people on a project is a possible conversation, and a team of n people has n(n − 1)/2 of those pairs. Ten people have 45. Fifty people have 1,225. Each new hire adds a person who has to be briefed by someone, and each one adds a whole row of new channels through which decisions, misunderstandings and half-finished interfaces have to travel.

This explains why the remedy for lateness looks so sensible and works so badly. A late project feels like a shortage of hands, so the instinct is to supply hands. But the work on a software system is tangled together: one part cannot be finished until another part's interface is settled. People cannot simply split it among themselves the way a crew can split a field to harvest. The existing team stops writing code to answer questions, and the newcomers produce little until they understand a system that is still changing under them.

Brooks's own title points at the mistake. A man-month treats people and months as interchangeable units, as if three people for four months and twelve people for one month were the same purchase. On a job where the pieces depend on each other, they are not the same purchase at all.

Watson's question had a practical answer, then, and it arrived as a law that could be written on a single line. The number of people is not the only thing that sets how fast a late project moves. How many conversations it takes to coordinate them matters at least as much.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before adding people to a late job, count the conversations they will create.** A small web shop that is behind on a client's site may want to call in three freelancers, yet each of them needs the files, the decisions made so far and an hour of someone's attention. That hour comes out of the very people who were already stretched. If you list who must brief whom, you can see whether help will arrive as hands or as interruptions, and you can choose a smaller scope or a later date instead of paying for help that slows everyone down.$$

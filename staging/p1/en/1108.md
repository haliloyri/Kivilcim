[[open]]
A four-year-old sits at a table. In front of them is one marshmallow. An adult has just stepped out of the room, and the door is clicking shut. Nobody is watching. The next few minutes will seem like nothing. Years later, though, a researcher will look back at those minutes and see something about who that child became.

[[story]]
In the 1960s, the psychologist Walter Mischel ran a simple experiment at a preschool on the Stanford University campus. His subjects were four-year-olds, and his equipment was about as low-tech as research gets: marshmallows.

Each child got the same deal. The researcher had to leave on a quick errand, and the child could choose. In substance, the offer went like this:

> You can eat this one marshmallow right now. Or, if you wait until I come back, you can have two.

Then the researcher left. Picture it from the child's side. There's a soft, sweet thing within arm's reach, a promise of twice as much later, and no idea how long "later" will be.

Some children didn't make it far. Within seconds of the researcher leaving, they grabbed the marshmallow and ate it. The offer of two never really had a chance.

Others held out, and they didn't do it by sitting still and staring down the treat. They covered their eyes so they couldn't see it. They talked to themselves. They sang. They found ways to be somewhere else in their heads while their bodies stayed in the chair. For a four-year-old, the wait of 15 to 20 minutes must have felt endless. When the researcher finally came back, these children got their two marshmallows.

If the story ended there, it would be a cute bit of trivia about preschoolers and sugar. It didn't end there.

About 12 to 14 years later, the same children were tracked down again. By then they were adolescents nearing the end of high school, and the differences were striking.

##The kids who had waited were now described as more socially competent, more self-assertive, and better able to cope with frustration and stress.##

The kids who had grabbed the marshmallow were more likely to be described as stubborn and indecisive, easily upset by frustration, and prone to envy or jealousy. There was an academic gap too. The children who had waited scored substantially higher on the SAT than the ones who hadn't.

Daniel Goleman tells this story in *Emotional Intelligence* because, for him, it shows something IQ tests leave out. He calls impulse control a "master aptitude," a basic emotional skill that other abilities rest on. His argument is that skills like this one predict how life goes in ways raw intelligence doesn't. He also argues that they aren't fixed at birth. They can be taught and strengthened.

[[lessons]]
$$**The waiters weren't just tougher.** Watch what the successful children actually did. They covered their eyes, sang, and talked to themselves. They weren't winning a staring contest with the marshmallow. They were steering their attention away from it. The skill on display was managing where their minds went, and that is far more learnable than some mysterious reserve of willpower.$$

$$**Some things matter that an IQ score misses.** The adolescent follow-up found differences in how these kids handled frustration and stress, how they got along with others, and how they did on the SAT. All of that traced back to a few minutes alone with a treat. That is the heart of Goleman's case: the ability to regulate your impulses shows up in places a pure measure of intelligence doesn't reach. (Larger replications that came after the book, such as Watts et al. in 2018, found a weaker long-term link. Hold the prediction loosely.)$$

$$**A master aptitude can still be trained.** Goleman calls impulse control a "master aptitude" because so much else depends on it: staying with a goal, taking criticism, not being ruled by envy. He doesn't treat it as a verdict handed down at age four. If the children who waited did it with tricks, then the tricks can be taught, and that's exactly what he argues.$$

[[reflect]]
- What is the marshmallow in your day, the thing you usually grab within seconds of the door closing?
- When you do manage to wait, what's your version of covering your eyes or singing?
- Where are you trying to beat a temptation by staring it down, when moving your attention would work better?
- Which frustrations in your life right now would get easier if you bought yourself a few minutes before reacting?

&&Think of a recent moment you gave in to an impulse. Looking back, where did that choice actually take you?&&

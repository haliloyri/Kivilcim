The cattle come up the ramp to the edge of a deep pool and stop. Some panic, some refuse to jump, some flip over backwards. The hands could call it stubbornness. The woman designing the new vat decides to look at the pool from where the cattle are standing.

[[story]]
In 1978 Temple Grandin was designing a dip vat for a feed yard in Arizona. A dip vat is a deep pool of pesticide through which cattle are moved in single file, to clear them of ticks and lice. She describes the situation in her own words in a piece for TIME: the designs of that time were poor. Animals were forced down a steep, slick slope into the vat, and they panicked. They refused to jump, sometimes flipped backwards, and sometimes drowned.

Grandin's first move on arrival, as she puts it, was to put herself inside a cow's head and see with its eyes. A cow's eyes sit on the sides of its head and give it a very wide field of view. From that angle the layout began to make sense as something frightening. A cow that would not enter the vat was telling her about the vat, not about itself.

She started with the floor. The steel ramp became concrete, because, as she wrote, "If I had a calf's body and hooves, I would be very scared to step on a slippery metal ramp." The concrete was grooved for footing, and the ramp sloped downward.

The real trick was at the bottom. The ramp looked as if it entered the water gradually, but below the surface it dropped away abruptly. The dip chemicals coloured the water, so the animals could not see the drop. Cattle stepped off the ramp and quietly fell in, because by then their centre of gravity had passed the point of no return.

What stands out is that none of this tried to persuade the animal. Elsewhere Grandin writes that cattle can balk at a coat slung over a fence or a flash of light on a car bumper, and an animal like that will not be talked into a slick ramp and dark water. So she changed what the animal could see and stand on: grooved concrete in place of slippery steel, a slope that seemed gentle in place of a visible cliff. In the same piece she argues that well-designed equipment gets used voluntarily, and she closes by noting that half the cattle in the United States are now handled in equipment she designed.

[[lessons]]
$$**Look through the eyes of whoever is resisting, because resistance often points at a flaw in the design.** A customer who abandons a form, an employee who ignores a new system or a child who will not sit down to dinner looks stubborn at first. See the same scene from their position, with what they can see and where they put their feet, and you may find a slippery step, a confusing screen or a room that is far too loud. Fixing the ground is usually cheaper than fighting the stubbornness, and it moves more people with less effort. Write down what they see, where they step and what meets them first, then design the answer.$$

[[open]]
A sheet of paper describes your personality in thirteen sentences. One says that you have a great need for other people to like and admire you. Another says that security is one of your major goals in life. You read down the page and find yourself nodding at every line.

[[story]]
In the late 1940s, a psychologist named Bertram Forer gave his introductory psychology class a personality questionnaire called the Diagnostic Interest Blank. There were 39 students. He told them that each of them would receive a brief personality sketch based on their answers.

A week later the sketches came back, and every student was asked to read theirs and rate how well it described them. The scale ran from 0, very poor, to 5, excellent. The students had no reason to doubt that the page in their hands was written for them alone.

It was not. Every student had been given exactly the same text. Forer had put it together from a newsstand astrology book, choosing thirteen statements that sound specific but fit almost anyone. The students learned this only after they had handed in their ratings.

The average rating was close to 4.3 out of 5. Published accounts of the study differ slightly on the exact figure, giving 4.26 in some places and 4.30 in others, but all agree on the picture: a group of students rated a page of astrology as a very good description of themselves. Forer published the demonstration in 1949 in the *Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology* under the title "The Fallacy of Personal Validation".

His point was about validation. A reader's feeling that a description fits is not a test of whether it is accurate, because the feeling is produced as easily by a generic page as by a precise one. The sentences that worked best were the ones broad enough to be true of most people, and soft enough that each reader could fill in their own example. "You have a great need for other people to like and admire you" has room for every reader's own story.

Had the students swapped sheets with the person at the next desk, the match would have been obvious within a minute. The experiment worked because each of them judged their own sheet without that comparison. The students were not foolish, and the sketch needed nothing clever. It took only a set of statements that everyone can agree with, and the pleasant sense of being understood did the rest.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you accept a description as personal, ask whether it would fit nearly everyone and what it would say about someone very different from you.** A colleague reads a short summary of a personality test and finds it uncannily accurate, then repeats it in a hiring discussion as if it were evidence. The same words would probably have fit the colleague at the next desk just as well. Look for statements that could be wrong, with specific, checkable details, and treat the warm feeling of recognition as a reason to look harder, not as a conclusion. You can even hand the same text to someone else and see what they say.$$

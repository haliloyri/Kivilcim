[[story]]
Walter Isaacson's *The Innovators* opens with Ada Lovelace, and this story is about the one piece of work that carries her name. In 1842 an Italian engineer, Luigi Menabrea, published a French article on Charles Babbage's Analytical Engine, a machine Babbage had described in a lecture in Turin and never finished building. Lovelace translated the article into English over roughly nine months.

Then she kept writing. She added seven notes, lettered A to G, and together they ran to about three times the length of the article itself. She signed them with her initials, A. A. L., and they appeared in 1843 in Richard Taylor's *Scientific Memoirs*. In Note A she wrote that the Analytical Engine "weaves algebraical patterns just as the Jacquard-loom weaves flowers and leaves", tying the machine to the punched cards that steered weaving looms.

Note G is the famous one. In it she follows, step by step, how the engine could compute the Numbers of Bernoulli, and she describes the diagram of the computation as very large. For the first number she counts nine operation cards. The same twenty-five cards, she writes, are enough for the successive computation of all the numbers. Because Babbage had sketched earlier programs that were never published, historians call Note G the first published computer program rather than the first program.

The same note holds a sentence that people still quote. The Analytical Engine, she wrote, has no pretensions whatever to originate anything. It can do whatever we know how to order it to perform. She wrote this in the same note that lays out the computation of the Bernoulli numbers, the note in which she shows at most length what the machine could be ordered to do.

Read the pages in order and the pattern is plain. She never pretends the engine thinks. She counts the cards, shows the loop, and places the whole burden on whoever writes the orders. The notes are long because she is doing the work a reader would otherwise have to do: spelling out each step, so that nothing depends on the machine knowing what was meant. A table with a column for every operation leaves no room for a vague instruction.

[[lessons]]
$$**Write down what your tool will do and what it will not.** Lovelace praised the engine's reach and, on the same page, stated its limit: it only performs what it is ordered to perform. Anyone who works with a tool can borrow that habit. A shop owner who buys scheduling software can do the same thing by listing which decisions the software makes and which still belong to a person on the floor. That list lets you hand work to the tool without handing it the judgment, and it shows you where to look first when something goes wrong, and which decisions remain yours.$$

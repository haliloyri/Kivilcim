A secretary warns a young mathematician that the professor behind the door will give him a few minutes at most, and that he does not spend time on subjects or people that bore him. The visitor is carrying a typed paper about blackjack. Hours later the sky outside is going dark and the two of them are still talking.

[[story]]
Edward O. Thorp, author of *A Man for All Markets*, told this meeting himself in a 1998 paper about the first wearable computer. In the autumn of 1960 he was a young mathematician at MIT with a result he was afraid to lose: a mathematical method for beating blackjack. He had worked out the five-count system, most of the ten-count system and what he called the ultimate strategy. He feared the idea would be copied, so he wanted it in print fast.

The place he chose was the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. A paper there needs a member of the Academy to communicate it, and the only mathematics member at MIT was Claude Shannon. Thorp booked an appointment for a chilly November afternoon. The secretary warned him that Shannon would be in for only a few minutes and not to expect more.

Thorp found a thinnish, alert man of middle height, with a genial crinkle around his eyes. He told the blackjack story briefly and handed over the paper. Shannon cross-examined him in detail, both to understand the method and to hunt for flaws. When the few minutes were up, he said Thorp seemed to have made the big theoretical breakthrough and that what remained would mostly be details and elaboration.

They changed the title together. "A Winning Strategy for Blackjack" became "A Favorable Strategy for Twenty-One", which sounded more sedate and respectable. Thorp reluctantly accepted some cuts. He would send the retyped revision right away, and Shannon would forward it to the Academy.

Then Shannon asked, "Are you working on anything else in the gambling area?" Thorp decided to spill his other big secret and told him about roulette. Ideas flew between them. Several hours later, as the wintery sky turned dusky, they broke off with plans to meet again about the roulette wheel.

The paper appeared in 1961, in volume 47 of the Proceedings. The same conversation started months of work in Shannon's basement, from November 1960 to June 1961.

Two things stand out in Thorp's account. He did not arrive with an idea; he arrived with a finished result that could be checked, so Shannon had something to cross-examine. And when the few minutes turned into hours, Thorp had a second subject ready in his pocket.

[[lessons]]
$$**Take your finished work to the sharpest critic you can reach.** A baker with a new recipe learns more from one retired pastry chef who picks it apart than from a kitchen full of people who just say it tastes good. Work that survives a detailed cross-examination also shows you that the interest in it is real, and the critic often spots what you have stopped being able to see yourself. Work you call finished but nobody has tried to break is, in practice, still a draft. And if you walk in with a second subject ready, a short appointment has room to turn into a long collaboration.$$

Forty-nine feet. That was the number, and for weeks it did not change. It came from the national forecasters, it was repeated again and again, and a city built its defences with it in mind. The people there had been handed one number by the experts, and they treated it as the answer.

[[story]]
In the spring of 1997 the Red River rose toward Grand Forks, North Dakota. The National Weather Service had put out its outlook in February: a crest of 49 feet. That was only 0.2 foot above the record flood of 1979, a flood the city had come through. In March the same value was repeated. The river had also run high in 1996 without causing damage.

Nate Silver uses the Grand Forks flood in *The Signal and the Noise* to show what happens when a forecast is heard as a single figure. The Weather Service's own review of the flood, written afterwards, says interviews in the area suggested the outlook created a sense of complacency. Repeating one value for weeks also made people lock on to it.

There was a second problem in how it was read. The outlook actually contained two peak values, one assuming no more precipitation and one assuming normal precipitation. Many people took them as a minimum and a maximum. In fact the higher one was about a median, with roughly a 50 percent chance of being equalled or exceeded. The river could go higher.

It did. On 14 April the first operational forecast for East Grand Forks was 50 feet. By 18 April the final crest forecast was 54 feet. The river peaked at 54.35 feet. The earthen levees could protect to about 52 feet, counting three feet of freeboard.

Nine in ten of Grand Forks' 52,500 residents evacuated. Of the city's homes, 9,001 were damaged, 83 percent of them, and downtown buildings burned.

The river was 5.35 feet above the number people had heard for weeks, and about 2.35 feet over what the levees could hold. A crest only 0.2 foot above a record the city had already survived sounded manageable. The uncertainty was in the outlook, but it did not survive the trip from the forecast to the sandbags. A short, round number is easy to repeat, and each repetition makes it sound more certain.

After the flood the Weather Service made major changes to how it forecasts floods. The lesson was about communication as much as hydrology: a forecast that people hear as a promise can leave them less prepared than no number at all.

[[lessons]]
$$**When you share a number, put its margin of error in the same sentence.** A project deadline, a sales forecast or a repair estimate said alone is heard as a promise, and people build their plans right up to that figure. Give the range as well, and listeners leave room on both sides, so a miss does not become a disaster. One number feels reassuring, but a range gets people to prepare, and on a bad day preparation is what counts. You can do it in a single sentence: this is the most likely value, and it could be off by this much.$$

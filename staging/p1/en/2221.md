Thirty-six young men volunteered to eat about half of what they normally would, for half a year, so that doctors could learn what hunger does to a person. Their weight fell, as everyone expected. The rest of what fell away was harder to predict, and it was written down week by week.

[[story]]
The study began at the University of Minnesota in the last stretch of the Second World War, under the physiologist Ancel Keys. Europe was full of people who had gone hungry for years, and Keys wanted to know how to feed them back to health. That meant first learning what prolonged undereating does to a body and a mind, and for that he needed people willing to go through it.

His volunteers were 36 conscientious objectors, men who had refused to fight and who took part in the experiment as a form of service. They first ate normally while the researchers observed them. Then came the semi-starvation phase of about 24 weeks. According to published accounts, the men ate roughly 1,570 kilocalories a day, around half their earlier intake, followed by a period of supervised refeeding.

The body responded as a system, not as a calculator. Most of the men lost more than a quarter of their body weight. The researchers recorded anemia, fatigue, extreme weakness, swelling in the legs and neurological changes. Published accounts also describe apathy and irritability, and men whose thoughts kept circling back to food. The study was built to follow all of this closely, so the changes were recorded as they happened, not reconstructed afterwards from memory.

For a long time that last detail was easy to underrate. The men were not only thinner, they were different people in how they spent a day. Interest in much else seemed to shrink while attention to eating grew. The experiment made clear that cutting what a person eats changes far more than the figure on the scale.

Decades later, in 2003 and 2004, 18 of the original men were interviewed. All of them said they would take part again, and many had gone on to work in fields connected with nonviolence. That does not make the ordeal gentler than it was. It shows how the men themselves judged it, once they could look back at the purpose it served.

The experiment also left a question for any later story about weight and eating. When intake is cut hard, you can predict that weight will fall. You cannot assume that nothing else will move with it.

[[lessons]]
$$**When you cut one input hard, the whole system reacts, so look for what else moves along with the target.** The men in this experiment lost weight exactly as planned, and with it came weakness, mood changes and a mind occupied by food, none of which were on the target list. A plan that watches only its target number will miss the other effects until they are large and hard to reverse. Before you trim a budget, a schedule or a team, name two things besides the target that you expect to change, and check on them early rather than at the final review.$$

[[open]]
A young manager sits at the side of a meeting room and watches a colleague present. In front of one senior leader, the colleague seems to shrink. In front of another, the same colleague is fully capable. She files the contrast away and keeps watching.

[[story]]
Liz Wiseman arrived at Oracle in 1989, straight out of business school, and stayed for seventeen years. A year in, at twenty-four, she was asked to manage the training department and to turn chief executive Larry Ellison's idea of an Oracle University into something real. She later ran the university and then the company's global human resources.

Oracle recruited from top schools and prized intelligence and drive. According to Wiseman, some of those brilliant people could still drain the intelligence out of a room. That observation sits at the start of her research, and it is the reason her book *Multipliers*, written with Greg McKeown and published in 2010, exists.

One example she describes is a colleague named Brian. She watched him present to two different leaders. Before one of them he seemed diminished, and before the other he was fully capable. Same colleague, two rooms, two very different people on display.

Her own career gave her the opposite contrast. Her first management job came from Bob Shaver, who, as she tells it, did not rescue her when she struggled. Once he introduced her to an Oracle client, a gray-haired man who flinched at the thought of someone so young running the operation. Shaver joked that she was not particularly well qualified for her job. Wiseman answered that nobody wants a job they are qualified for, because then there would be nothing left to learn.

Put the two scenes side by side and a puzzle appears. Brian's ability did not change between the two meetings; what changed was the person across the table. Wiseman's ability to do her job was in doubt in the client's eyes, yet her boss treated the doubt as a joke and left the work in her hands.

That is the question her later study of more than 150 leaders tried to answer in numbers. Not "who is smart?" but "what happens to everyone else's intelligence when this person walks in?" The usual talent review looks at one individual at a time, and it would have rated Brian on a single performance, perhaps the worse one.

Wiseman's telling suggests another habit of attention. Watch the same person in front of two different leaders. If he is sharp in one room and quiet in the other, the difference tells you about the leaders as much as about him.

[[lessons]]
$$**Watch a colleague in front of several different leaders before you judge their ability.** One meeting, one review or one bad presentation records how a person performed with a particular audience, and the audience is part of the result. If someone is sharp with one manager and silent with another, the second room deserves as much scrutiny as the person. A team review that scores only the individual misses that. The same holds for you: ask yourself whom people become when you walk in, and whether your questions open a room or close it. Seeing that gap early lets you act on the cause, whether it is the room, the leader or the person, instead of filing a quiet colleague under "weak".$$

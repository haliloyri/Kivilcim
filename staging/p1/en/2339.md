[[open]]
The boxes are packed, the labels printed, and the test kits are waiting to be sent out to laboratories. Then the final quality-control result lands on a screen, and the kits are already sealed. Someone has to decide whether to open the boxes again or ship them as they are, and whether any written standard exists to say which.

[[story]]
In February 2020 the CDC, America's national public health agency, made its own test for the new coronavirus and sent it out to roughly a hundred public health laboratories across the country. Michael Lewis's *The Premonition* (2021) deals with the failures of that pandemic response, the CDC's among them. According to NPR's reporting on the agency's internal review, the final quality-control test on 6 February suggested the kit could fail 33 percent of the time. By then the kits were already boxed.

The kits were not recalled, and no performance alert went out. The review found that the first round of quality control had used an "incorrect" testing procedure. The agency also had no set accuracy threshold for releasing a test, so the call that day rested on the judgement of the head of the lab.

Trouble reached the CDC from the field quickly. By 8 February, New York City's public health laboratory was reporting inconclusive results from two of the reagents. Reporting on a later audit by the inspector general of the health department says contamination during the kit's manufacture made the test fail in some public laboratories.

The internal review did not boil the problem down to one mistake. It listed "process failures", a lack of "appropriate recognized laboratory quality standards", and the absence or failure of document control. The head of the lab is no longer in that job, and the CDC removed the leadership of the viral diseases division that oversaw it. The review recommended setting release criteria in advance and having outside groups examine CDC tests.

A CDC spokesman said the agency had "acknowledged and corrected mistakes along the way". When the report was written, the inspector general's investigation was still under way.

What decided the outcome was not one lab or one number. Three questions had gone unanswered: how good the kit had to be, what to do if a measurement came back wrong, and who would decide, on the strength of which document. Without a threshold, even a 33 percent warning stays an opinion, and one person says "good enough" while another says "not yet proven". With a written rule the question changes from "do we ship?" to "was the rule met?". A pass line that was not written before the test lets everyone pull it their own way once the result is in.

[[lessons]]
$$**Write the pass line before the result arrives, then keep to it.** A baker who checks a batch before it goes on sale, and decided in the morning what result would send it back, does not bargain over the call in the evening under pressure. A check without a criterion is a guess dressed as a check, and a guess changes with the mood of the room. When you set the line in advance, a calm day makes the hard decision instead of a rushed one, you only have to read the paper when the moment comes, and you leave a clear reason for why you stopped or why you shipped.$$

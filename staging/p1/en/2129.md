Upstairs a church service is under way. Downstairs, in a basement room, twenty students sit and listen to it through loudspeakers. Half of them have taken a dose that will soon make the sermon feel enormous. The other half have taken one that mostly makes their skin tingle and flush.

[[story]]
On Good Friday, 20 April 1962, a doctoral student named Walter Pahnke ran an experiment at Marsh Chapel, at Boston University. He was a graduate student at Harvard Divinity School, working under the supervision of the psychologists Timothy Leary and Richard Alpert, whose psilocybin research at Harvard is part of the history Michael Pollan covers in *How to Change Your Mind*. Pahnke wanted to know whether psilocybin could reliably produce a religious experience in people already inclined toward religion.

Twenty graduate students of divinity from the Boston area took part. They were divided at random, and the design was double-blind: half received psilocybin, the other half a large dose of niacin, a vitamin that causes warmth and flushing. That was a clever choice, because a dummy that does nothing is easy to spot, while niacin makes the body feel something. The volunteers waited in a basement room and followed the service over loudspeakers. Among them was the religious scholar Huston Smith, who according to published accounts later called the experience "the most powerful cosmic homecoming I have ever experienced."

Pahnke reported that nearly all of the psilocybin group had profound religious experiences, and that the control group did not match them. Then, decades afterwards, someone checked. In 1991 Rick Doblin published a long-term follow-up of the participants together with a critique of the method. He found that nearly all of the psilocybin group still described the day as a genuine mystical experience and one of the high points of their spiritual lives. He also found flaws: the double-blind procedure had not been carried out properly, some questions on the questionnaire were imprecise, and Pahnke had not mentioned that several subjects went through acute anxiety. One man had fled the chapel convinced he was chosen to announce the return of the Messiah, and he had to be restrained and given the drug Thorazine.

That is the part worth dwelling on. The headline result was real enough to survive a twenty-five-year follow-up, but the first account of it was tidier than the day itself. Nothing in Doblin's critique erased the finding. It only showed which parts of the picture had been left out, and a reader who sees the whole picture can judge the finding properly.

[[lessons]]
$$**Print the failures next to the successes, because a result is only as trustworthy as the worst thing you were willing to report.** Pahnke's main finding held up for decades, yet the missing anxiety cases cost his report some of its credibility. In your own work, a project summary that lists only what went well invites a sceptical reader to go looking for what was left out. Put the awkward detail in the first version: the missed deadline, the client who complained, the test that did not behave. The people who rely on your numbers will then trust the good ones.$$

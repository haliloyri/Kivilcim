A narrow stone room lets in light only through thin slits in the wall. The roof is ordinary, the floor plain, the air dim and quiet. Long ago a man the Romans called the terror of Carthage washed his body here after working in the fields.

[[story]]
Seneca is resting at a country house, he tells Lucilius in Letter 86, that once belonged to Scipio Africanus. He has paid his respects to the great man's spirit and to an altar he is inclined to think is the tomb. Scipio's soul has returned to the skies, Seneca is convinced, not because he commanded great armies but because of his moderation and sense of duty. He admires that trait more in Scipio's withdrawal to Liternum, a voluntary exile, than in his defence of Rome. Modern sources give about 183 BC as the year Scipio died there, and where he was buried remains uncertain.

Then Seneca walks through the house. It is built of hewn stone. A wall encloses a wood, towers are buttressed on both sides for defence, and a well hidden among buildings and shrubbery is big enough to keep a whole army supplied. The bath is small and buried in darkness, in the old style, because the ancestors did not think a hot bath could be taken except in the dark. In its wall are tiny chinks, you could not call them windows, that let in light without weakening the fortifications.

Seneca sets this beside his own age. People now feel poor unless their walls shine with large costly mirrors, their Alexandrian marble is edged with Numidian stone, their ceilings are buried in glass, their pools are lined with Thasian marble and the water pours from silver spigots. A bath is only fit for moths, they say, if it does not get the sun all day through the widest windows. Scipio's bath is nothing like that, and he went there, Seneca writes, to wash off sweat, not ointment.

Seneca adds, citing those who report the old ways, that the early Romans washed their arms and legs every day and bathed all over once a week. Then the letter turns sharply. The present owner, Aegialus, is a careful householder, and from him Seneca has learned that a tree can be transplanted however old it is. We old men, he says, must learn this, for every one of us is planting an olive-yard for a successor. He has seen such trees bear fruit after three or four years of nothing.

Aegialus's method is described in detail. He lops the branches of large trees to a foot, packs the trunk with manure, sets it in the hole, then heaps up the earth and treads it down hard. That keeps out cold and wind and lets the soft young roots take hold. Seneca ends the letter with a joke: he will say no more, lest, as Aegialus did with him, he end up training Lucilius to be his competitor.

[[lessons]]
$$**Plant the tree late, because the shade is often for someone else.** A person who has put off learning a trade for years can say it is too late and stop, or can behave like an old olive and expect to take root in new soil. What Seneca learned from Aegialus is that a late start is no prohibition and that a bare first stretch does not mean the effort was wasted. A small step taken today can turn into something that helps another person later, even if you never see the fruit yourself. Seeing your slow, unglamorous tasks as olive-yards makes it easier to keep going through the years that bring nothing back.$$

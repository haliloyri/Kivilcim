A sacred white horse wades into a river and is swept under. For the rest of that summer an entire army stands on the banks with spades. The river is the Gyndes, the army belongs to King Cyrus, and Babylon, the city he was marching on, has to wait for spring.

[[story]]
In *The Consolations of Philosophy*, Alain de Botton gives Seneca the chapter on frustration, and a reviewer notes that it includes Seneca's warning against raging at lifeless things. The best-known example is Cyrus and the river. Herodotus tells it in his *Histories*, and Seneca retells it in his essay *On Anger* as a case to avoid.

Cyrus was on his way to Babylon when he reached the Gyndes. In Herodotus, one of his sacred white horses went into the water in high spirits, tried to cross, and the current swept it away. Cyrus was very angry with the river. He threatened to make it so weak that in future even women could cross it without wetting a knee.

He stopped the march on Babylon. He split his army in two, marked out straight lines on both banks, and set the men digging: one hundred and eighty channels on each side, three hundred and sixty in all. The work was finished, but it took the whole summer season. Only when the next spring began did he resume his advance on the city.

The horse was gone, and the river was merely an obstacle that the king chose to turn into an opponent. Seneca does the accounting that Cyrus did not. The king lost time, which matters enormously in a war. He lost the courage of his soldiers, which was worn down by useless labour. And he lost the chance of falling on an enemy who was not yet prepared. Seneca puts it in one line: he was waging against the river the war he had declared against his foes.

Notice what the anger actually produced. It was no outburst that passed in a minute. It was a long, organised project, with lines marked out and a great multitude at work, and everything about it looked like effort. The king won his victory over a stream, and it cost him the summer.

Seneca adds that this frenzy, as he calls it, was not Cyrus's alone. His Roman example is an emperor who had a beautiful villa torn down because his mother had once been held prisoner in it, so that people who used to sail past it unnoticed now ask why it lies in ruins.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you act on anger, ask whether the target can help you reach your goal.** A manager whose launch slips because of a faulty tool may spend a whole week drafting complaints to the supplier while the actual deadline sits untouched. Anger tends to grab whatever is nearest and make it the enemy, and the effort feels productive because it is real effort. If you stop and name the thing you actually want, you can often see quite quickly that the next useful step points somewhere else, and you take it while there is still time to use it well.$$

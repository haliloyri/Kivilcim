Imagine buying a company worth about $23 billion. You'd expect lawyers, auditors, data rooms and months of checking every claim the seller makes. Now imagine doing it in a single two-hour meeting, sealing it with a handshake and never checking the books at all.

[[story]]
Stephen M. R. Covey opens one of the key arguments of *The Speed of Trust* with a deal that sounds almost reckless.

In 2003, Warren Buffett's company, Berkshire Hathaway, bought McLane Distribution from Wal-Mart. This was not a small side purchase. McLane was a distribution business of about $23 billion. A deal of that size normally goes through a long, expensive ritual. Teams of accountants go through every number. Lawyers draft pages of guarantees in case something turns out to be wrong. Each side assumes the other might be hiding something, and pays a lot of money to find out.

This deal skipped all of that.

##Buffett and Wal-Mart agreed the deal in one two-hour meeting and shook hands.##

Buffett's team did no due diligence. They didn't send in auditors to verify what Wal-Mart told them. Buffett later explained that he simply expected the information to be accurate, and it was. Less than a month after that meeting, the deal was done. Buffett reported the figure as 29 days.

Covey's point isn't that Buffett was brave or lucky. It's that trust has a price tag, and you can see it on the calendar.

He puts it as a simple economic rule. When trust goes up, speed goes up and cost goes down. When trust goes down, speed goes down and cost goes up. We usually treat trust as something soft, a nice quality in a person or a company. Covey treats it as a hard number. A low-trust deal isn't just unpleasant. It takes longer and costs more, because every claim has to be checked and every risk has to be protected against.

You don't need a multibillion-dollar deal to see this. Think of a supplier you've worked with for years. A problem gets solved in one phone call. The same problem with a supplier you don't trust takes three emails, a meeting and a contract amendment. The difference isn't good manners. It's time and money.

There's an important catch, though. That two-hour meeting did not create the trust. Wal-Mart and Buffett each came to the table with long, public track records. The handshake meant something because both sides had spent decades making sure it would.

[[lessons]]
$$**Trust shows up on the bill.** A deal that normally takes months of audits and legal work closed in under a month after a two-hour meeting. Covey's rule is that trust changes both speed and cost. When you trust someone, you stop paying for protection against them, and that saving is very real.$$

$$**Fast trust is built slowly.** Buffett could skip due diligence because Wal-Mart had a reputation worth relying on, and Wal-Mart could trust Buffett for the same reason. The handshake was the last two hours of a process that took decades. You can't copy the gesture without the history behind it.$$

$$**You can estimate the cost of low trust.** Take any slow process and ask how long it would take if you fully trusted everyone involved. The gap between that answer and reality is roughly what low trust is costing you. For McLane, that gap was months.$$

[[reflect]]
- Which process in your work takes far longer than it would if you fully trusted the people involved?
- Whose word is enough for you, and what have they done over time to earn that?
- Is there a relationship where people check your work more than it needs checking? What might they still be waiting to see?

&&What would someone need to see from you, over the years, for your handshake to be enough?&&

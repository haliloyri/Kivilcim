[[open]]
In a small Georgia city, a police chief tells his officers to keep their clubs out of sight and treat the demonstrators with courtesy. The marchers are arrested without a scratch, the cameras find nothing to show, and the campaign that brought them there quietly runs out of steam.

[[story]]
The campaign was in Albany, Georgia, and the failure stayed with Martin Luther King Jr.'s organization, the Southern Christian Leadership Conference. Police chief Laurie Pritchett had understood something that the protesters had counted on him not to understand: brutality on camera helps the people being beaten. So he arrested them calmly, and the story that reached the rest of the country was dull. The New York Herald Tribune judged it one of the heaviest defeats of King's career.

Wyatt Tee Walker, King's chief of staff, carried that lesson to the next city. Gladwell gives him a chapter of *David and Goliath*, and what he took from Albany was a question about the opponent. A movement that depends on a violent reaction needs an opponent who will provide one. Birmingham, Alabama, had Eugene "Bull" Connor, its commissioner of public safety, who had a long record of meeting Black protest with force.

The plan for Birmingham was called Project C, and the C stood for confrontation. It began in the first days of April with sit-ins and marches, and its design assumed that arrests would fill the jails and that Connor would react.

Then organizers turned to the young. In early May, hundreds of children and teenagers walked out of a church to march, and hundreds were arrested. Within days, Connor ordered firefighters to turn their hoses on marchers and police to bring dogs. Photographs of that reached newspapers across the country.

The campaign ended on 10 May with an agreement to begin desegregating parts of the city. Walker's plan had needed Connor to be exactly who he was, and he was. Every picture of a hose turned on a child did the persuading for the movement, and the people looking at those pictures were readers of newspapers in cities across the country.

What stands out is how the weaker side used the stronger side's own character. Walker did not try to overpower Birmingham's police. He built a design in which their force became the evidence. That also explains why the same tactic failed in Albany: the plan was only as good as the opponent's willingness to cooperate with it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you choose a tactic, study what your opponent is likely to do, because a plan that works in one place can be useless against a different person.** The same marches failed against a patient police chief in Albany and succeeded against an impulsive commissioner in Birmingham. Before a negotiation, a competitive bid or a hard conversation, ask what this particular person tends to do under pressure, and build the plan around that. A strategy copied from another victory carries hidden assumptions about the other side, and those assumptions are the first thing to check. Write the assumption down in one sentence, then look for evidence from this opponent's own past behaviour.$$

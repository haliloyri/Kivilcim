On a screen, past quiz clues sit in neat rows. Each carries a small mark: known, or not known. The player does not read the clues for pleasure. He reads the pattern of the marks, because it shows exactly where the next hour of study will do the most good.

[[story]]
Roger Craig, a programmer who built his own study database, won $77,000 in a single episode of the quiz show Jeopardy!, in 2010. At the time, that beat the earlier single-day record of $75,000, held by Ken Jennings, and it stood until 2019. Craig's own talks and interviews describe how he prepared, and the method matters more than the money.

He began by downloading the show's archive of past clues from the J-Archive and cleaning it into a relational database. Then he used text mining to group the clues by topic, which showed him how the questions were actually distributed. Instead of only reading widely, Craig studied the shape of the quiz itself.

The next step was to find out what he knew. He built a simple tool in which every clue could be marked as one he knew or one he did not. From those marks he could model his strengths and weaknesses by topic, rather than relying on a feeling about which subjects were his weak ones. The gaps were no longer guesses. They were rows in a table.

The logic is plain. Rereading familiar pages feels comfortable, because what you already know goes down easily. Marking turns that comfort into a measurement: a clue is either known or it is not, and the count of unknowns shows the size of the job. Looking at your own gaps can be unpleasant, but Craig's table turned that discomfort into a study plan.

From the labeled data he generated flashcard decks for the spaced-repetition program Anki, so each fact he missed came back at growing intervals. He says he had used spaced repetition for years, and that nearly all his review of material happened within that kind of system.

He has said he did not release his tool, because the clues belong to the show and are not his to share. Parts of this account come from his own descriptions, so the details are his, but the structure is clear enough. He diagnosed before he studied, he aimed the studying at the diagnosis, and he scheduled the review so that effort went to what was slipping.

Whether the method explains the result, nobody can prove. What the story does show is a different way to spend preparation time. Measure the gaps once, then work mostly on those gaps.

[[lessons]]
$$**Measure what you do not yet know before you decide what to study.** Reading everything evenly feels productive, but it spends hours on facts you already hold. Craig's labeling turned a vague sense of weakness into a ranked list, and the review schedule kept returning the items that were slipping. You can do a small version at work: list the questions a client or exam might ask, mark each one as known or unknown, and give the unknown ones your next study session. Study time then goes where it is needed, instead of material you already own, and each session starts from evidence.$$

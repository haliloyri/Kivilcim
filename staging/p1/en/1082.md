[[open]]
A fox circles a hedgehog. It is quick and cunning, and every day it comes back with a new plan: a feint from the left, a pounce from above, a patient ambush. The hedgehog has exactly one move. It rolls into a ball of spikes. And every single time, the fox walks away with nothing but a sore nose. Jim Collins thinks most of us are playing the fox.

[[story]]
In *Good to Great*, Jim Collins and his research team set out to answer a stubborn question. Why do some companies make a sustained jump from good results to great ones, while others in the same industry, with similar chances, never do? So they paired them up. On one side were the companies that made the leap. On the other were comparison companies that faced the same markets and didn't.

When they looked at how the two groups thought about strategy, Collins reached for an old image. He borrowed it from Isaiah Berlin's essay "The Hedgehog and the Fox."

> The fox knows many things. The hedgehog knows one big thing.

The fox keeps devising new attacks. It is clever and adaptable, and it is always working on the next idea. The hedgehog doesn't bother with any of that. It does the same simple thing every time, and the fox always loses.

That, Collins found, was the pattern in the data. ##The good-to-great companies were hedgehogs, and the comparison companies were foxes.## The winners built their strategy on one simple concept they understood deeply. The others were scattered and inconsistent. They chased whatever looked promising that year.

Collins called this the Hedgehog Concept, and he described it as the place where three circles overlap. The first circle is what you can be the best in the world at. The second is what drives your economic engine. The third is what you are deeply passionate about. The concept doesn't live in any one of these circles. It lives in the small space where all three meet.

Collins is careful about the first circle. He isn't talking about a goal, a vision statement, or a promise to be the best. He means an honest understanding of what you *can* be best at. The flip side matters just as much: knowing what you cannot be best at, even when that thing is your current core business. Plenty of companies are good at something they will never be great at, and staying there quietly holds them back.

The economic circle gets its own sharpening. Collins frames it as a single "economic denominator," one "profit per X" metric that most drives the business. Choosing X forces a company to decide what its business really runs on.

Walgreens shows how this works in practice. The company settled on being the best, most convenient drugstore. It tracked profit per customer visit. Then it acted on that focus in a way that cost something real: it exited its restaurant business so it could put everything into the drugstores. The restaurants weren't the thing Walgreens could be best at, so they had to go.

None of this arrived in a flash. Collins reports that the good-to-great companies reached their Hedgehog Concepts through years of discussion, going back over the question again and again. No single brainstorming session produced it. The simple answer was the result of a long, patient process.

[[lessons]]
$$**Simple beat clever.** The comparison companies weren't stupid. They behaved like the fox, full of new plans and shifting attacks. The good-to-great companies won by doing one thing they understood deeply. When a new opportunity looks exciting, the question is whether it pulls you away from the ball of spikes that actually works.$$

$$**Understanding, not ambition.** Collins drew a sharp line between wanting to be the best and understanding what you can be best at. The first is a wish. The second is a hard look at reality, and that includes admitting where you'll never lead, even in your core business. A Hedgehog Concept starts with honesty about your limits.$$

$$**One number clears the fog.** Walgreens chose profit per customer visit. With that one denominator in place, the restaurant business stopped looking like a nice extra and started looking like a distraction. A single "profit per X" gives you a test for every decision: does this move the number or not? The answer also tells you what to walk away from.$$

[[reflect]]
- List what filled your last month. Which items were fox moves, clever and new but shallow?
- Name something you do competently that, honestly, you could never be the best in the world at.
- If you tracked just one "profit per X" in your work or life, what would X be? What would you drop because of it?
- Remember that the companies took years to find their concept. What question could you keep coming back to, rather than trying to solve it in an afternoon?

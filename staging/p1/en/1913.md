[[open]]
Gold cups, crystal and murrhine bowls from the palace, robes woven with gold thread and jewelled ornaments are put up for sale in Trajan's Forum, day after day. The seller is the emperor himself, and some of the clothes belonged to his wife.

[[story]]
Ryan Holiday and Stephen Hanselman's *The Daily Stoic* is built around the Stoics, and Marcus Aurelius is one of its central figures. This story concerns a choice he made while he was at war with the Marcomanni and their allies along the Danube frontier. It is told by the Roman historian Eutropius, who wrote about two centuries later.

The war had drained the treasury. Marcus did not want to lay new levies on the provinces or on the Senate, so he looked at what he owned. According to Eutropius, he put the furnishings of the imperial household up for auction in Trajan's Forum in Rome.

The list is specific. There were gold vessels, cups of crystal and of murrhine, the silk and gold-woven clothing of his wife and himself, and a great many jewelled ornaments. The sale did not end in an afternoon: Eutropius says it ran for two months without a break, and that it raised a large sum of gold.

The fighting was run from Carnuntum and, in Eutropius's account, dragged on for three years. Then it ended in victory. Marcus defeated the Marcomanni and their allies, and he celebrated a triumph in Rome with his son Commodus.

What came next is the detail most often forgotten. Marcus gave the money back to any buyer who wanted to return what he had bought. He did not press anyone who preferred to keep the cup or the robe. A buyer could hand the goods back and take the money, or keep them and hear nothing more about it.

Look closely at the order of the choices. The emperor could have taxed, and Rome would have complained and paid. Instead he paid first, out of his own cupboards, and the provinces and the Senate were spared the new levies. So a ruler who sells his wife's silk is hard to accuse of asking others for sacrifices he would not make himself.

Eutropius is the source we have for this account, so it rests on one ancient voice. But the logic is plain enough to stand by itself: when something costly has to be given up, the person asking goes first.

[[lessons]]
$$**Open the bill with your own comforts before you reach for anyone else's.** A manager who must cut costs can start by trimming her own perks, and a parent asking the children to spend less can start with his own small luxuries. People measure the fairness of a sacrifice by who made it first, long before they read the reasons. When you go first, the request that follows comes from someone who has already paid, and that is a very different request to hear. It also tells you something about the plan itself, because anyone who is willing to pay first has usually thought about what the cost really is.$$

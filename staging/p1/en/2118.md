[[open]]
In a tuberculosis hospital in Sierra Leone, a visiting writer looks at the boy in front of him and guesses nine, the age of his own son. The boy is seventeen. The thing that has kept him so small is the same thing that is trying to kill him.

[[story]]
In 2019 the novelist John Green visited Lakka Government Hospital in Sierra Leone, the country's dedicated TB hospital, and met a patient named Henry Reider. Green has said in interviews that he took him for a nine-year-old, the same age as his own son, who is also called Henry. The boy looked like a child.

He was seventeen. Years of malnutrition and tuberculosis had stunted his growth so badly that, in Green's words, he looked much younger than he was. Henry had multidrug-resistant TB, a form that the standard drugs no longer touch, and he was already on the last line of antibiotics available to him. Green has repeated what a doctor told him, that very little was believed to be possible for Henry.

That meeting is why *Everything Is Tuberculosis* (2025) exists. Green has said he wrote the book partly because of one kid he met, one who demanded his attention. He asked himself what the circumstances Henry was living in said about the world we have built, and what world we might build instead.

Henry survived. Over the following years he gained access to treatments that are routine in wealthy countries, and, as Green puts it, he rallied. A caption in a published interview says he went on to college after beating multidrug-resistant TB. Green has stayed in touch with him.

Green chose to build his book around a person instead of a table of figures. "In the end, we're not moved by statistics but by human stories," he told one interviewer. He is just as plain about the medicine: the drugs exist, he says, but too often they are not near the people who need them.

Green also describes himself as someone who lives very close to despair. He told one interviewer that we can treat and cure the disease, and that failing to do so is a huge stain on humanity. He keeps a note in his wallet about child mortality to remind himself that positive change is possible.

Set his line about human stories beside his remark about the drugs and Henry's case is easy to read. The drugs that eventually helped him existed all along. What changed his odds was distance, between a hospital in Sierra Leone and the places where the right drugs are kept. A reader who cannot picture a million deaths a year can picture a teenager who looks nine.

[[lessons]]
$$**Follow one case from start to end before you trust a total.** A large number tells you how big a problem is, but one person's path through it tells you where the problem actually sits, and that is the part you can act on. A shop owner who is staring at a pile of complaints learns more from tracing a single order from checkout to the customer's door than from sorting the pile into categories. Once you have walked one case through, you can usually name the exact step where it went wrong, and that step is where effort pays.$$

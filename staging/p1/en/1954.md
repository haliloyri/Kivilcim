[[open]]
In restaurants near Washington Metro stops, a woman passes encrypted computer disks to the people she meets there. On weekdays she sits in a government building as the Defense Intelligence Agency's top analyst on Cuba, and nobody who works beside her has found a reason to doubt her.

[[story]]
Malcolm Gladwell's *Talking to Strangers* spends time on Ana Montes, and her case is a real one from the files of the FBI. In 1984 she held a clerical job at the Department of Justice, and she spoke openly against American policy in Central America. Cuban officials noticed, met with her, and she agreed to help Cuba. Court documents later called it a classic tale of recruitment.

Montes joined the Defense Intelligence Agency in 1985, already a fully recruited agent by the FBI's account. She rose to become the agency's top analyst on Cuba, known across the intelligence community for her expertise. She held a top-level clearance, and according to one report she also passed a polygraph.

Her method left almost nothing on paper. She memorized classified details at work, then typed them up on a laptop at home and copied them to encrypted disks. She handed the disks to her handlers in restaurants near Metro stops, and she received her instructions as coded messages over a shortwave radio.

Inside the agency, one colleague had a feeling. Scott Carmichael was a security and counterintelligence investigator, and FBI documents describe him as an astute colleague who, acting on a gut feeling, told a security official that she might be under the influence of Cuban intelligence. He had no proof, only a sense that something did not fit.

On 21 September 2001 she was arrested and charged with conspiracy to deliver national defense information to Cuba. She pleaded guilty and received a sentence of 25 years, and she was released in January 2023.

Look at what her colleagues had to go on. A top analyst, a long career, a clearance, a reported polygraph pass: every visible fact pointed the same way, and each one made suspicion feel unreasonable. The book's wider argument, drawn from psychologist Timothy Levine's work, is that people lean toward believing the person in front of them, and a record like hers is exactly what feeds that lean.

What finally moved the case was one investigator's discomfort. He was willing to turn it into a report instead of dropping it because everything else looked fine. The report was not an accusation; it asked that someone with the right tools take a look.

[[lessons]]
$$**Give a nagging mismatch one quiet check, whoever it concerns.** A good record tells you what someone has shown you so far, and it cannot tell you what they keep out of view. Think of a supplier who has delivered perfectly for years, yet one invoice does not add up, or a friend whose story about last weekend has a small gap in it. A single calm question or a quick look at the paperwork costs little, and most of the time it settles the matter in the person's favour. Either way you replace a vague unease with an answer, and you stop carrying the doubt around.$$

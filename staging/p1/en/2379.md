A werewolf looks like a neighbour until the moon comes up. The people who fear it are not afraid of its teeth so much as of the turn: something familiar changing without warning into something that wants you dead. Folklore has one answer, a bullet made of silver.

[[story]]
Fred Brooks opened a 1986 paper with exactly that image. The essay, "No Silver Bullet," appeared in the proceedings of the IFIP Tenth World Computing Conference, was reprinted in *IEEE Computer* in 1987, and later joined the 1995 anniversary edition of *The Mythical Man-Month*. The werewolf was software: a project that looks harmless and then goes over budget, past its date and full of faults. Everyone in the business, he wrote, was hunting for the silver bullet that would kill it.

Brooks borrowed a distinction from Aristotle to explain why the hunt kept failing. Some difficulties are accidents, the ones that come with how software is made today and are not part of the problem itself. Writing in assembly language was one of his examples, and waiting on batch processing was another. Both were real obstacles, and better tools had largely removed them.

Other difficulties are essential, built into what software is. He named four. Software is complex, and its parts interact in ways that make the whole grow much faster than the sum. It must conform to the arbitrary rules of the outside world, such as institutions, other systems and interfaces someone else designed. It is constantly asked to change. And it is invisible, so there is no floor plan or blueprint that lets anyone see it all at once.

From this he drew a prediction that sounded harsh in 1986. No single development in technology or management technique, he wrote, promised even one order of magnitude of improvement in productivity, reliability or simplicity within a decade. The reasoning is that once the accidental work is mostly gone, most of what remains is the essential work, and no tool removes the problem it is built to describe.

He did not stop at pessimism. He pointed to a few attacks that go at the essence: buying software instead of building it, refining requirements with prototypes, growing a system step by step instead of constructing it in one pass, and finding and developing great designers. On that last point he contrasted products shaped by one or a few minds, such as Unix and Pascal, with ones made by committee, such as COBOL and PL/I.

[[lessons]]
$$**Sort your obstacles into the ones a better tool removes and the ones that come with the problem.** A bakery that is always behind on Saturday orders might buy a bigger oven, which helps only if the oven was the limit; if the real limit is that customers change their orders on Friday night, no oven touches it. Naming which kind of difficulty you face tells you whether to shop for a fix or to change how you plan, ask and confirm. Then you can spend your money and your patience where they can actually work.$$

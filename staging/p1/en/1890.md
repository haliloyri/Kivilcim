[[open]]
A female digger wasp backs out of a burrow in the ground and flies off. A while later a second female lands at the same entrance and goes in. To a human watcher, two wasps sharing one nest looks like a small act of cooperation, the kind that is easy to admire.

[[story]]
The biologist Jane Brockmann studied these wasps, the species *Sphex ichneumoneus*, in two populations in North America. Was the sharing what it seemed? In 1979 she published a paper with Alan Grafen and Richard Dawkins, the author of *The Selfish Gene* (1976), titled "Evolutionarily stable nesting strategy in a digger wasp". Its question was simple: when a female finds herself in a burrow someone else dug, why is she there?

They tested two explanations, and each one was written as a model with predictions. The first said that wasps either found a burrow or joined one, and that a founder does better when a joiner moves in. If that were true, the two roles should end up equally successful, and the result would be a cooperative arrangement. The field data rejected this model clearly. A model that predicts equal success gives a precise target, and when the data miss it, the model fails cleanly. That is what makes a model more useful than a good story: it can lose.

The second explanation was less flattering. Some females dig their own burrows, but they often abandon them when conditions turn unsuitable. Other females, the enterers, take over such burrows without checking whether they are truly empty. On this account, two wasps in one burrow are not partners at all. The sharing is a side effect of one female exploiting what another has left behind, and nobody is helping anybody.

This second model fitted the numbers well in one of the two populations and badly in the other. The authors described it as one of the few known examples in nature of a mixed evolutionarily stable strategy, where a species keeps both behaviours in the population because each does equally well while the other exists. They also suggested that the word "decision" might be clearer than "strategy", since the wasp is committing its time to one course of action and is not working out a plan.

The paper is a lesson in how a good story can mislead. A shared burrow looks like teamwork. Tested properly, the cooperation model failed, and a plainer account of digging and entering explained more. The same sight, two wasps and one burrow, can come from very different causes, and only the pattern of costs and gains tells them apart.

[[lessons]]
$$**When two people seem to be working together, check who pays the cost and who collects the benefit.** In a group project, one student may do the research while another puts a name on the cover and presents the result, and from outside it looks like a team. The test is to look at what each person put in and what each takes out, rather than at the fact that they share a deliverable. Once you can see that, you can ask the question kindly and early, since the answer may be innocent, and either rebalance the work or stop treating the arrangement as teamwork you can build on.$$

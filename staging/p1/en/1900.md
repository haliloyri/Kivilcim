A woman stands in a crowd of villagers in the hills of Burundi and listens to an outsider's plan. The plan has a price on it, and the price is more than most people there will see in years. She says one sentence about a single franc, and the meeting changes direction.

[[story]]
In December 2005, Deo Niyizonkiza travelled to Kigutu, his home village in Burundi, with an idea. He was a medical student who had fled war, survived homelessness in New York and studied abroad, and he wanted to build a health clinic where he grew up. According to a profile in Columbia Magazine, a committee was formed at a gathering of community members that same Christmas Day.

The land was transferred in 2006. Deo took leave from his medical school at Dartmouth and, as the magazine reports, spent fifteen months living in a tent while he oversaw the work. The clinic, called Village Health Works, was founded in 2007 on ten acres, around 6,000 feet above sea level.

Then came the road. A clinic is no use if patients and supplies cannot reach it, and an engineering firm quoted $50,000 to build one. A villager named Cécille objected, and the magazine quotes her words: "If you have only one franc, don't spend it on the road."

The community did not stop at objecting. Hundreds of people cleared the brush with machetes and built the seven-mile road by hand. Seven miles of road, cleared by hand with machetes, is a great deal of shared labour, and it turned a quote into a village project. Years later, after the president of Burundi first visited the clinic in 2011, the government re-graded it.

Notice what did not happen. The experts did not lose the argument by being proved wrong about the need for a road, since the road was built. What changed was who built it and how. A figure that looked like a fixed cost turned out to be partly a choice, and the person who saw that was not an engineer. She was someone who lived there and knew what a franc was worth.

Planning for people from the outside is a habit with a built-in blind spot. The planner knows the technical options and the market price. The residents know what they have: labour, time, land, relationships, and a sense of what is worth paying for. The meeting in Kigutu is a small case of those two kinds of knowledge meeting, and of a project that went along with the villagers' answer and let them carry the road.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you pay for a solution, ask the people who live with the problem how they would do it with what they already have.** In a project, a household budget or a team reorganisation, the first quote you receive is rarely the only route. Someone close to the work often sees a cheaper or more fitting way, and may only speak up if the room invites it. Put the question on the table early, such as "What could we already do ourselves?", and treat the answer as real information, not as a sign of resistance, even when it sounds like an objection.$$

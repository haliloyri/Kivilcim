[[open]]
On Omelek, a small island in the Kwajalein Atoll in the Pacific, a young company's rocket lifts off with a 19.5-kilogram satellite on top. Within a minute a fire has ended the flight. The crew will return to this island twice more over the next two and a half years, and both returns will end the same way.

[[story]]
Walter Isaacson's *Elon Musk* is a biography of the founder of SpaceX and Tesla, and one of the stretches that tested him hardest was the early Falcon 1 programme. The first launch, on 24 March 2006, flew from Omelek Island with FalconSAT-2, a 19.5-kilogram satellite, on board. The rocket failed less than a minute in. Published accounts trace it to a leak in a fuel line that started a fire.

Flight two came on 21 March 2007, carrying a demonstration satellite for DARPA and NASA. It went much further, up to 289 kilometres, but it never reached orbit. Accounts describe contact between the stages as they separated, then growing oscillations in the second stage and an engine that shut down early, about seven and a half minutes after liftoff.

Flight three followed on 3 August 2008 with several small payloads, NanoSail-D among them. This time the first-stage engine kept pushing for a moment after it was meant to stop, and the two stages bumped into each other. Another failure, with another cause, and another launch to pay for.

All three had lifted off from the same island in the Pacific. Three flights had three different causes: a fuel line, an oscillation, and thrust left over after shutdown. None repeated the one before. The money, though, drained at the same rate with every launch. Musk later told Eric Berger of Ars Technica that he had run out of money; he had put his capital into Tesla and SolarCity, and he spoke of having three failures under his belt. At a 2017 IAC talk he said the fourth flight was the last money he had for Falcon 1.

What stands out is that each failure exposed a different part of the rocket. Fixing a leaking fuel line does nothing for an oscillating second stage, so every fault was its own engineering job and its own launch to pay for. A team that makes the same mistake three times and a team that meets a new problem every time are not in the same place, but a bank balance cannot tell them apart. For anyone with a limited number of attempts, the useful question is which uncertainty the next attempt will close. With three launches behind them and a little money left, the crew on the island prepared for a fourth.

[[lessons]]
$$**Count how many real attempts your money buys, and give each one a new question to answer.** A launch, a pilot or a budget cycle allows only a limited number of tries, and that number is usually smaller than the plan assumes. Before the first attempt, write down how many you can afford and what each one should teach you, so that even a failure removes one cause from the list. Three flights with three different causes meant three real lessons, yet the money went out at the same pace, and that mismatch is the point. If you can see the count from the start, you choose which risk to take first instead of discovering the limit on your last try.$$

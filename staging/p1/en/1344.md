[[open]]
Dan Harris was a skeptical reporter handed the religion beat at ABC News. Somewhere in that assignment he came across a description of the voice in your head, the one that never stops talking, and it matched his own mind with uncomfortable accuracy. The problem was what came next. The teacher who described it so well didn't seem to know what to do about it.

[[story]]
The description came from Eckhart Tolle. As Harris relays it, Tolle's claim is that the ego is essentially the incessant voice in our heads: the running commentary that judges, plans, worries and compares all day long. Most people, Tolle says, identify with that voice completely. They assume it simply is them.

His next move is the one that struck Harris. We are not the voice, Tolle argues. We are the one who can be aware of it. The commentary keeps going, but there is something in you that can hear it, and that something is not the same as the words.

For Harris, the diagnosis landed hard. His own inner narration was relentless, ambitious and anxious, always pushing and always worried about what came next. Reading Tolle, he recognized it immediately. Here was a clear account of the thing running his days.

##But a diagnosis is not a treatment.## Harris found Tolle vague on the question that mattered most to him: how do you actually stop being run by the voice? It's one thing to be told you are not your thoughts. It's another to know what to do at the moment a familiar thought shows up and starts giving orders.

So the insight sat there, convincing but hard to use. For Harris, a good question with a fuzzy answer wasn't enough.

He found the practical method elsewhere, in mindfulness meditation and teachers such as Mark Epstein and Joseph Goldstein. Mindfulness gave him what Tolle hadn't: an exercise. It trains you to notice thoughts as they arise, instead of automatically believing them and acting on them. The voice doesn't go silent. You simply get more practiced at seeing it as a voice.

That's the shape of his story on this point. A skeptic finds an idea that describes his mind almost too well, refuses to stop at the description, and keeps looking until he finds something he can practice. The idea that he isn't the voice in his head only became useful once he had a way to catch that voice in action.

[[lessons]]
$$**The commentary isn't you.** Tolle's point, as Harris relays it, is that most of us mistake the running voice in our heads for who we are. Seeing it as a voice, rather than as the truth about yourself, is the first shift in how much power it has.$$

$$**Diagnosis isn't a method.** Harris found Tolle's description of the problem compelling and his prescription vague. Plenty of ideas explain a problem beautifully and leave you with nothing to do on a Tuesday afternoon. It's worth noticing when you've been given only half, and going looking for the other half rather than stopping there.$$

$$**Practice turns insight into skill.** Harris got from understanding to change through mindfulness meditation, a repeated exercise in noticing thoughts as they arise. The insight stayed the same. What changed was having a way to use it when the voice actually started talking.$$

[[reflect]]
- What does your inner voice say most often when things go wrong?
- Can you remember a moment when you noticed a thought instead of simply believing it?
- Is there an idea you agree with completely but have never found a way to practice?

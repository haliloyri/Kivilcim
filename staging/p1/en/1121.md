[[open]]
Picture a hotel ballroom in Manhattan full of investors and stock analysts. They've come to hear a new CEO talk about money: margins, growth, the numbers they'll trade on tomorrow. He steps up and starts pointing out where the emergency exits are. Some of them are already reaching for their phones. By the time he left the job, they would wish they had listened.

[[story]]
It's October 1987. Paul O'Neill has just been named CEO of Alcoa, one of America's big industrial companies, and this is his first presentation to Wall Street. The room expects a certain speech. A new chief usually talks about profits, about trimming costs, about where growth will come from. Analysts want something they can put in a report.

O'Neill doesn't give them any of that. He talks about worker safety. He tells the room he intends to make Alcoa the safest company in America, and the goal is zero injuries. Not fewer, zero. And to show he means it right down to the room they're sitting in, he points out the ballroom's emergency exits.

The audience doesn't know what to do with this. People glance at each other, waiting for the part about money. It never really arrives. One investor gets up, walks out, and calls his clients with blunt advice: sell.

> "The board just put a crazy hippie in charge, and he's going to kill the company."

That was the snap judgment, and on the surface it made sense. A CEO facing Wall Street had set aside the numbers everyone wanted and staked his tenure on something that looked like a side issue.

But O'Neill had a mechanism, not just a slogan. He set one strict rule. Whenever a worker was injured, anywhere in the company, it had to be reported to him within 24 hours. And the report couldn't just describe what happened. It had to come with a plan to make sure it never happened again.

Think about what that rule demands. A unit president can't send the CEO a prevention plan in 24 hours unless they hear about the injury almost immediately. So unit presidents had to stay in constant contact with their floor managers. Floor managers needed to know what was going on at the machines, so workers were encouraged to speak up and suggest improvements. And a real plan to prevent an injury usually meant changing how the work itself was done, so processes got redesigned.

##O'Neill never ordered better communication or leaner processes. His safety rule couldn't be met without them.##

Those redesigned processes did more than prevent injuries. A safer way of running a production line was often a better way of running it, so quality and efficiency rose along with safety.

Then the results came in. Within a year of that confusing speech, Alcoa's profits hit a record high. By the time O'Neill retired in 2000, the company's annual net income was roughly five times larger than when he arrived, and Alcoa had become one of the safest companies in the world. The "crazy hippie" had delivered exactly what the investor feared he would ignore.

In *The Power of Habit*, Charles Duhigg uses this case to explain what he calls **keystone habits**. These are habits that, once changed, set off chain reactions that reshape other habits across an organization or a life. At Alcoa, safety was the keystone. O'Neill pulled that one stone, and the rest of the arch rearranged itself around it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Find the habit that drags others with it.** Safety at Alcoa wasn't one improvement on a long list. Getting to zero injuries couldn't happen without new reporting habits, new communication habits and new process habits across the whole company. O'Neill picked the one change that forced dozens of others, which is why a single goal ended up reshaping everything.$$

$$**A small, strict rule can rewire how information moves.** "Report every injury to me within 24 hours, with a plan" sounds like bureaucracy. In practice it made executives and plant floors talk all the time, because nobody could meet the deadline otherwise. The rule was simple enough to enforce and demanding enough to change behavior. It worked because it had a clock on it and it asked for a plan, not an excuse.$$

$$**The indirect goal can deliver the direct result.** Wall Street wanted profit talk, and O'Neill never made profit his headline. Yet profits hit a record within a year, and by 2000 annual net income was about five times larger. Chasing the thing people demanded head-on might have divided the company. "Nobody gets hurt" was a goal unions and managers could both stand behind, and the money followed the better way of working.$$

[[reflect]]
- Look at your own list of things to fix. Is there one change that, like Alcoa's safety rule, would force several of the others to happen?
- Where are you spreading effort across many goals when one lever might move the rest?
- If you set a single strict rule for yourself, with a deadline built in, what would it be?
- Which goal in your team or family could everyone support, even people who disagree about everything else?

&&If you could change only one habit this year, which one would quietly pull everything else along with it?&&

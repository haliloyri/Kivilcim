[[open]]
A lab assistant adds iodine to a dog's urine. The dog had been given quinine, a bitter drug, and the scientists wanted to know what happened to it inside the body. Tiny emerald-green crystals form in the dish. The crystals turn out to be the seed of the first synthetic light polariser, the kind later used in sunglasses and camera filters.

[[story]]
The man behind the experiment was William Bird Herapath, a physician and toxicologist. He was studying why quinine passed through the body unchanged. The year was 1852. According to the account that later reached the public, his assistant added the iodine to the urine of a dog that had been dosed with the drug.

Herapath put the green crystals under a microscope. Something odd happened when he laid them over one another. In one position they let light through, and in another they went completely black. They were polarising light, sorting it by direction, which at the time was done with costly natural minerals such as tourmaline. Colleagues named the compound herapathite in his honour, and he went on to refine a method for making large sheets, using acetic acid instead of the dog.

Then the discovery sat there. Decades later, an American called Edwin Land came across herapathite while reading an account of the kaleidoscope by its inventor, David Brewster. Land's idea was to use huge numbers of tiny crystals instead of one big one. His first polarising sheets contained Herapath's crystals, and in 1937 he founded the Polaroid company. The wartime quinine shortage then complicated the supply, because the drug was needed to treat malaria in soldiers.

Morgan Housel includes this chain in *Same as Ever*, drawing on Safi Bahcall's account, as an example of a pattern he sees everywhere: the useful thing often comes from a direction nobody was looking.

Look at what was actually being pursued. Herapath wanted to understand a drug's journey through the body. Land wanted a way to cut glare. Neither goal contained the other, and nobody could have drawn the route from one to the other in advance, while the link between them was a byproduct on a laboratory dish that someone was curious enough to look at twice.

It took two people with different questions, decades apart. For Herapath the crystals were a surprise inside a pharmacology problem, and he followed the surprise far enough to learn how to make them in sheets. For Land they were the missing material for a different goal, and he only found them because he read widely, in a book about a toy.

[[lessons]]
$$**Examine the odd byproduct of your work before you throw it out.** Every project produces leftovers, such as an unexpected customer complaint, a failed prototype or a spreadsheet column nobody asked for. Most of it is noise, but a few are the start of something you could not have planned, because planning only reaches as far as the question you already know how to ask. Keep a small place where the strange results go and look through it from time to time. The cost is an afternoon of curiosity now and then, and the possible payoff is a use no one has named yet.$$

[[open]]
At a music academy in Berlin, the faculty are asked which of their violin students could one day play as international soloists. Then a researcher sits each of those students down and puts one plain question to them: how many hours a week did you practise alone, in every year since you started?

[[story]]
The study was run by K. Anders Ericsson, who would later write *Peak* with Robert Pool, together with Ralf Krampe and Clemens Tesch-Römer. At the Music Academy of West Berlin they asked the faculty to nominate violin students with the potential for careers as international soloists. Fourteen were nominated and ten agreed to take part. The researchers then found ten other good violinists from the same department and ten students from the music education programme, which had lower admission standards. All three groups were matched for sex and age.

Each student sat for an interview. They described their lives with the violin and estimated how many hours a week they had practised alone in every year since they began. The researchers multiplied those weekly figures by the weeks in a year and added them up. Students also rated activities for how relevant, how effortful and how enjoyable they were, and kept a diary for one week, divided into 15-minute blocks.

The totals up to age 18 followed the ranking. The best violinists had averaged 7,410 hours of solo practice, the good ones 5,301, and the future music teachers 3,420. These were hours alone, not lessons or ensemble playing, and they were counted only to 18 so that time at the academy would not blur the comparison.

The numbers need careful handling. They are estimates from memory, and the diaries showed that students tended to overstate how much they practised. The authors judged the estimates valid but biased, and they found that the bias did not differ between groups. The study also cannot show that solitary practice made the best violinists the best. It shows that groups chosen at the same academy had very different histories of working alone.

What the comparison does show is a spread inside one city and one age bracket. The best group's average was more than double the future teachers' average, and the interview asked only about hours spent alone, the part of a violinist's training that nobody else can do for them.

Many readers know a different figure from this research, 10,000 hours. According to later accounts of the work, the best group had passed that mark by about age 20. It is an average for a group of ten people, not a threshold, and the study never named an hour count at which expertise begins. A 2019 attempt to replicate it, by Macnamara and Maitra, found differences smaller than the original ones.

[[lessons]]
$$**Keep a log of your own focused practice instead of trusting your memory of it.** The students' estimates ran higher than their diaries, and almost everyone remembers their own effort generously. Someone learning guitar after work can write one line each evening about the minutes spent on the part that is still hard, and after a month that page will say more about their progress than their impression does. A record like that also lets you compare this month with last month rather than yourself with somebody else, which keeps the question honest. A real number also tells you what to change next, because it shows how little or how much of your week the hard part actually gets.$$

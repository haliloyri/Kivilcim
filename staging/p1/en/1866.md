A postal worker in Harlem sits down to gather, into one small guide, the places where Black travellers could stop without being turned away or put at risk. He has no government backing and no large publisher behind him. Within thirty years that guide would be in more than two million hands.

[[story]]
Isabel Wilkerson's *The Warmth of Other Suns* tells of Black Americans setting out through a country that discriminated against them. This story concerns a guide that travellers of those years carried, the Green Book. Victor Hugo Green was born in 1892, lived in Harlem and worked for the postal service for 39 years.

In 1936 he published the first edition in Harlem. Its purpose was to help Black travellers avoid danger, humiliation and harassment in a country where discrimination was both legal and routine. Some towns did not want Black people on their streets after sunset, and some businesses would not serve them. The guide showed, entry by entry, where a traveller could stay and where they would be served.

His wife Alma helped from the start, but her name appeared as editor and publisher only in 1959. When Victor died in 1960, she ran the whole operation. Her work had gone uncredited on the title for more than twenty years, which says something about how much of the labour behind a useful thing stays out of sight. Over its lifetime the guide listed more than ten thousand businesses, among them more than 1,400 tourist homes, private houses that took in travellers.

Distribution found another partner. James A. Jackson, a Black marketing executive at Esso, arranged for Esso stations across the country to sell the book. Green announced Jackson's appointment in the 1939 edition. By 1962 the Green Book had a circulation of more than two million.

It was published for about thirty years, a long run for a guide that began as one man's side project in Harlem. The 1964-65 and 1966-67 editions were put out by Langley Waller, and the last of them welcomed the Civil Rights Act of 1964 as a new bill of rights for everyone.

The strength of this story lies in the detail, not in the size of the book. Green was not in a position to change a discriminatory order, but he knew how to collect and spread information. An address and a name could mean the difference between a safe night and a dangerous one for a traveller. No giant organisation was needed. One person's accurate, usable knowledge, gathered and passed along, became a tool that reached millions, and the value of the list came from each line answering a question a traveller was asking: can I stay here, can I eat here.

[[lessons]]
$$**Turn what you know into a concrete list that lowers someone else's risk.** Even where you cannot change a big system, gathering what you know from your own corner and sharing it can save another person time, money or safety. Writing down for a new teammate which meetings matter and which supplier keeps its word is a small version of this. The more addresses, names and figures a list contains, the more useful it is, and a list you keep updating is worth far more than the first version. Start with the question a newcomer asks most often, and answer it in one specific line.$$

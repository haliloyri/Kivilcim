A letter crosses an ocean. It is short and it asks for something specific: copies of a document. The writer addresses someone far away, he knows the name, and he has a reason to think the document might help the people he speaks for.

[[story]]
The writer was B. R. Ambedkar, the leading voice of India's Dalits, the people long labelled untouchables. The man he wrote to was W. E. B. Du Bois, the American scholar and activist. The letter is undated, and it is thought to date from 1946. In it Ambedkar asked for copies of the petition that the National Negro Congress had submitted to the United Nations in search of minority rights. According to published readings of the letter, he added that he knew Du Bois by name, as everyone working for minority rights did.

Du Bois answered on 31 July 1946. He wrote that he had often heard of Ambedkar and his work, and that he had "every sympathy with the Untouchables of India." The letters are preserved in archives, with Du Bois's papers held at the University of Massachusetts and copies in the South Asian American Digital Archive. Two people who led movements for the rights of groups ranked at the bottom of very different societies had, for once, written to each other directly.

The details are modest, and they should be told as modest. Nothing in the surviving letters says what Ambedkar did with the petition, and it would be wrong to claim a great collaboration came of it. What the exchange shows is a method. Ambedkar appears to have wanted to learn how a minority could take its case to the United Nations, and he did not start from nothing. He looked for another group that had already written a petition for the same forum, and he asked for the text.

That choice carries an idea about how to work. The injustice he faced was in India, the petition he wanted was American, and the document was a model, a way to see how a case is made in the language of an international body. Wilkerson's *Caste* leans on the same instinct, setting India, Nazi Germany and the United States side by side so that each can be read in the light of the others. The method is old, and it keeps working: a case made somewhere else, in a form that has already met an audience, teaches more than a lecture on how to begin.

[[lessons]]
$$**When your problem looks unique, someone has probably already written a draft of it.** Ambedkar asked a stranger on another continent for a finished document and used it as a starting point for his own. In your own work the equivalent is a template, a proposal or a post-mortem written by someone whose situation resembles yours. Send the short message, name what you want to see, and say why you are asking. One letter costs far less than building the whole argument from nothing, and the person who answers may be glad that the work helped someone else.$$

[[open]]
A psychologist walks into an interview room and drops a pile of papers. He laughs loudly, makes a joke about his tie, and watches the candidate across from him. The questions he asks afterwards matter less than what that person does in the next two seconds.

[[story]]
In *Supercommunicators* (2024), Charles Duhigg takes the reader to NASA in the 1980s. President Reagan had called for a space station, and that changed the kind of astronaut the agency needed. Crews would no longer spend a few days together; they might live side by side for six months to a year. A brilliant pilot or scientist was not enough. NASA wanted people with emotional intelligence, and the kind that could not be faked.

That was the difficulty. Candidates were smart and prepared, and many of them could perform warmth in an interview. For two years, in Duhigg's telling, the agency's top psychologists could not come up with a screening method they trusted.

Then one psychologist noticed a pattern in the people who had succeeded. When they were asked questions, their answers were much like everyone else's. What differed was how they laughed. So he built it into his interviews. As he came into the room he spilled his papers on the floor, laughed loudly and joked about his tie. Then he watched whether the candidate laughed back.

Laughter explains why a joke about a tie could carry so much. Studies suggest that about eighty percent of laughter is not a response to anything funny. People laugh to signal that they want to connect, so a loud laugh from a stranger works as an invitation, and a candidate who laughs along is accepting it. A candidate who sits stiffly is making a different choice, whichever words come out next.

Duhigg has also described a second move from the same interviews. Later in the conversation the psychologist would tell a sad story about a relative who had died, and note who asked questions or showed concern. Both moments gave him a sample of how a person handles an unscripted, shared moment, which is what months of living together would be made of.

Duhigg uses the episode to make a wider point. Much of what we communicate travels outside the words: in tone, in timing, in whether our energy matches the other person's. People who connect well tend to pick this up, and respond to it, long before they notice they are doing it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Answer a small signal such as a laugh before you move on to business.** Suppose a new hire opens your first one-on-one with a nervous joke about the parking. If you push on with the agenda, she learns that her attempt to connect was not received, and she will try less next time. A smile, a short laugh or a single sentence back takes two seconds and tells her you noticed. You can watch for these small offers in meetings and at the start of calls, and treat each one as a question about whether you are going to meet the person or just run through your list.$$

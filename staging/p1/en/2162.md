One worker in four has no job. Shares are worth a tenth of what they were three years earlier. Meanwhile, inside company laboratories across the same country, the payroll of people hired to invent things keeps growing. Both are true at once, and only one of them makes the headlines.

[[story]]
Morgan Housel's *Same as Ever* uses the 1930s as proof of how badly people read their own moment. The decade has a reputation, and it is earned: by 1932 almost a quarter of Americans were out of work, and the stock market had lost roughly 89 percent of its value from its 1929 peak.

The economist Alexander Field went looking at a different set of numbers. In a 2003 article he called the 1930s the most technologically progressive decade of the century, and he later built the case into a book, *A Great Leap Forward*. His evidence is not about mood. Total research and development employment in the United States went from 10,918 in 1933 to 27,777 in 1940.

What that research produced was often unglamorous. In a review of Field's book, the economist David Henderson lists improved beet-sugar extraction, railroad ties that lasted eight to twenty years, and paint that dried in hours instead of three weeks. Another gain raised the capacity of electric generating plants by 40 to 90 percent with little extra fuel or labour.

The payoff shows up in one comparison. By 1941 about as many people were working, and about as much capital was in use, as in 1929. Yet real output was 33 to 40 percent higher. Field calculates that productivity grew 2.3 to 2.8 percent a year over those twelve years, which he describes as the best twelve-year stretch of the twentieth century. He also argues that the Second World War does not explain most of it.

Notice what this does not say. The pain was real, and unemployment was still 6.3 percent in the last quarter of 1941. Nobody who stood in a job line in 1932 was comforted by a laboratory payroll.

But output grew without extra hands or extra machines, and that can only mean something got smarter. Two ledgers were running side by side. One recorded what hurt, in lines at employment offices and collapsing prices. The other recorded what was being built, in lab notebooks, factory routines and quieter ways of making things. The first was loud and the second was slow, so people living through the decade mostly saw the first.

Housel's wider point is that the picture people hold of any period is made from whatever is easiest to see. The sorrow of the 1930s was easy to see. The compounding of know-how was not.

[[lessons]]
$$**In a dark stretch, keep a second ledger of what is quietly being built.** A company can have a terrible year on the income statement while one team rewrites a process that will save it a month of work every quarter after. A person can go through a rough year and still come out with a skill, a habit or a relationship that did not exist before. When the news is bad, judge the period by two columns, the visible damage and the unseen accumulation. Doing so does not make the damage any smaller, but it stops you from deciding the whole season was lost.$$

[[story]]
Marco Polo, the Venetian who served at the court of Kublai Khan, left one of the fullest descriptions of the Mongol postal service, called the yam. Jack Weatherford's *Genghis Khan and the Making of the Modern World* gives the system a prominent place in the story of the Mongol empire. Polo's own words, in the Yule translation, show how it worked.

Every twenty-five miles or so, he says, along the main roads, stood a post-house, which the Mongols called a *yamb*. Each was a large building with fine beds and everything a messenger could want. At some stations four hundred horses stood ready, at others two hundred. Polo claims there were more than 300,000 horses and more than 10,000 such buildings across the empire, figures that are his own and hard to check.

Between the post-houses, about every three miles, he describes a little settlement of some forty houses where foot-runners lived. Each wore a broad belt hung with bells. As he ran the three miles to the next station, the bells jingled a long way off, and a relief runner was waiting. The first man handed over his charge and received a slip of paper from the clerk, and the new man ran his three miles. With a change at every stage, Polo reports, the emperor received news from ten days away in a single day and night.

For true emergencies there were riders. Polo says they covered a good two hundred or two hundred and fifty miles in a day and as much by night, taking a fresh, saddled horse at every station. Each carried a gerfalcon tablet showing that he was on an urgent express, and with it he could make anyone on the road give up a horse if his own broke down. The clerks wrote down the time each courier arrived and left, and officers toured monthly to punish the slack ones.

The cost was spread out. Each town or village near a station was asked to supply as many horses as it could. In empty country, according to Polo, the emperor paid for them himself, and the runners were exempted from tribute and paid besides. The Secret History of the Mongols, in published accounts, lists the setting up of post stations among the good deeds of Chinggis's son Ögedei, so the system's roots go back before Polo's time.

What stands out is that no single person goes far. A runner covers three miles, and a horse covers one stage. What travels from Cambaluc to a distant province is the message, and the speed lives in the handoffs: the ready horse, the waiting man, the slip of paper, the clerk who notes the time. It is a system where nobody has to be heroic, because each stage has already been prepared by someone else.

[[lessons]]
$$**Speed comes from the handoffs, so prepare the next person before you pass anything on.** In a bakery or a clinic, the busiest hour often goes wrong at the moment one shift hands over to the next, when a note is missing or nobody knows what is half-finished. If the next person is ready, with the horse saddled and a clear slip of paper in hand, the work doesn't slow down while the first person explains. Fixing the handover costs less than asking each person to work faster, and it lets you move work across many hands without losing it.$$

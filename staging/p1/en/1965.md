Tens of thousands of Seventh-day Adventists filled in a food questionnaire, once, at the start. Their answers were later set against the national record of deaths. The headline that came out of it was a single number, 0.88, and the range printed beside it matters as much as the number.

[[story]]
The Adventist Health Study 2 is a large North American cohort, and it is one of the large studies on plant-based diets and mortality, the territory Michael Greger's *How Not to Die* covers. Between 2002 and 2007 researchers recruited 96,469 Seventh-day Adventist men and women. After exclusions, 73,308 remained for the analysis of diet and death that appeared in *JAMA Internal Medicine* in 2013.

At the start, each person completed a food frequency questionnaire. The researchers sorted the answers into five dietary patterns: non-vegetarian, semi-vegetarian, pesco-vegetarian (fish but no other meat), lacto-ovo-vegetarian and vegan. Then they waited for deaths, which they identified through the National Death Index up to 2009.

During a mean follow-up of 5.79 years there were 2,570 deaths among the 73,308 people. After adjusting for demographic and lifestyle factors, the researchers compared each pattern with the non-vegetarians. All vegetarians combined had a hazard ratio for death from any cause of 0.88, with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.80 to 0.97.

A hazard ratio compares the risk of dying between groups, so 0.88 describes an association with roughly 12 percent lower risk. The patterns looked different one by one. Pesco-vegetarians had the lowest estimate at 0.81, with a range of 0.69 to 0.94. Vegans were at 0.85, but their range ran from 0.73 to 1.01, so it touched the line where there is no difference at all. Lacto-ovo-vegetarians were at 0.91 (0.82 to 1.00) and semi-vegetarians at 0.92 (0.75 to 1.13). The associations were larger and more often significant in men than in women.

This was an observational study, so it describes who died, not why. People chose their diets, and a single questionnaire at the start cannot follow what they ate over the next years. The authors wrote that vegetarian diets were associated with lower all-cause mortality and some reductions in cause-specific mortality, which is a careful way to say what the data show.

The fine print is where the study teaches something. A reader who keeps only the 0.88 hears a clean message. A reader who looks at the ranges hears a quieter one: the direction is consistent, the vegan interval brushes against no effect, and the lowest estimate belonged to the pattern that still included fish.

[[lessons]]
$$**The range around a number tells you how loudly you may repeat it.** A result of 0.85 sounds like a verdict, yet a range that reaches 1.01 says it is compatible with no difference at all, and a range of 0.69 to 0.94 says something firmer. The next time someone quotes you a percentage from a study, an A/B test or a survey, ask for the interval or the sample size before you pass it on. You can still use the finding, only at the volume it earned, and you will be the one in the room who knows what it can carry.$$

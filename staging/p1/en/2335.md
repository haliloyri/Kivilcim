[[open]]
Under the stands of a university stadium, in a court once used for rackets, a group of scientists stand around a huge stack. A man pulls a long rod out of it. After a lunch break they will find out whether a reaction can sustain itself, and they have made sure beforehand that it can be stopped.

[[story]]
Richard Rhodes's *The Making of the Atomic Bomb* reaches its first great proof in Chicago. Construction of the experimental reactor, Chicago Pile-1, finished on 1 December 1942, and on 2 December it went critical. The place was an abandoned rackets court beneath Stagg Field at the University of Chicago, and the team was led by Enrico Fermi.

The key part was the brake. The pile contained cadmium control rods, about fourteen feet long, which absorb neutrons. With the rods in, the reaction was held back. Pulling them out let it build. According to the Atomic Heritage Foundation, George Weil pulled them out under Fermi's direction.

That afternoon, after a lunch break, the pile reached what physicists call criticality. Its multiplication factor, k, rose to 1.0006, a hair above the point at which each generation of neutrons exactly replaces itself. The output at that moment was only half a watt.

Fermi did not stretch the moment. After less than five minutes at critical, he ordered the reactor shut off. Later runs went further, and the pile eventually operated at a maximum of 200 watts, but on that first afternoon the aim was a yes, not a performance. The question was whether it could be done at all, and half a watt answered it. Asking for more that afternoon would have meant taking a risk for nothing.

Then came the celebration, and it was modest. Eugene Wigner had saved a bottle of Bertolli Chianti. The team drank it from paper cups, and they signed the straw wrapping around the bottle.

Arthur Compton telephoned James Conant with a report. As Atomic Heritage records it, Compton said the Italian navigator had just landed in the New World. Conant asked whether the natives were friendly, and Compton answered that everyone had landed safe and happy.

The detail that stays is the order of operations. A team about to start a self-sustaining nuclear reaction for the first time built the means of stopping it first, and the numbers they chose to demonstrate were minuscule. The same logic shows in the celebration, which was a bottle and some paper cups after the shutdown, not before it. Every part of the day was shaped around one sentence: it must be possible to turn this off.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you start something you cannot easily undo, build the brake, and stop at the first proof.** Power you can't stop is a liability, however impressive it looks. In your own work this might be a feature released to five percent of users with a switch to turn it off, or a new process trialled for one week with a date to review it. A small, controllable proof gives you the answer you need and leaves you free to scale up the next time with confidence. That is a better trade than speed. It also gives whoever approves the work a clear reason to say yes.$$

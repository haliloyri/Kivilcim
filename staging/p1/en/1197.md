A young psychiatrist sits across from a veteran who, on paper, is doing well: a good job, a family, a home. The war is years behind him. And yet, in every way that matters, he is still there. That meeting set the direction for Bessel van der Kolk's life's work.

[[story]]
In the late 1970s, Bessel van der Kolk began working as a psychiatrist at a Veterans Administration clinic in Boston. In the opening chapter of *The Body Keeps the Score*, he describes his first patient there, a Vietnam veteran he calls Tom.

Tom was, by outward measures, successful. He was a lawyer. He had a wife and young children. But he was tormented. Memories of the war intruded constantly. Certain days, certain sounds, certain situations sent him right back. He couldn't feel close to his own family. He felt as if part of him had never come home.

Tom was far from alone. Van der Kolk met many veterans with similar experiences. The war had ended. They were back in ordinary American towns. But ordinary sights and sounds could set off reactions as if they were still in combat. Their bodies went on high alert. Their hearts raced. They felt the old fear, the old rage, the old helplessness, in response to things that posed no real danger now.

##For these men, the past wasn't something they remembered. It was something they kept living.##

Van der Kolk tried a simple test. He showed some veterans Rorschach inkblot cards, the ambiguous shapes psychologists use to see how people interpret the world. Most people see all sorts of things in them: animals, faces, landscapes. Many of the veterans saw scenes from the war in the cards. Others saw nothing at all, as if their minds had lost the ability to imagine anything new.

That second group troubled him as much as the first. It suggested that trauma didn't just add painful memories. It could also narrow a person's world, making it hard to be curious, playful or open to the present.

From these early experiences, van der Kolk reached the conclusion that runs through his whole book. Trauma, he argues, is not simply a story about something bad that happened in the past. It leaves an imprint on the mind, the brain and the body, and it can be re-experienced in the present, as though it were happening again. The body, in his phrase, keeps the score.

This way of seeing changed how he understood his patients. Their reactions weren't weakness or stubbornness. They were the body's attempt to protect them from a danger it still believed was there.

[[lessons]]
$$**Trauma can be relived, not just remembered.** Tom and the other veterans weren't simply recalling bad times. Their bodies reacted as if the war were still happening. Van der Kolk's insight was that trauma lives in the present for those who carry it, which is why well-meant advice to "just move on" rarely helps.$$

$$**The body can stay on alert.** Ordinary sounds and situations set off the old alarm long after the danger ended. Understanding that this is a body stuck in protection mode, not a character flaw, allows for more patience, both from others and from the person themselves.$$

$$**Understanding reduces shame.** Seeing reactions as the body's attempt to protect helped van der Kolk treat his patients with more compassion. The same shift can help anyone who supports someone who has been through something terrible: curiosity and patience instead of quick judgment.$$

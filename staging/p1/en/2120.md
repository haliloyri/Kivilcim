[[open]]
In a hospital ward, a newborn is given a vaccine from a medicine spoon, stirred into breast milk. The same thing happens twice more, two days apart. Every step is routine, and the one thing nobody can see in the spoon is what will make the babies ill.

[[story]]
In December 1929 the general hospital in Lübeck, Germany, began a campaign to protect newborns from tuberculosis. Each baby was to receive the BCG vaccine, a live, weakened bacillus developed by Albert Calmette and Camille Guérin, within its first ten days of life. The dose went into breast milk and was given by medicine spoon, three times, about two days apart.

In the spring of 1930 babies started to fall sick. Over roughly four months, 251 of the 412 newborns in the campaign received vaccine that was contaminated with living, fully virulent tuberculosis bacteria. According to a 2016 review of the case in PLoS Pathogens, 228 of those 251 showed clinical or X-ray signs of TB. Seventy-two died of the disease, among 77 who died within a year, and autopsies confirmed TB in every one of the 68 cases in which one was performed.

Investigators had to work out what had gone wrong. A later analysis concluded that the vials held varying amounts of fully virulent *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*, the kind that causes the disease. The source was an uncommon strain, called the Kiel strain, that had been cultivated in the very laboratory where the vaccine was prepared. The possibility that BCG itself had turned dangerous was conclusively ruled out.

The children were followed until 1933. No antibiotic treatment existed yet. The review reports that 156 infants recovered on their own, and that six still had active disease after three years. Accurate details of the case were published in German in 1935, and the authors note that for a long time they were available only in that language.

The case was a major scandal around the world at the time. Yet the review's reading is that the vaccine was not the cause. Its authors draw two conclusions from the babies' fates: newborns showed high resistance to TB overall, and the size of the infecting dose strongly shaped the outcome.

There is a lasting lesson in how the investigators got to the answer, and it applies well beyond medicine. They did not stop at the label on the vial or at the name of a trusted vaccine. They looked at what shared a laboratory with it.

[[lessons]]
$$**When a trusted product fails, check what shares its workspace before you blame the product.** The label tells you what something is supposed to contain, while the surrounding process decides what actually ends up in it. A small bakery that suddenly gets complaints about a trusted recipe should look at the shared mixer, the shared shelves and the shared hands before changing the recipe. Following the process around a good idea saves you from throwing out what works, it lets you fix the real fault, and it tells the people who rely on the product what actually happened to it.$$

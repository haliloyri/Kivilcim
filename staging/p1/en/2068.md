Someone has etched a small fly into the porcelain of a men's room urinal, near the drain. No sign explains it, and nobody posts a warning. The airport's own management says that tiny drawing changed how the room stays clean, and the story behind it starts long before the airport.

[[story]]
Richard Thaler guessed that whoever invented the urinal fly must have been an economist. Aad Kieboom, the deputy director of Amsterdam's Schiphol Airport, corrected him on NPR in 2009: the man was a maintenance worker named Jos van Bedaf. Thaler and Cass Sunstein use the fly as an example of a nudge in *Nudge*.

According to Kieboom, Van Bedaf got the idea as a young man, in the 1960s, during his military service. Someone had made a small dot in a urinal there, and that bathroom, as the story goes, stayed much cleaner. Van Bedaf kept the observation in mind for years and later brought it up at work.

At Schiphol he proposed a fly instead of a dot. The airport adopted the idea and had flies etched into its urinals. Kieboom said spillage on the men's room floor fell by 80 percent.

Why a fly? Kieboom passed on Van Bedaf's own reasoning: a fly is the animal men would like to aim at, and if you hit it well enough, it cannot fly away. Van Bedaf died in the early 1990s, and the idea spread without him. Thaler said on the same broadcast that fly urinals had been spotted in Moscow, in Singapore and at JFK in New York.

The same method works at home. A guest on the program, Julie Power, said she wrote "aim" in big red letters on the back of the toilet bowl for her two boys and drew a circle around it, and that they aimed at the circle. Thaler added that a friend of his claims a Cheerio thrown into the middle of the bowl does the same job.

Nobody orders the men to do anything, and no fine or rule is involved. The fly puts one thing, a point to aim at, exactly where the act takes place. The entomologist May Berenbaum told the same broadcast that the idea was old: late nineteenth-century Victorian urinals in Britain often carried little pictures of bees.

As for the number, the 80 percent comes from what Kieboom said on air and rests on the airport's own account. It was not presented as the result of an independent measurement. The story still shows how much a single small drawing can steer behavior without a ban or a word from anyone.

[[lessons]]
$$**Put a target exactly where the behavior you want takes place.** A workshop foreman who wants tools returned after work can paint the outline of each tool on the pegboard, so the reminder is built into the wall and never has to be written again. People tend to go where the right spot is plainly in front of their eyes, because the correct move no longer takes any thinking. Instead of repeating a rule or hanging up another warning, you can place one small, concrete marker at the point of action and steer people by the setup of the space rather than by words, which spares both you and them a lot of repetition.$$

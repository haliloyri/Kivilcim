At a breakfast table in a research laboratory, a young engineer admits he is stuck on a problem. The older man across from him offers no solution. He asks one question, then another, and somewhere around the second coffee the younger man hears himself say what he should try.

[[story]]
Bell Labs, the research arm of the American telephone system, was full of brilliant people, and its patent department kept a close eye on what they produced. According to Jon Gertner's history *The Idea Factory*, lawyers in that department once wanted to know why some employees were so much more productive than others. They went looking for an organizing principle, something that explained who invented a lot and who did not.

What they found was odd. The only common thread was that the people with the most patents often shared breakfast or lunch with one man, an electrical engineer named Harry Nyquist.

Nyquist was not handing out ideas. Gertner reports a colleague describing what he did: "he drew people out, got them thinking." His real skill, in other words, was asking good questions. He did not supply the answers. He made it easier for the other person to find them.

David Brooks tells this story in an interview with the Templeton Foundation. He calls Nyquist an illuminator, someone who lights up the people around him, and says that schooling and intelligence did not explain the success of those who ate with him. Gertner's account stops at the patent counts and the meals. What caused what, whether the meals made people productive or productive people simply sought him out, no one measured.

Consider what a question does that advice cannot. Advice ends a conversation: the listener nods and leaves with somebody else's idea. A question keeps the problem in the other person's hands. It forces half-formed thoughts into words, and putting them into words often exposes the gap that was invisible a minute before. By Gertner's account, Nyquist left the thinking to the people across the table, and that may be exactly why they went back to him.

Still, the pattern is worth sitting with. A laboratory spends enormous effort on equipment, budgets and hiring brilliant minds. Yet the best predictor the lawyers could find was none of those. It was a person with a habit of paying attention, and a table where others could think out loud.

There is a quiet lesson about where good work comes from. We tend to imagine an inventor alone with a notebook. The record from Bell Labs suggests something more social: ideas often ripen in the space between two people, when one of them listens closely enough to ask the question that the other had not yet thought to ask.

[[lessons]]
$$**Be the person who asks the next good question, because it can unlock work you could never do for someone else.** When a colleague describes a stuck project, the urge is to jump in with advice or a fix. Ask instead, "What have you already tried?" or "What would you do if it could not fail?", and then stay quiet long enough to hear the answer, even if the silence feels a little long. People who feel listened to think better, and sometimes the idea you were about to offer turns out to be one they already had, which is the best outcome for both of you.$$

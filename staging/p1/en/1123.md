[[open]]
Picture the 200-meter butterfly final at the Beijing Olympics. The best swimmer in the world dives in, and soon his goggles start filling with water. The lane lines blur. The wall he needs to find turns into a smear of blue. The whole world is watching, and he can barely see. What could keep anyone calm in that moment? The answer started long before Beijing, with a swimmer lying in bed with his eyes closed.

[[story]]
Charles Duhigg tells this story in *The Power of Habit*. It isn't really about talent. Michael Phelps had plenty of that, and Duhigg is clear that his body gave him real advantages in the water. But his coach, Bob Bowman, wasn't satisfied with drilling physical technique. He set out on purpose to build Phelps a set of small routines, habits and mental patterns that would prepare him to race long before he got anywhere near the starting block.

The most important one sounded odd. Every night before sleep, and every morning after waking, Bowman told Phelps to do one thing:

> "Watch the videotape."

There was no actual tape. Bowman meant a race played in Phelps's own head, in vivid detail. The dive, each stroke, the turns, the final touch on the wall. Phelps ran that perfect race over and over, night and morning, until he knew it well.

Phelps didn't only picture the perfect version, though. He also rehearsed things going wrong. What if something broke mid-race? What would he do? By the time a setback showed up in real life, he would already have a plan for it.

Race days followed the same logic. Phelps went through a fixed sequence of stretching, warm-up laps and music, the same way every time. Then, just before the start, Bowman would give the cue:

> "Put in the videotape."

The race stopped being a new, frightening event and became the next run of a plan Phelps had already carried out many times.

Then came Beijing, 2008, and the final of the 200-meter butterfly. Phelps dove in, and his goggles began filling with water. As the race went on, he could see less and less. He couldn't clearly see the wall, the lane markings or his rivals.

This is where most people would panic. You're in an Olympic final, you're effectively swimming blind, and the moment you've trained years for is falling apart.

Phelps didn't panic. ##He started counting his strokes, because he already knew how many each length required.## He didn't need to see the wall. He knew roughly where it would be, because he had swum this race so many times in his mind. He kept swimming, counted, and reached for the wall when the count told him to.

He touched first. He won the gold medal and set a world record while barely able to see.

Afterward, Phelps said the swim had felt the way he had imagined it. A race that should have been a disaster had gone according to the tape.

[[lessons]]
$$**Rehearsal turns pressure into a replay.** Bowman's "watch the videotape" routine, every night and every morning, meant Phelps had swum the Olympic final many times before he ever reached Beijing. When the real race came, his mind wasn't meeting something new. It was running a script it already knew well, and that is why the race could feel "the way he had imagined it."$$

$$**Rehearse the failure, not just the win.** Phelps didn't only picture perfect races. He pictured things going wrong. So when his goggles flooded, he didn't have to invent a plan while going under. He already had one: count the strokes, trust the numbers, reach for the wall. Panic fills the space where a plan should be, and Phelps had filled that space in advance.$$

$$**Fixed routines save willpower for the moment that matters.** Stretching, warm-up laps, music, then "put in the videotape." Because the race-day sequence never changed, Phelps didn't have to decide anything before the start. He was on autopilot, which left his full attention free for the race itself, including the part where his goggles filled with water.$$

[[reflect]]
- Think of a high-stakes moment coming up in the next few weeks. Are you preparing for it, or just hoping it goes well?
- In that moment, what is your version of the goggles filling with water? Name the single most likely thing to go wrong.
- Decide your first calm move if it happens. Make it as simple as counting strokes.
- Notice what you do right before pressure moments. Is it the same every time, or do you improvise while your nerves climb?

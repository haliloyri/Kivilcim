A wound in a man's back, a dozen doctors, and not one sterilized finger or instrument among them. The bullet itself had missed the spine and every vital organ. What followed lasted most of a summer, and it was the care, more than the shot, that decided it.

[[story]]
Candice Millard's *Destiny of the Republic* makes a hard claim about President James Garfield, who was shot on 2 July 1881 at a Washington railway station. In an interview she gave in 2011, she said that the bullet had missed his spinal cord and vital organs, and that infection caused by his medical care killed him. Reporting on her work adds that he would almost certainly have survived if the wound had been left alone.

Sixteen years earlier, the British surgeon Joseph Lister had developed antisepsis, a way of keeping wounds and instruments clean with carbolic acid. He urged doctors to sterilize their hands and instruments. Many prominent American doctors dismissed his methods. According to Jeffrey Reznick of the National Library of Medicine, they largely held to the miasma theory, the belief that bad air caused disease, rather than to germ theory.

Dr. D. Willard Bliss was summoned by Robert Todd Lincoln and took charge of the case. Millard describes him as uniquely arrogant and ambitious. He publicly insisted that the bullet lay on the right side of the president's body.

Within minutes of the shooting, about a dozen doctors had searched the wound with unsterilized fingers and instruments. Garfield developed abscesses and infection throughout his body, and over the weeks he went from about 210 pounds to about 130 in less than three months.

Garfield died on 19 September, after about eighty days. The autopsy showed that Bliss had been wrong about the bullet's position, and Millard says Bliss was publicly disgraced afterward. Reznick's judgment is that Garfield died because of what his doctors did and what they failed to do. Reports add that American doctors soon adopted antiseptic methods.

The mechanism is worth following step by step. Lister's precaution asked for little: clean hands, clean instruments and some acid. The doctors in the room were many, the physician in charge was certain, and a bullet that stays hidden invites one more probe. Every extra probe was one more chance for infection, so the search itself kept making the problem it was trying to solve. Certainty about where the bullet lay and certainty about the method fed each other, and nobody in the room had a rule that said: stop, and do the cheap safe thing first.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before a step you cannot undo, ask what the cheapest safeguard would be, and judge who proposed it only afterward.** A safeguard that costs a few minutes deserves a trial even when it comes from a rival, a newcomer or a foreigner. When someone senior sounds sure, certainty is a mood and not evidence, so ask what would show them wrong. Build the pause into the process, such as a checklist before a deployment or a second read before an irreversible message, because in the moment the loudest person will rarely volunteer to slow down, and the quiet person with the cheap idea is easily talked over.$$

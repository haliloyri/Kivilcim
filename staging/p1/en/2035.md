In one ledger a single line changes and another does not. A fund hands back about 2.7 billion dollars to its investors, and its capital shrinks by more than a third. The positions on the other side of the ledger stay exactly where they were. The same bets now rest on a much thinner cushion.

[[story]]
Twenty-eight to one does not sound shocking if you set it beside the big Wall Street firms. According to a table in the GAO's report on the LTCM episode, simple balance-sheet leverage, assets divided by equity capital, was 34 to 1 at Goldman Sachs, 30 to 1 at Merrill Lynch, 28 to 1 at Lehman Brothers and 22 to 1 at Morgan Stanley Dean Witter. LTCM's figure at the end of 1997 was 28 to 1. By that measure it looked like one of the pack.

The picture changes once you look at what sat behind the ratio. At the end of 1997 LTCM had 129 billion dollars in total assets, and the President's Working Group report says it was significantly larger than any other reporting hedge fund family. It also held over-the-counter derivatives with a notional value of 1.3 trillion dollars, outside the balance sheet. The report adds that the fund was the most highly leveraged large hedge fund reporting to the CFTC, and that its assets of more than 125 billion dollars in August 1998 were nearly four times those of the next largest.

Then comes the decision at the heart of this story. LTCM's net returns had been about 40 percent in 1995 and 1996 and a little under 20 percent in 1997. At the end of 1997 it returned about 2.7 billion dollars to its investors. That cut the fund's capital by roughly 36 percent, to 4.8 billion. According to the report, the managers did not shrink the positions; they raised the leverage by reducing the capital.

Roger Lowenstein's *When Genius Failed* is about this fund, and the interest of the decision lies in its timing. It came after strong years, when handing money back looked tidy and the returns behind it were strong. But each returned dollar left the same positions standing on a slimmer base. Even on the smaller 4.8 billion, the August 1998 balance sheet implied leverage above 25 to 1.

By 31 July 1998 the capital stood at 4.1 billion dollars. In August it lost 1.8 billion more. The cost of the thin cushion became visible only on the bad day, because the same shock hits a smaller base as a much bigger percentage.

[[lessons]]
$$**After a good year, work out what a bad day would cost before you shrink your cushion.** Success makes spare money, time or capacity feel wasteful, yet a cushion earns its keep only when something goes wrong. In your team, your household budget or your own calendar, ask how easily you could rebuild the margin you gave up, and how long that would take after a hit. Write down the figure for the bad scenario first, check how much of it you could absorb, and only then decide whether to give the slack away. A cushion you can afford to lose is the only kind worth releasing.$$

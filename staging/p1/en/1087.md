[[open]]
Picture two companies selling the exact same computer. One tells you, "We make great computers." The other tells you it believes in challenging the status quo. Same machine, same shelf. According to Simon Sinek, the only real difference is the order in which they say things. He built a famous idea on that difference, and then propped it up with a piece of brain science that may not hold up.

[[story]]
In *Start With Why*, published in 2009, Simon Sinek draws three circles, one inside the other. He calls it the Golden Circle. At the center sits **Why**, the purpose or belief behind what you do. Around it sits **How**, the way you go about it. On the outside sits **What**, the thing you actually make or sell.

His first observation is that most organizations talk from the outside in. They open with what they make, then explain how it's better, and they rarely get around to saying why they exist at all.

His signature example is Apple. In Sinek's telling, Apple's message runs in the opposite direction. It opens with a belief:

> We believe in challenging the status quo. We believe in thinking differently.

Then it names how that belief shows up: beautifully designed products that are easy to use. Only at the very end does it mention, almost in passing, that the company happens to make computers.

Now set that beside a hypothetical ordinary computer company:

> We make great computers.

That sentence isn't false, and it isn't bad. But it hands the listener a job, which is to compare. Great compared to what? Which features, at what price? The conversation shrinks to a spec sheet and a price tag.

From this contrast Sinek draws the line the book is known for:

##People don't buy what you do; they buy why you do it.##

He could have stopped there and let the examples carry the argument. Instead he went further and grounded the model in biology. The Why and the How, he says, correspond to the limbic brain, which he describes as the part that governs feelings, trust and decisions but has no capacity for language. The What corresponds to the neocortex, the seat of rational, analytical thought and language. His conclusion is that people decide on feeling and then rationalize the decision afterward. So if you start with Why, you're speaking directly to the part of the brain that makes the call.

That's a tidy picture, and it's easy to see why it's persuasive. The three circles seem to line up neatly with the brain's own layers.

But critics outside the book have pushed back on this part, and hard. They call the brain account oversimplified. The idea of a sharply bounded "limbic system" is considered dated. The neocortex isn't a uniquely human feature, because other mammals have one too. And decisions don't come out of a separate emotion zone and a separate logic zone. They involve networks spread widely across the brain.

That leaves the story with an awkward tension. Many people find the Golden Circle genuinely useful for thinking about how they communicate. Yet the explanation offered for *why* it works is the very part experts consider outdated. So does the idea fall with its justification, or can it stand on other ground?

One way to test it is to set the neuroscience aside and look only at what can be observed. Belief-led organizations tend to build loyal followings. Feature-led ones tend to get pulled into competing on price. That pattern comes from marketing and organizational behavior, not from brain anatomy, and it's enough to keep the idea standing.

[[lessons]]
$$**A What invites comparison; a Why invites allegiance.** "We make great computers" asks the listener to line you up against rivals on features and price. Apple's belief-first message asks something else entirely: whether the listener shares that view of the world. The product is identical, but the question in the listener's head changes. When you lead with purpose, you shift what people are judging you on.$$

$$**The most scientific-looking part can be the weakest.** The limbic brain and neocortex mapping is what makes the Golden Circle sound rigorous, and it's the piece critics reject. The idea of a bounded limbic system is dated, other mammals have neocortex, and decisions run through distributed networks. Sounding like science and being settled science are different things.$$

$$**A borrowed explanation makes a good idea fragile.** The communication insight has its own evidence: belief-led organizations building loyal followings while feature-led ones slide into price wars. Tying it to brain anatomy doesn't add strength. It adds a weak point, because when someone questions the brain story, the whole Golden Circle gets questioned with it. Rest an idea on what you've actually observed.$$

[[reflect]]
- When you explain your work, look at the order of your sentences. Does "what I do" come before "why I do it"?
- Think of a time you defended a good idea with a shaky reason, and the shaky reason became the thing people attacked.
- Pick a conviction you hold firmly. Remove its most impressive-sounding argument. Is anything still holding it up?
- Notice when you reach for "the brain works like this" to win a point, and ask what you've actually seen instead.

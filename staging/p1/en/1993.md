[[story]]
Charles Duhigg opens *Supercommunicators* (2024) with Felix Sigala, an FBI negotiator who spent two decades at the Bureau. He joined after college and military service, worked a few years as a field agent, and then took on the jobs that needed a gentle touch: reluctant witnesses, fugitives who might surrender, grieving families. The snake case was one of his. When the FBI's Crisis Negotiation Unit, which handles hostage situations, ran into something unusually tangled, it called people like him.

In 2014 the US Department of Defense commissioned psychologists, sociologists and other researchers to find better ways of teaching persuasion and negotiation to military officers. Officials kept naming Sigala as the best negotiator they had ever worked with, so the researchers sought him out.

He did not look the part. He looked like a middle-aged dad, with a mustache, a little padding and a soft, slightly nasal voice. Asked how he thought about communication, he offered to show them instead, and asked one scientist for a favorite memory. The scientist ran a large lab with millions of dollars in grants and dozens of staff. He told Sigala about his daughter's wedding, and about his mother, who died a few months later. Sigala answered with a story of his own: his sister had married in 2010 and later died of cancer.

The exchange went on for forty-five minutes. Sigala asked follow-up questions and matched each disclosure with one of his own. When another scientist described trouble with a teenage daughter, he talked about an aunt he had never managed to get along with. He told the group he had been painfully shy as a child, that his father was a salesman and his grandfather a con man, and that he had learned to connect by imitating them.

Then a psychology professor, a single mother, asked why so many people had recommended him. Sigala asked her what she would tell someone going through a divorce. She started describing her own separation. He stopped her gently and pointed out what had just happened: she had shared an intimate part of her life with colleagues after less than an hour in their company.

His explanation was ordinary. Listening closely, asking probing questions and answering emotion with emotion builds trust. It is a set of skills, he told them, and there is nothing magical about it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Answer a personal question with a personal answer of your own.** Suppose a new colleague mentions she is nervous about moving house, and you reply with a tip about removal companies and nothing else. Sharing a small, true piece of your own life in return tells her the exchange goes both ways, and that is usually what makes people lean in. You can practice it in any ordinary talk: ask one follow-up question, listen to the whole answer, and then offer one honest detail of your own before you move on to business. Trust of this kind rarely comes from a speech about yourself, and it rarely comes from questions alone.$$

A man in his early thirties sits down to write to the ruler of a city he has barely seen. He offers bridges that can be carried and set up fast, covered vehicles, cannon, and tunnels dug without a sound. The craft he actually trained in comes last, almost as an afterthought.

[[story]]
Walter Isaacson's biography *Leonardo da Vinci* follows one life across decades, and this story is about a single page of it. Around 1482 Leonardo, then about thirty, left Florence for Milan and wrote to Ludovico Sforza, who held power there. The page was a job application.

He did not lead with painting. The text survives as a copy in the Codex Atlanticus, kept at the Ambrosiana library in Milan, and it is laid out as a list of about ten offers, most of them military. Light, strong bridges that an army can carry. Ways to flood a ditch or to cut a tunnel without being heard. Covered vehicles, cannon and other machines for a siege.

Only near the end does the letter turn to peacetime. There Leonardo says that in architecture, sculpture and painting he can do as well as anyone. Published accounts add one detail: the surviving copy is a draft written by someone from his circle, not in his own hand.

The order of the list is the interesting part. A ruler who worries about war reads a page of siege machines differently from a page of pictures. Leonardo seems to have known who was reading, and he put first the offers that reader could use soonest.

It worked. He was taken on and stayed in Milan for roughly seventeen years, and those are the years in which he painted the Last Supper. The first thing he sold Sforza differed from what history later remembered him for.

A job letter is a small act of translation. The sender has many abilities, the reader has one pressing problem, and the letter has to connect the two in the first lines. Leonardo's list does that: each item answers a question a ruler might be asking, and each one is stated plainly enough to be judged.

Notice what he left out: no philosophy, no defence of his talent. He wrote down what he could build and left the reader to imagine it working.

A list of offers also asks to be believed, and a letter cannot prove much. That is why the items are so specific: a bridge that can be carried, a tunnel that makes no noise. A specific claim can be tested, and a ruler could ask to see it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Open an offer with the problem the other person is trying to solve.** When you pitch for a job, a budget or a project, the listener is scanning for relief from their own worries, not for a tour of your strengths. Leonardo put the skills a ruler could use at once at the top of the page and placed painting, sculpture and architecture at the end. Before your next pitch, write down the one worry your listener has this week and check that your first sentence speaks to it. Your best work still gets its turn once you have been invited to the table.$$

A chord sounds, and a three-year-old says which one it is. The same thing happens the next day, and the day after that, every day for nineteen months. It does not look like a gift on display. It looks like a lesson, repeated until something changes.

[[story]]
Absolute pitch is the ability to name a note the moment you hear it, with no reference tone to lean on. It is rare, and for a long time it has been treated as a gift: either you have it or you do not. That belief has a practical consequence. If an ability is thought to be fixed at birth, nobody tries to teach it.

The Japanese researcher Ayako Sakakibara tested that belief with a training programme. Her study, published in 2014 in the journal *Psychology of Music*, followed 24 children between the ages of two and six, none of whom had absolute pitch when they began. They were trained with the Chord Identification Method developed by Eguchi in 1991, learning to identify chords by ear. An earlier report by Sakakibara, in a Japanese journal, had followed a single three-year-old who trained every day for 19 months on nine kinds of chords.

All the children who stayed in the programme acquired absolute pitch. Two stopped training before the end and are outside that result.

The most interesting part lies in the mistakes. Sakakibara found that the children's errors changed character during training. Early on they seemed to judge chords by tone height, how high or low the sound was. Later they began, little by little, to attend to chroma, the quality that makes a C sound like a C whether it is played high or low. The errors suggested a shift from one strategy to the other, and that shift appears to be part of how the ability arrived.

Seen that way, a wrong answer is information. It shows what the child was listening to at that moment, and a change in the pattern of wrong answers shows the listening itself changing, long before the answers turn right.

The limits are plain. This is a study of young children in a structured programme, and it shows what training can do at that age, not what every adult would achieve. Other researchers who studied music-school children have warned that sampling and dropout make it risky to conclude that anyone can learn absolute pitch. What the 2014 report does is move the question. Instead of asking who has the gift, it asks what practice looks like when the thing being practised is a sharper and sharper distinction.

[[lessons]]
$$**Read your mistakes for the strategy behind them.** When Sakakibara's children got a chord wrong, the pattern of wrong answers showed what they were listening to, first height and later chroma. A person learning to give presentations can write down after each talk what they leaned on, for instance reading from the slides, even when the talk went fine, and then watch that habit change as the weeks pass. Mistakes that change character suggest you are moving, and mistakes that repeat in exactly the same way suggest it is time to try another approach, such as speaking from a few notes.$$

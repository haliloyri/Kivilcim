In an operating theatre, one person holds the knife. Around the table stand people who never cut anything: someone to put the patient under, someone to hand over instruments, someone to keep the records. Nobody suggests that the surgeon should vote with them on where to make the incision.

[[story]]
Fred Brooks put that picture into *The Mythical Man-Month*, and he credited the idea to Harlan Mills, who had proposed it in 1971. Mills described a programming team organised in a star around one person, the chief programmer. The design resembles a surgical team, in which a surgeon is supported by an anaesthetist, nurses and others.

The chief programmer was the engineer who understood best what the system was meant to do. This person did the core technical work together with a backup programmer, who could take it over if needed. Everyone else existed to support those two, and none of them was expected to produce the central design. A program clerk kept all the technical records of the project. A toolsmith built and maintained the tools the developers used. A language lawyer knew the programming language in depth, the way a specialist knows one corner of the law.

The backup programmer deserves a second look. With two people who both know the core work, the project does not hang on one person's illness or holiday. The chief still decides, but the work is never held in a single head with no copy.

Look at what the arrangement removes from the chief programmer's desk. Filing, tool repair and disputes about the finer points of the language all belong to someone else. The person who is designing the system can stay inside the system. Each question that reaches the chief is one that only the chief can answer, and the clerk, the toolsmith and the lawyer take the rest.

Brooks also argued that good programmers are five to ten times as productive as mediocre ones. If that is true, the surgical structure puts the scarce talent where it counts: with the one person who decides, and not spread thinly across a committee of equals.

The structure also fits the arithmetic of his best-known law. The more people have to agree on every decision, the more conversations there are, and each of them takes time away from the work. In a surgical team, most of those conversations never have to happen, because the structure has already settled who decides and who supports. The surgeon does not put the incision to a vote, and nobody in that room expects a vote.

[[lessons]]
$$**Give each piece of important work one owner, and let the others serve that owner.** A family planning a long journey will often get a better trip if one person books the route while another handles the paperwork and a third looks after snacks and maps, instead of everyone voting on every stop. The owner can then keep the whole plan in one head, and the helpers know exactly which questions they may settle without asking. You spend less time negotiating and more time on the thing that matters, which is usually the part only one person can do well.$$

A man is being hurt, and he knows exactly what's coming. His response isn't a scream or a plea. It's a calm, almost dry warning. When the worst happens, he simply says: didn't I tell you? The man was Epictetus, and he had no legal freedom at all.

[[story]]
Epictetus was born into slavery in the first century AD, in the Roman Empire. He was lame for much of his life. He owned nothing and controlled almost nothing about his own circumstances. And yet he became one of the most influential teachers of Stoic philosophy, and his ideas are still read today. Ryan Holiday returns to him again and again in *Stillness Is the Key* as an example of a calm, free mind.

There's an ancient story about him. It comes down to us through the early Christian writer Origen, who was quoting a philosopher named Celsus. According to the story, Epictetus's master once began twisting his leg, perhaps out of cruelty or boredom. Epictetus calmly told him that if he kept going, the leg would break. The master continued. The leg broke.

> Didn't I tell you it would break?

That was all Epictetus said.

##Whether or not it happened exactly that way, the story captures something real about his teaching.##

Epictetus taught that the most important distinction in life is between what is up to us and what is not. His handbook, the *Enchiridion*, opens with it. Our judgments, our choices, our desires and our responses are up to us. Our body, our reputation, our property, other people's actions and the circumstances we're born into are not.

Most suffering, he argued, comes from mixing these up. We pour energy and anguish into things we cannot control, and neglect the one area where we have real power: how we interpret and respond to what happens.

For an enslaved man, this wasn't an abstract idea. Almost everything external in his life was controlled by someone else. His freedom, if he had any, had to be found inside. And he claimed that it could be. No one, he said, could force him to judge something as terrible, or to lose his composure, unless he agreed.

Holiday sees this as the heart of stillness. It isn't about having a peaceful life with nothing going wrong. Epictetus's life was full of hardship. It's about keeping a steady mind by focusing on what's truly yours: your own thoughts and actions. Everything else, you do your best with and then accept.

The result is a kind of freedom that doesn't depend on circumstances, which is why a man with no freedom could teach it so well.

[[lessons]]
$$**Separate what's up to you from what isn't.** Epictetus's handbook opens with this distinction for a reason. Our judgments and actions are ours; outside events and other people aren't. Sorting a worry into those two piles is simple, and it immediately shows you where effort is useful and where it's only adding pain.$$

$$**Inner freedom doesn't wait for outer freedom.** Epictetus was enslaved and physically disabled, yet he taught freedom. His point wasn't that circumstances don't matter. It was that even in the worst circumstances, your response remains yours. That makes steadiness possible in situations you can't change.$$

$$**Calm is about judgment, not the absence of pain.** Whether or not the leg story happened exactly as told, it shows calm in the middle of real suffering. For the Stoics, the pain itself wasn't the enemy. Losing yourself in panic and resentment on top of it was, and that part was under your control.$$

[[reflect]]
- What is worrying you right now that isn't actually up to you?
- Within that worry, what part is up to you?
- When did you last stay steady in a situation you couldn't change, and how did you manage it?

&&If you focused only on your own judgments and actions today, what would you let go of?&&

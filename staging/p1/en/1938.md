[[story]]
Marquet recalls in a podcast interview that he once sat through a briefing: one person talking and twenty people listening. He decided it was a poor use of twenty people. On USS Santa Fe he cancelled the briefings, and the rule that replaced them became a line in *Turn the Ship Around!*: don't brief, certify.

In a certification, each person has to explain his own role and the critical decisions that fall to him. Marquet gives the sound of it: "Okay, John, what's your position? What are you gonna be doing?" A team leader questions the members, and then decides whether the team is ready to go ahead.

The difference lies in who has to speak. In a briefing, a listener can nod through anything. In a certification, the one who does not know his part has to say so out loud, while there is still time to fix it.

The same attitude shaped how Santa Fe treated visiting inspectors. Squadron staff came aboard for inspections, and Marquet let the crew show them the ship's problems instead of hiding them. One inspector's reaction, as Marquet remembers it: "I think you guys are crazy. They're showing me their problems."

Marquet reports that the ship improved faster than any other. It often earned grades of B-plus or A-minus, and in the final reports it was given the benefit of the doubt. Published records list, for 2000, the Arleigh Burke Fleet Trophy for the most improved ship in the fleet.

A talk he gave at the Naval Postgraduate School in 2013 adds a detail. Visiting inspectors found that he had eliminated briefings, and that his officers kept no command log and no "tickler" file. Marquet argued that this built pride and a sense of personal responsibility. He also put it this way: your legacy is the grade your people achieve in your absence.

The method costs time, since an hour of real questions takes longer than ten minutes of slides, and it only works if the questions are real. An inspection that finds a problem the crew has already named is also a different conversation from one that uncovers a hidden one.

Think about what the crew learned from those weeks. Telling the truth about a weak spot did not get them punished. It got them better grades, and a reputation for being straight with the people who checked them.

[[lessons]]
$$**A test someone takes on their own shows more than a talk they sit through.** If you onboard a new colleague by walking them through slides, you learn only that they were present, while asking them to run the first five minutes of a client call aloud shows you exactly what is missing. It also tells them it is safe to say "I don't know that part yet", because the point is to find the gap early. Use it before work that is costly to redo, and let the person who will do the work be the one who explains it.$$

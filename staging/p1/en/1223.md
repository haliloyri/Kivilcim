People who met Bill Clinton, supporters and critics alike, often described the same experience. When he talked to you, they said, he made you feel like the only person in the room. It wasn't what he said that stayed with them. It was how completely he seemed to be there.

[[story]]
In *The Charisma Myth*, Olivia Fox Cabane sets out to explain what charisma is made of. She breaks it down into three elements.

The first is **power**: the sense that a person can affect the world around them, through influence, expertise, status or strength. The second is **warmth**: the sense that they will use that power to benefit us, that they care and have goodwill toward us. Charismatic people, she argues, tend to project both.

But underneath both sits a third element, which she treats as the foundation: **presence**.

Presence means being fully in the moment with the person in front of you. Not half-listening while thinking about your next meeting. Not planning your reply. Not glancing at your phone. Just there.

##Without presence, Cabane argues, power and warmth don't come across, however hard you try.##

This is where the Clinton stories come in. What people described wasn't a clever technique. It was attention. For those few minutes, it felt as though he cared about nothing but them. That impression, Cabane suggests, is a large part of what people experience as charisma.

The difficult truth is that most of us are much less present than we think. Our minds constantly drift to worries, plans and distractions. And Cabane argues that people can tell. When your attention wanders, it shows in small ways: your eyes glaze slightly, your facial expressions lag a fraction behind what the other person is saying. Most people wouldn't be able to describe what they noticed, but they sense it. They feel that you're not really with them, and they trust you a little less.

So how do you become more present? Cabane offers a surprisingly physical answer. When you notice your mind drifting, bring your attention to a bodily sensation for a moment: your breathing, or even the feeling of your toes inside your shoes. It sounds odd, but the body only exists in the present. Focusing on a physical sensation pulls your attention out of the past and future and back into the here and now. From there, you can return your full attention to the person in front of you.

It's a small practice. But Cabane argues that repeated over time, it can transform how people experience you.

[[lessons]]
$$**Presence is the foundation.** Power and warmth matter, but Cabane argues they only come across when you're actually present. The people remembered as most charismatic are often those who made others feel fully seen. Attention, not eloquence, is the base everything else rests on.$$

$$**People notice when you drift.** Glazed eyes and slightly delayed reactions give you away, even if the other person can't say exactly what they noticed. They just feel less connected. That's why multitasking in conversations costs more than it seems: it quietly signals that someone isn't your priority.$$

$$**Your body can bring you back.** Focusing briefly on your breath or your toes pulls attention into the present, because the body only exists now. It's a simple, private tool you can use in any conversation to return from your own thoughts to the person in front of you.$$

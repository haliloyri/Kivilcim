You've probably done it. You knelt on a drop cloth, wedged a screwdriver under the rim of a paint can, and levered until the lid finally gave. Then came the drips down the side and the fight to get it shut again. The strange part is that this went on for decades, and almost nobody asked why. Seth Godin did, in *Purple Cow*, because one company finally did something about it.

[[story]]
Seth Godin built *Purple Cow*, published in 2003, around one blunt claim: a boring product can't be rescued by advertising. If you want people to notice, the remarkable thing has to be the product itself. The jingle, the slogan and the media buy can't do that job.

To make the point, he picks one of the least glamorous objects in any hardware store: the paint can.

For decades, house paint came in round metal cans. Anyone who has painted a room knows the routine. The can was awkward to carry. You needed a tool just to pry it open. Pouring was messy, and resealing it was its own small chore. Nobody liked any of this. But nobody seemed to think it could be different either. The attitude was roughly:

> A paint can is a paint can.

That attitude is the tension at the heart of this story. The opportunity wasn't hidden. Millions of people ran into it every time they painted. It had simply become normal. It was so familiar that people stopped treating it as a problem and started treating it as a fact of life.

Dutch Boy looked at that fact of life and decided it wasn't one. What they chose to change matters. They left the paint and the ad campaign alone and changed the container.

They launched Twist & Pour, a plastic container with a real handle, a lid you twist off and a spout for pouring. There was no screwdriver, no prying and no fighting the lid back on. The redesign went straight at the exact annoyances everyone had been quietly putting up with.

##The change in the product itself did the marketing work.##

Godin reports that the new container brought increased sales, along with better distribution and more support from retailers. Nobody had to be persuaded that twisting beats prying. The benefit was obvious the moment you held the thing.

This is why the case matters so much to Godin's argument. It shows his claim that remarkability has to be designed into the product from the start. You can't add it afterward as a layer of marketing. Dutch Boy didn't find a cleverer way to describe a metal can. They made something that explained itself the moment you picked it up.

Godin also writes about people he calls "otaku." He borrows the Japanese term for a small group who are passionate about a topic, go looking for new things and spread the word. Remarkable products reach these people first, and they carry the news outward. That makes the paint can example sharper still. Nobody is a paint-can enthusiast, and there's no fan club for containers. But someone who paints a lot notices a lid that opens without a screwdriver, and they tell a friend. Even in a category this dull, a product that fixes an annoyance people actually feel gives them something to talk about.

That's the whole story. There was no breakthrough chemistry and no famous campaign. A company simply stopped accepting a hassle everyone else had agreed to live with.

[[lessons]]
$$**The remarkable part lives inside the product.** Dutch Boy changed the container, not the slogan. A handle, a twist-off lid and a spout are features you notice in your hands, not claims you read on a billboard. Godin's point is that no amount of advertising could have made the old metal can interesting. The only way to make it worth talking about was to rebuild it.$$

$$**If marketing only describes, it can only shout.** In many companies, one team makes the product and another team is told to sell it. When marketing's job is defined as "describe what we already have," the only remaining move is to describe it louder, more often and more expensively. Dutch Boy's win came from treating the product itself as the marketing decision.$$

$$**The biggest openings hide in complaints nobody voices anymore.** Everyone disliked the metal paint can, yet no one questioned it, because "a paint can is a paint can." The annoyance was so universal that it had stopped sounding like a complaint at all. Opportunities like that aren't secret. They're sitting in plain view, disguised as the way things have always been.$$

[[reflect]]
- Think of the product, service or process you work on. What does everyone dislike about it but never question?
- When your team wants more attention for something, is the first instinct to describe it better or to change it?
- Recall the last time you told a friend about a product. Was it an ad that moved you, or a small annoyance it quietly removed?
- Which "screwdriver moment" do the people you serve go through, and simply accept, every time they deal with you?

&&What do your customers or users complain about that you've come to treat as normal?&&

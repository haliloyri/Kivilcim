On the third floor of a tower, above a chapel and a bedroom, a man turns over one book after another and dictates whatever comes into his head. One of the things he puts down is an awkward story about a friend. This friend is a man nobody could accuse of weakness, and he failed at the one moment he most wanted not to.

[[story]]
The man in the tower is Michel de Montaigne, and he describes the room himself in his essays: his library, where he spent most of his days and most of the hours in them. In one early essay, on the force of imagination, he says he is among those most sensitive to it. Everyone is jostled by imagination, he writes, and some people are knocked flat. Another person's cough tickles his own throat, and he would rather not visit the sick he loves.

Alain de Botton gives Montaigne the chapter on inadequacy in *The Consolations of Philosophy*. Reviews in Kirkus and Publishers Weekly add that de Botton confesses in the book to a bout of impotence of his own, and that Montaigne's writing helped him through it. Montaigne himself wrote about that same trouble, and plainly.

Montaigne tells the story of a friend whom he says he can vouch for as for himself, a man who could not be suspected of any insufficiency or of being bewitched. The friend heard a companion tell of an unusual failure at a very unfortunate moment. When he found himself in the same situation, the horror of that story took hold of his imagination, and he suffered the same fate. From then on, the memory of the disaster kept returning and tyrannising him, and he relapsed again and again.

Then he found a remedy. Before an encounter, he would frankly confess his susceptibility to the person he was with. That eased the agitation in his mind. Once the other person knew a slip was possible, a slip was expected, and the pressure on him loosened.

Montaigne reports the whole thing in a matter-of-fact way, as something he knows from experience. The friend's body was fine and his character beyond question. What had gone wrong was a picture in his head, borrowed from a stranger's story, and the failure was fed by his fear of failing again.

What worked was not strength of will. It was the opposite: an admission out loud, before the moment arrived, that turned a secret dread into a shared fact. Montaigne put the account into a book that he published for readers to see, which is its own way of agreeing with his friend.

[[lessons]]
$$**Tell the person involved what you fear will go wrong before the moment arrives.** A presenter who opens with "I'm nervous, so bear with me if I lose my place" often finds the nerves ease, because the thing he was hiding is now out in the open. A secret dread keeps all its weight, since every sign of it feels like exposure, while a spoken one costs only a moment of awkwardness. Say it early, in one plain sentence, to the one person who actually needs to know, and notice how much of the pressure belonged to the hiding rather than to the fear itself.$$

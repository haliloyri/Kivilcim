In the weeks before a US president flies to Beijing, a group of people is handed a list of things that might happen on the trip. Next to each item, they write how likely they think it is. Months later, the same people are asked to look back and say what number they had written down.

[[story]]
Nassim Taleb's *Fooled by Randomness* keeps returning to one habit: once something has happened, it starts to look as if it had to happen. This story is about the experiment that gave that habit its first careful measurement. In 1975 the psychologists Baruch Fischhoff and Ruth Beyth published it under the title "I knew it would happen," and it used a real event, President Nixon's trips to China and the Soviet Union in 1972.

Before the trips, participants were given possible outcomes and asked to estimate the probability of each. For China, one outcome was that Nixon would meet Mao. For the Soviet Union, one was that he would visit Lenin's tomb. Each person wrote down numbers for a list of such events.

Then the trips happened, Beijing in February and Moscow in May of 1972, and the world moved on to other news. Between two weeks and six months afterwards, the researchers went back to the participants. They asked two things: what probability had you given each event, and do you think it occurred?

The recalled numbers had moved. For events that people believed had occurred, they remembered having given a higher probability than they actually had. For events they believed had not occurred, they remembered a lower one. In effect, they gave their earlier selves credit for foresight. And they were confident that they were reporting their earlier judgments accurately.

Fischhoff called this pattern creeping determinism. Notice what the shift followed: not what had objectively happened, but what each person believed had happened. The numbers written before the trips are what make the change visible, because the later memory could be set beside the earlier record. Without that record, each recollection would simply have felt true, with no signal that the outcome had edited it. Later work has repeated the effect many times, and reviews of that work confirm it.

The effect has a cost beyond embarrassment. If you remember being more certain than you were, you cannot learn how well you actually predict. A forecast that was a coin toss feels, afterwards, like something you saw coming, and the chance that you will take a bigger risk next time rises with it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Write your prediction down, with a probability, before the outcome is known.** Memory does not keep a record of what you thought, because it rebuilds the past from what you know now. A parent who guessed how a child would settle into a new school, or a manager who guessed how a hire would perform, will tend to remember having been right once the result is in. A dated note with a number on it can be checked a month later, and it shows you how often your confident guesses were close. It gives you a real track record in place of a flattering one.$$

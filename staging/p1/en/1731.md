[[open]]
Today's story is about an idea that everyone said would never work. You've almost certainly seen it at a fair or an amusement park. But first, a quick question: when was the last time someone looked at one of your ideas and said, "That won't work"? Keep it in mind. We'll come back to it at the end.

[[story]]
It's 1889, and Paris is hosting a World's Fair. The French pull off something that stops everyone in their tracks: Gustave Eiffel's iron tower, about 300 meters tall. Nothing built by human hands had ever gone that high.

On the other side of the Atlantic, Americans take it almost personally. Because four years later, in 1893, it's their turn. Chicago is hosting the next World's Fair. Daniel Burnham, the man in charge of building it, stands up in front of America's engineers and throws down a challenge. We have to beat Eiffel, he tells them. We have to *out-Eiffel* Eiffel. And frankly, he says, you engineers haven't shown me yet that you can.

The proposals start pouring in. And most of them are... the same idea in different sizes. A taller tower. A thicker tower. One person even suggests dropping visitors from the top of a giant tower in cars tied to rubber cables. Everyone is trying to beat Eiffel at Eiffel's own game.

Then a young engineer from Pittsburgh shows up, in his early thirties: George Washington Gale Ferris. His firm inspects steel for bridges and railroads. As he tells it, the idea hits him at a dinner, and he has it sketched out before the meal is even over. Not a tower. A giant steel wheel that turns, with passenger cars hanging from its rim. People climbed the Eiffel Tower to look down. Ferris was going to *carry* them into the sky.

How does the fair's committee react? They turn him down. They're convinced that something that huge, and that delicate-looking, will never hold its own weight. It'll buckle. It'll blow over. Some of them think Ferris has lost his mind.

And this is the first turning point. Ferris doesn't argue. He spends his own money hiring engineers and running the numbers, over and over. He keeps looking for investors and keeps strengthening the design. Then he applies again. Near the end of 1892 he finally gets the green light, with one catch: the fair won't give him a cent. He has to raise hundreds of thousands of dollars himself. And opening day is only months away.

Construction starts in winter. A Chicago winter. The ground is frozen about a meter deep, so the crews break through it with dynamite. Under the frost they hit quicksand. They blast jets of steam to thaw the soil, keep pumps running around the clock, and drive the foundations deep into the ground. The axle at the center of the wheel is the largest single piece of steel ever forged up to that point.

On May 1, 1893, the fair opens. The wheel isn't ready. Weeks go by. Everyone's waiting.

In early June comes the first test. The cars aren't even attached yet. The steam engine starts up, and that enormous ring slowly... begins to turn. The math holds. A few days later, on the first ride with cars, one of the passengers is Ferris's wife, Margaret.

On June 21, 1893, the wheel opens to the public. About 80 meters tall, 36 cars, each big enough for 60 people. More than two thousand people in the air at once. It becomes the most talked-about thing at the fair, Chicago's answer to Eiffel.

##Today, every wheel like it, anywhere in the world, still carries his name: the Ferris wheel.##

[[lessons]]
$$**"Impossible" often just means "I haven't seen the proof yet."** When Ferris was turned down, he didn't try to win the argument. He took the committee's fear seriously, *it will collapse*, and came back with the one thing that answered it: the numbers. Arguing puts people on opposite sides of the table. Evidence puts them on the same side.$$

$$**Trying to beat a rival at their own game usually means losing.** While everyone else was thinking "taller tower," Ferris changed the question. Not *how do I build something higher*, but *how do I give people something they'll never forget*. He didn't copy Eiffel. He answered him.$$

$$**Ideas are born at dinner. They get finished in frozen ground.** The romantic part of this story is the sketch at the table. The real part is dynamite, quicksand, steam, and a missed opening day. Owning an idea means owning the boring, brutal part too.$$

[[reflect]]
Let's go back to the question from the start: when did someone last tell you, "That won't work"?

- What did you do? Argue? Let it go? Or go and get proof?
- What was the real fear behind that "no"? Money, risk, time? Did you ever ask?
- And the other way around: whose idea did you shoot down last? If they'd brought you the numbers, would you have changed your mind?

&&What's the Eiffel Tower in front of you right now, and are you building a bigger copy of it, or a completely different answer?&&

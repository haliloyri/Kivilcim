Picture a room full of politicians spoiling for a fight. One man there has a reputation as the loudest patriot and the army's surest friend. Everyone knows how his speech will go. Then he stands up and says the opposite. Nobody in that chamber understood what they had just watched, and the next few minutes may change how you read the next speech you hear.

[[story]]
The year is 1850, and the place is the Prussian parliament. One of the deputies is Otto von Bismarck. At this point he isn't the towering figure history remembers. He's a member of the chamber with a clear reputation: an ardent nationalist and a firm supporter of the army. If anyone in that building could be counted on to beat the drum for Prussian strength, it was him.

The question on the table was war with Austria. Many deputies were pushing for it. Prussia and Austria were rivals, and to a large part of the chamber a fight seemed like the proud, patriotic thing to demand. Given everything people knew about Bismarck, they expected him to join them. Maybe they even expected him to lead the charge.

So he rose to speak, and he argued against war. He made the case for peace. His colleagues were surprised, and you can see why. The man they had filed away as the hawk of hawks was pleading for restraint.

Now look past the chamber to the one listener who mattered most. The Prussian king was reluctant to go to war. He didn't want the fight many of the deputies were calling for. And here was a known nationalist, a friend of the army, standing up and giving voice to the king's own caution. That carried weight. A peace argument from a timid man is easy to dismiss. The same argument from a firebrand sounds like sober judgment.

The speech won Bismarck royal favor. Soon afterward he was given an important post. The speech that baffled his fellow deputies had turned into a step up for him.

Robert Greene uses this episode in *The 48 Laws of Power* to illustrate Law 3: **Conceal Your Intentions.** The law says to keep people off balance and in the dark by never revealing the purpose behind your actions, and to use decoys and smoke screens to hide it. In Greene's reading, the peace speech was one of those smoke screens. Bismarck's words pointed one way while his real aim pointed somewhere else. That aim was his own advancement. He needed access to power, and the king held it.

##Years later, as prime minister, Bismarck led Prussia into war against Austria.##

The man who had argued for peace in 1850 became the man who waged the very war he had once argued against. The deputies who heard that speech couldn't have known it at the time. What he said told them almost nothing about what he wanted.

[[lessons]]
$$**A stated position can be a tool.** The deputies heard Bismarck's peace speech as a statement of belief, but Greene reads it as a means to an end. The words served a goal Bismarck never said out loud: getting closer to power. When someone suddenly takes a position that doesn't fit what you know about them, it's worth asking what the position gets them, and not only whether they mean it.$$

$$**Echoing the decision-maker buys access.** The king was reluctant to fight. Bismarck gave that reluctance a voice, and the voice was more convincing because it came from a known nationalist. The reward was royal favor and an important post. Anyone who wants something from you may do the same thing and tell you your own opinion back, well phrased.$$

$$**The track record tells you what the speech hid.** In 1850, no one listening could separate Bismarck's words from his aims. Only time did that, when the "peace" speaker became the prime minister who led Prussia into war with Austria. One statement in one moment is a thin basis for judging intentions. A pattern of actions over years is much harder to fake. Greene presents these laws as dynamics you can learn to recognize, so understanding concealment is also a way to protect yourself from it.$$

[[reflect]]
- Think of a time someone told you exactly what you wanted to hear and later moved the other way. What did their actions show you before the reversal came?
- In negotiations or career moves, do you tend to lay out your full goal early, the way Bismarck never did?
- Bismarck's speech was persuasive partly because it surprised people. When has an unexpected position from someone lowered your guard?
- Where is your own line between keeping a goal private and actively misleading people?

&&If you judged the people around you by their track record instead of their speeches, whose intentions would suddenly look different?&&

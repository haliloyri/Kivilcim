Two people are asked the same plain question: how happy are you right now? One of them has just won a fortune in a state lottery. The other is a patient in a rehabilitation institute, paralysed after an accident. The researchers want to know whose answer will be higher, and by how much.

[[story]]
Stone and Heen's *Thanks for the Feedback* builds on the idea that each of us has a baseline of happiness we keep returning to. Sheila Heen has described this research in a talk at Google: a year after winning the lottery, or a year after going to jail, people are about as happy or unhappy as they were before. The most famous study behind this idea is older than the book by decades.

In 1978 the psychologists Philip Brickman, Dan Coates and Ronnie Janoff-Bulman published it in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*. They interviewed 22 people who had won $50,000 or more in the Illinois State Lottery, 22 neighbours who had not, and 29 accident victims at a rehabilitation institute, 11 paraplegic and 18 quadriplegic.

The lottery winners turned out not to be significantly happier than the control group. On a scale from 0 to 5 they averaged about 4.0, and the people who had won nothing about 3.8. The winners also took less pleasure from ordinary things, the small events of an average day. A huge piece of luck had made the everyday a little duller by comparison, because every ordinary pleasure now stood next to the day of the win.

The accident victims rated their present happiness lower than both other groups, around 3.0. That is lower, as you would expect, yet not as low as many would have guessed, and the researchers noticed something else. These patients looked back at their past lives and rated them as especially happy, a comparison that did not lift their present.

Brickman's reading was adaptation. The peak of winning becomes the new yardstick, and the new pleasure fades into habit. Notice what the study does not say: nobody claimed the accident victims felt fine. It says that the distance between an event and your mood is smaller, and shorter, than we expect.

That is why Stone and Heen care about it. When someone hands you critical feedback, the first minutes feel like a verdict. Your mood drops, and the drop feels permanent. The research suggests your baseline is stronger than that moment. The same pull that flattened the lottery winners' joy works on a bad review too, so you will probably be back near your usual level sooner than the first hour tells you. That is no reason to ignore the comment, only a reason not to rule on it while the sting is at its sharpest.

[[lessons]]
$$**Let the first reaction settle before you decide what a comment means.** Brickman's lottery winners and accident victims both ended up closer to ordinary levels of happiness than anyone predicted, which suggests that how bad or good an event feels right now is a poor guide to how it will feel later. A sharp comment in a Friday review can seem like the end of your standing at work, yet on the following Wednesday you may see it as one useful point among many. So when feedback stings, write down the one fact you heard and postpone the verdict about yourself. You will decide better from your baseline than from the peak of the sting.$$

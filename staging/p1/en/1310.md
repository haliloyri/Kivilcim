Set a hot cup of coffee on the table and leave it. Whatever you do, it drifts toward room temperature. Now think of money sitting in a bank account, earning interest. It does not drift back to anything. It keeps growing on itself. Donella Meadows says these two ordinary things run on two different kinds of loop, and that the second one has a darker side most people miss.

[[story]]
In *Thinking in Systems: A Primer*, Donella H. Meadows describes two basic feedback loops.

The first is the balancing loop. It pushes a stock, some amount of something, toward a goal. Her examples are homely. A thermostat notices the room is too cold and turns the heat on, then turns it off once the room is near the setting. A hot cup of coffee cools toward the temperature of the room around it.

The second is the reinforcing loop. Instead of pulling toward a goal, it amplifies whatever direction the stock is already moving. Her example is money in a bank account. The more money you have, the more interest it earns. The interest is added to the money, so next time it earns even more. The loop feeds itself.

That self-feeding produces exponential growth. But Meadows is clear that it works in both directions. A reinforcing loop can drive runaway collapse just as easily as runaway growth.

##The same structure that compounds savings can compound a decline.##

From the inside, that is hard to see. When things snowball upward, it can feel like talent or good fortune. When they slide faster and faster downward, it can feel like bad luck. Meadows's point is that in both cases you may be watching the same structure, a reinforcing loop, running in opposite directions.

So how do you tell which loop is in charge? She argues that a system's behavior over time gives it away. Something that keeps settling back toward the same level is likely governed by a balancing loop. Something that keeps accelerating, up or down, likely has a reinforcing loop in control.

Seeing that structure is the first step. It will not fix anything by itself. But it tells you where to look for leverage, instead of pushing hard at the wrong spot.

[[lessons]]
$$**Balancing loops pull things back.** The thermostat and the cooling coffee both move toward a set point. If something keeps returning to the same level however hard you push, a balancing loop may be aiming at a goal. It is worth asking whether that goal is the one you actually want.$$

$$**Reinforcing loops amplify direction.** Interest earns interest, so small differences compound over time. The same shape can compound a decline. That makes early direction matter: a reinforcing loop does not care whether it is taking you up or down.$$

$$**Behavior over time reveals the loop.** Meadows says you can read which loop dominates from how a system behaves. Settling means balancing; accelerating means reinforcing. Knowing which one you are facing is what makes a sensible intervention possible, rather than a guess. It tells you whether to question the goal a system is chasing or to look at what keeps feeding the snowball.$$

[[story]]
Caldwell Esselstyn is a physician best known for treating heart disease with plant-based eating, the approach Michael Greger champions in *How Not to Die*. In 2014 he and his colleagues published a paper in the *Journal of Family Practice* under the title "A way to reverse CAD?" It followed a smaller earlier study that had reported arrest and even reversal of coronary artery disease, and it answered the doubt that followed: would this work in a larger group of ordinary patients?

The paper tracked 198 consecutive patients with established cardiovascular disease. They were volunteers who wanted to move to a plant-based diet alongside their usual cardiovascular care, and they received counselling to help them do it. The authors counted a patient as adherent if they had eliminated dairy, fish and meat.

By that definition 177 of the 198 were adherent, which is 89 percent. Among those 177, the authors counted one major cardiac event judged to be recurrent disease, a stroke, for a recurrent event rate of 0.6 percent. They had stayed on the diet for a mean of 3.7 years.

The twenty-one who had not stuck to it told a different story. Thirteen of them, 62 percent, had adverse events.

It reads like a verdict, yet 13 against 1 is a comparison between two groups that formed themselves, and the authors did not present it as a verdict. They wrote that the approach deserved a wider test, to see whether adherence could be kept up in broader groups of people. There was no control group, and nobody was randomly assigned to eat one way or another. The patients volunteered, which means they were people already inclined to change, and the authors judged for themselves which events counted as recurrent disease.

The comparison between the two groups has the same hole. Patients who follow a demanding plan for years may also differ in other ways from patients who give it up, and the paper cannot separate what the diet did from who the dieters were. Only a design that handed out the diet by lottery, so that motivated and unmotivated patients landed in both groups, could pull the two apart. That is exactly the wider test the authors asked for, and it is also what the Lifestyle Heart Trial's randomised design set out to do on a smaller scale.

[[lessons]]
$$**When people sort themselves into groups, the sorting is part of the result.** Those who kept to a hard plan and those who dropped it were never comparable to begin with, so the gap between them mixes the plan with the people. You meet the same trap in the office when the staff who attend the voluntary training outperform the ones who skip it, and at home when the family member who "really sticks with" a routine seems to benefit most. Before you credit the method, ask who chose it and who quit, and what else was different about them.$$

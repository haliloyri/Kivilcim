On her hands and knees in a Maine condo, a woman works a brush along the grout between marble tiles. Someone has told her to scrub it extra hard. Whatever she thinks about that, she keeps behind her teeth and goes on scrubbing.

[[story]]
In Portland, Maine, Barbara Ehrenreich spent her weekdays with a house-cleaning service, one of the jobs that make up *Nickel and Dimed*. She worked on a cleaning team that moved from house to house. A published summary adds that new cleaners watched training videos on the approved methods, and that the pace of each house was set by the time the company allotted to it. A clock like that leaves a person little room to look up, which is why the price list is easy to miss and worth reading.

Customers paid the company $25 for each hour of a cleaner's work, according to a Salon review of the book. The cleaner herself was paid $6.65 an hour. Most published accounts agree on that figure, though one older write-up gives a slightly different one. Either way, a quarter of the price reached the person holding the brush.

The company set other terms as well. Cleaners were not allowed to eat or drink while they were in a customer's home, so a day on the allotted clock ran without a snack or a sip of water.

Then there was the marble. A review in The Nation recounts a million-dollar condo where Ehrenreich, on her hands and knees, was told to scrub the marble grout extra hard. She fantasised about the kind of revenge George Orwell wrote about, but she said nothing, because speaking up would have blown her cover as an undercover writer.

Hold the two numbers side by side. For one hour of the same labour, the customer sees $25 and the worker sees $6.65. The gap is where the company's costs and profit live, and the worker can see none of it from the floor. She sees the small slice that arrives in her hourly wage, and the customer sees a bill with no one's face on it.

Her silence on the marble had a particular reason, and it points to something larger. Her cover required it. A regular employee in that spot would have had a quieter and more ordinary reason: she needed the job. In both cases the person with the brush could not afford to tell the person with the checkbook what she thought of the instruction.

[[lessons]]
$$**Learn both ends of your hourly rate: what the buyer pays for your time and what reaches you.** The distance between the two is usually explained by rent, equipment, insurance and a margin, but you can only judge whether it is fair once you can see it. A freelancer who has never written down what a client pays an agency for her work, or an employee who has never asked what the customer's invoice says about his team, is negotiating with half the numbers. Ask for the price chain, and keep it beside your own rate when you talk about a raise.$$

A captured scribe stands before the conqueror of his people with a handful of small carved stamps in his hands. They are worth nothing as loot, and they are not weapons. Yet the conqueror listens to him explain what they do, and keeps listening with real interest.

[[story]]
Around 1204 the Mongols defeated the Naimans, the most powerful people of western Mongolia. In the aftermath, a man named Tata-tonga was brought before Chinggis Khan, the title Temujin carries in Jack Weatherford's *Genghis Khan and the Making of the Modern World*. Tata-tonga was a Uyghur scribe, and he was holding the official seals of the Naiman khan. Published accounts, which draw on the Chinese history of the Mongol dynasty, the *Yuan shi*, tell what happened next.

The seals, Tata-tonga explained, were a form of certification. When a representative of the Naiman khan went out to collect taxes, he carried papers stamped with this seal, and the stamp proved he had the authority to ask. Tata-tonga had worked as a scribe and tax collector, writing records in the Uyghur script.

Chinggis took to the idea at once, according to those accounts. A ruler's word normally reached only as far as a voice could carry or a rider could travel. Here was a method by which his word could travel without him: written down, stamped, and trusted by people who had never met him.

Tata-tonga adapted the Uyghur script to the Mongolian language as well as he could. Chinggis then ordered him to teach it to his sons and nephews. The man who had arrived as a prisoner holding the seals of a defeated khan was now the teacher of the next generation of the ruling family.

His own family did well out of it too. According to the same account, Tata-tonga and his descendants stayed among the privileged until the Mongol rule in China came to an end. The script he helped to adapt is still used for Mongolian in Inner Mongolia today.

The conqueror had the seals in front of him and the scribe at his mercy. He let the scribe explain what the object was for, and he concluded that the most useful thing to take from the Naimans was a working method, worth more than any pasture or horse. Many leaders would have seen a defeated rival's paperwork as a curiosity. This one treated it as a tool that someone else had already tested.

The scribe's explanation was modest, too. He made no case for writing in general. He described what one stamp did on one tax road, and the larger idea followed from that. Concrete knowledge, offered plainly by someone who actually did the job, is what turned a captive into a teacher.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask the people who do the work what each tool is for before you decide what to do with it.** When you take over a team, a shop or a household routine, there are usually objects and habits you can't explain at first sight, like a form that gets stamped or a spreadsheet that gets sent on Fridays. Someone who has used it every week can tell you in two minutes what it protects against and what depends on it. Listen first and judge afterwards, and you may find that the most useful thing you inherited is something you were about to throw away.$$

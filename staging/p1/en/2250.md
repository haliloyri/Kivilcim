[[story]]
An ex-member of a Chicago street gang thought two academics might want to see how the gang worked from the inside. He gave them its books: the accounts of one Black Kings set, covering narcotics sales and extortion over four years. The academics were Sudhir Venkatesh and Steven Levitt, co-author of *Freakonomics*.

Keeping those books was dangerous. The set had a treasurer, an enforcer and a runner who collected goods from the supplier. The treasurer wrote down on notebook paper what each member earned and what the set took in overall, and in police hands the notebooks would have been evidence. The authors report that many leaders kept such records anyway, and some members even accused the bookkeepers of earning more than they wrote down. Leaders argued that they would rather risk jail than face rough punishment from their superiors for failing to run the drug trade well.

What the books showed was a pyramid. In one of the authors' papers, a typical foot soldier, the member who sold on the street, earned under $2,000 a year on the books for about twenty hours of selling a week. Even counting extra sales on the side, his wage stayed under $6 an hour. Officers worked about thirty hours and were estimated at $7.70 an hour. The leader of the set averaged $63,000 a year over the period studied. All foot soldiers received the same monthly wage, whatever they sold. Below them waited the youngest members, hoping to be let in, and in this set it was those newcomers who paid dues to the gang, not the foot soldiers who did the selling.

So why take the job? Some foot soldiers valued working with their peers and setting their own hours, and many had few other options beyond fast-food or cleaning work. In their economic analysis of the gang's finances, Levitt and Venkatesh also concluded that the hope of rising to a leader's income was a main draw. By their account, members at the bottom were paying their dues in the hope of a climb that few of them would complete.

The authors did not claim that every gang looked like this. Not all of Chicago's neighborhood gangs were commercial, they noted, and even in the ones that were, most members earned only slightly above minimum wage. This was one set's ledger over four years. It is a rare look at a pay structure that is usually hidden, and that is what makes the pattern worth having.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask what a low-paid job is really paying for.** A junior designer who stays late every evening at a studio for modest pay may be buying a chance to lead a project one day, and that bet can be reasonable or foolish depending on how many people before her actually made the climb. The salary alone tells you little, while the rate of promotion tells you a great deal. Before you accept a stretch of poor pay for a later prize, ask who has reached the prize recently, how long it took them, and what the average person on your rung earns in the meantime.$$

A contestant sits under hot studio lights, a lot of money riding on one question, and no idea of the answer. She has two lifelines left. She can phone a friend she picked in advance for being clever, or she can turn to a hall full of strangers and ask them to vote.

[[story]]
On the television quiz show *Who Wants to Be a Millionaire?* a contestant who got stuck could use help in two different forms. One was "phone a friend": before the show, contestants named a person they trusted to know things, and in the hot seat they could call that person for thirty seconds. The other was "ask the audience": the people in the studio each pressed a button for the answer they believed was right, and the contestant saw the vote.

James Surowiecki uses this show in *The Wisdom of Crowds* as a kind of accidental experiment. The friends were chosen on purpose, usually the cleverest and best-informed people a contestant knew, so they were as close to experts as a game show could manage. The audience was nobody in particular, whoever had come to watch.

According to Surowiecki's account, the experts gave the right answer about 65 percent of the time. The audience of ordinary people chose the right answer about 91 percent of the time. Other writers who have counted the show's results quote somewhat different percentages, but they agree on the direction: the studio crowd did better than the phoned experts.

Surowiecki himself says this was not a controlled study, and it should not be read as one. The questions were not the same for each lifeline, and people tended to choose the lifeline to match the question. Still, the gap was large enough that it stuck.

The reason is arithmetic, not magic. A friend on the phone is one mind, and even a very good mind has holes. Ask it a question about opera when it knows football, and the answer is a guess. A studio audience is hundreds of minds with hundreds of different holes. For any one question, a few people know the answer for sure, and most of the others know a little or lean the right way. The ones who know vote the same way. The ones who do not know scatter their votes across the wrong choices, so no single wrong answer collects many. The right answer wins by the sum of the small leans.

That also tells you where the method fails. As the questions grew harder and more obscure, the audience was less helpful, because on a question nobody in the room knows, a hundred guesses are still guesses. Crowds are good at pooling scattered knowledge. They cannot produce knowledge that is not in the room.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you hand a question to your one best expert, ask whether a varied group already holds the answer between them.** An expert covers one corner of a problem deeply, while a mixed group covers many corners thinly, and a problem that spans corners often suits the group better. In a project that touches sales, engineering and customer service, ask all three for a quick independent view before you phone the specialist. Use the expert for questions that need depth, and use the crowd for questions where the pieces are scattered. The choice costs you ten minutes and spares you the risk of betting everything on one person's blind spot.$$

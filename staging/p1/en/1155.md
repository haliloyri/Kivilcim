You make a small mistake: a typo in an email, a missed detail in a meeting. Within seconds, a voice in your head delivers a full verdict. *Careless. Typical. You always do this.* Shirzad Chamine thinks that voice has a name, and that it isn't nearly as wise as it sounds.

[[story]]
Shirzad Chamine spent years as an executive coach, working with CEOs and senior leaders. These were intelligent, experienced, successful people. And yet he kept seeing the same thing. At crucial moments, they undermined themselves. They attacked themselves after mistakes. They imagined disasters when things were uncertain. They felt they weren't enough even after real success.

In *Positive Intelligence*, he gives this pattern a name. He calls these self-defeating voices **Saboteurs**.

Saboteurs are automatic patterns of thinking and feeling. What makes them dangerous is that they don't feel like enemies. They feel like you. They sound like good judgment, high standards or healthy caution.

##The most powerful Saboteur of all, Chamine says, is the Judge.##

The Judge is the master Saboteur, and nearly everyone has one. It constantly finds fault. With you: *that wasn't good enough*. With other people: *they're so incompetent*. With circumstances: *this always happens to me*. It tells you it's keeping you sharp. In practice, it mostly generates stress, anxiety and disappointment.

The Judge doesn't work alone. Chamine describes nine accomplices. There's the **Stickler**, who demands perfection. The **Pleaser**, who needs everyone's approval. The **Hyper-Achiever**, whose self-worth depends on performance. The **Victim**, who dwells on how unfair things are. The **Hyper-Rational**, who treats feelings as distractions. The **Hyper-Vigilant**, always scanning for danger. The **Restless**, always chasing the next thing. The **Controller**, who needs to be in charge. And the **Avoider**, who steers around anything unpleasant.

Most people recognize a few of these immediately.

Chamine explains why they're so hard to shake. Saboteurs usually began as survival strategies, often in childhood. At some point, being perfect, pleasing people or staying on guard really did help. The problem is that they kept running long after they stopped being useful.

So what do you do about them? Chamine doesn't suggest fighting or silencing them. He suggests something simpler. Notice them, and name them. When the voice starts, you say to yourself: *that's my Judge talking.* Or: *there's the Controller again.*

It sounds almost too simple. But naming a Saboteur puts a small gap between you and it. Once you can see it as a pattern, and not the truth, it loses much of its power.

[[lessons]]
$$**The harshest voice isn't the wisest.** The Judge presents itself as honesty and high standards. Chamine's point is that it mostly produces stress and disappointment rather than better work. When your inner criticism goes from "this needs fixing" to "you always fail," it's worth asking whether that's judgment or the Judge.$$

$$**Saboteurs once protected you.** Chamine explains that these patterns usually started as survival strategies. Being perfect or pleasing people may have genuinely helped once. That's why they feel so right, and why shame about having them doesn't help. They're old tools running past their usefulness.$$

$$**Naming a Saboteur weakens it.** "That's my Judge talking" is a small sentence with a real effect. Labeling the voice separates you from it, so you can see it as a habit of mind rather than a fact. You don't have to win an argument with it. You just have to recognize it.$$

After the 2011 accident at Fukushima, Hans Rosling looks at two counts that rarely appear side by side. One is the number of people reported killed by the radiation. The other concerns the people who died during the escape from it. The two numbers do not point the way most people would expect.

[[story]]
Rosling tells this story in *Factfulness*, written with Ola Rosling and Anna Rosling Rönnlund. It illustrates one of ten instincts he says distort our view of the world. Others include the gap instinct, the negativity instinct, and the urgency instinct. This one is the fear instinct.

His claim about fear is precise. Fear makes us pay attention to what is frightening, not to what is actually dangerous. The two often overlap, but not always. When they come apart, vivid risks dominate our thinking while real ones get neglected.

Fukushima shows how far apart they can get. After the 2011 accident, the fear of radiation was enormous, and it drove a hasty evacuation.

##No one was reported killed by the radiation, but the evacuation itself is linked to many deaths, especially among elderly people.##

The frightening thing was the radiation. The deadly thing, in this case, turned out to be the rushed escape from it. People fled a danger that, by the reported count, killed no one, and the flight carried its own cost.

To keep fear from steering decisions like this, Rosling offers a rule of thumb. The risk something poses depends on two things: how dangerous it is, and how much you are exposed to it. Risk equals danger times exposure.

The formula sounds almost too simple. But it forces a second question that fear tends to skip. Fear looks at the danger and stops there. Something can be terrifying in principle and still pose little risk if your exposure is small. Something dull can be risky if you are exposed to it every day.

Rosling's practical advice follows from this. When you are frightened, calm down first. Then calculate the risk before deciding. That order matters, because the moment of greatest fear is also the moment when a quick, dramatic reaction feels most justified.

The point is not that fear is always wrong, or that radiation is harmless. It is that a reaction can cause harm of its own, and that harm needs to be weighed too. At Fukushima, the response to the fear appears to have cost more lives than the thing people were afraid of. Measuring both danger and exposure is a way to catch that before acting, not after.

[[lessons]]
$$**Frightening isn't the same as dangerous.** Rosling's fear instinct explains why vivid threats grab our attention while quieter ones slip past. The feeling of fear tells you how scary something is. It doesn't tell you how likely it is to hurt you, and treating those as the same leads to lopsided decisions.$$

$$**Risk needs two numbers.** Danger times exposure is a short formula with a big effect. It makes you ask how bad something is and how much of it you will actually face. Fear tends to answer only the first question, which is why a calm second look, with exposure written next to danger, can change the decision.$$

$$**Reactions carry their own risk.** At Fukushima, the radiation was reported to kill no one, while the fear-driven evacuation is linked to many deaths among elderly people. Any response to a threat, especially a rushed one, has costs that belong in the calculation alongside the threat itself.$$

[[reflect]]
- Which risk in your life gets a lot of your worry but very little of your exposure?
- Which ordinary risk gets little worry, even though you face it often?
- When did a fearful reaction end up costing more than the thing you feared?

&&The next time something frightens you into a quick decision, will you pause to estimate both the danger and your actual exposure, and see whether the decision changes?&&

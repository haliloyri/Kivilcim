[[open]]
On the floor of a Cameroonian rainforest, a researcher bends over a pile of chimpanzee droppings and scoops a sample into a tube. No animal is caught, and none is even seen. The tube will go to a laboratory and carry part of the answer to a question about a pandemic.

[[story]]
David Quammen's *Spillover* turns to the origin of AIDS, and this story is about one study behind that account. The question was where HIV-1, the virus that causes AIDS, had come from. By the time this team went into the field, an earlier paper in *Nature* in 1999 had identified a chimpanzee subspecies, *Pan troglodytes troglodytes*, as the reservoir of the virus. Its authors noted that the natural range of that subspecies matches the places where HIV-1 groups M, N and O are found. That work, however, used samples from captive animals, and nobody had yet shown the virus in wild chimpanzees.

So in the study published in *Science* in 2006, led by Brandon Keele and Beatrice Hahn, the team went looking in the wild. They collected 599 faecal specimens at ten forest sites across southern Cameroon, picking them up from the forest floor, which meant they never had to catch or disturb a single animal. Mitochondrial DNA in the droppings told them which species each sample came from. Of the 599, 446 were chimpanzee, 67 were gorilla and 86 were too degraded to use.

The droppings were then tested for antibodies to the chimpanzee virus, SIVcpz, and for its genetic material. Thirty-four samples reacted, and they came from sixteen individual chimpanzees. The infection was far from evenly spread. In three communities between 23 and 35 per cent of the chimpanzees carried it, in two others only 4 or 5 per cent, and in five communities none at all.

Then the researchers compared the genetic sequences of the chimpanzee viruses with those of human HIV-1. The pandemic form, group M, traced back to chimpanzees in southeastern Cameroon, and the rarer group N to chimpanzees in south-central Cameroon. According to the study, the virus behind a worldwide pandemic could be followed to particular forest communities.

The evidence was never dramatic: a heap of droppings, a tube, a laboratory test. It took 599 of them, gathered patiently across ten sites, to turn a suspicion into a map.

[[lessons]]
$$**Look for the source in what the source leaves behind.** A café owner who wants to know why afternoons are quiet could interrogate every regular, but the till receipts, collected for a month, will say more with no one feeling watched. Indirect traces can be gathered at scale, repeated and compared, which a single conversation or one dramatic observation cannot match, and they do not change the thing being studied. When a problem has no obvious witness, ask what it leaves lying around, and collect enough of it to see a pattern. The result may be less vivid than a witness, but it is easier to check and to repeat.$$

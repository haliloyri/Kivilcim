Two students sit in French classes, one in a beginners' course and one in an advanced course. Each gets the same offer: a teacher who points out what they do well, or a teacher who points out what they still get wrong. The researchers want to know whether the two students will choose alike.

[[story]]
Stacey Finkelstein and Ayelet Fishbach of the University of Chicago ran this kind of question through five studies, published in the *Journal of Consumer Research* in 2012 under the title "Tell Me What I Did Wrong: Experts Seek and Respond to Negative Feedback." One of the studies used students in beginning and advanced French courses, and the others tested beauty services, environmental donations and a media message.

The pattern was consistent. In the authors' words, novices seek more positive feedback than experts, and they respond more to it. Experts did the reverse: they sought negative feedback and responded better to it.

In the French study the instructors' feedback either praised progress or pointed to insufficient progress. Beginners were more likely to change their behaviour after praise, while advanced students were more motivated by being told they were falling short. The authors' explanation is about commitment. Praise strengthens a novice's commitment to keep going, while criticism tells an expert that the pace is too slow, and experts care about pace.

What does this have to do with a book on receiving feedback? Stone and Heen split feedback into three kinds, appreciation, coaching and evaluation, and argue that people need all three at different moments. Seen through the French study, the question "which kind do I need now?" has a sensible answer that depends on where you are. A beginner who gets only a list of mistakes may lose heart and stop. An expert who gets only applause may learn nothing new, because applause confirms the pace and says nothing about speeding it up.

In their Google talk, Stone and Heen also separate fishing for compliments from actually asking what you could improve. The two requests can sound alike and do different jobs. The first checks your standing. The second asks for coaching, and it is the one an expert has the most use for.

There is an uncomfortable corollary. If you have become good at something, you may be starved of the kind of feedback you most want, because the people around you default to praise. They are being kind, and they may be giving you the wrong thing. The fix starts with you: nobody can guess that you have outgrown praise unless you say so.

[[lessons]]
$$**Say which kind of feedback you need, and ask experts around you for the negative kind once you are good.** Finkelstein and Fishbach found that novices respond most to praise and experts most to being told what falls short, so the same sentence can fuel one person and stall another. Ask your colleague for what fits your stage: early in a new skill, "what is working?"; after years, "what is the one thing that would make this noticeably better?" People tend to hand you praise by default, and a precise request is often the only way you will get coaching. It also tells the giver that you can take it.$$

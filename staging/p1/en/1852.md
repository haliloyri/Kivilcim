Three miles into his own race, Micah True looks back over his shoulder and shouts "Andale huevones!" His own translation: get moving, lazy ones. Five Raramuri runners are behind him, a steep trail is ahead, and the finish is a town in the canyon below. He set the pace, and he also wrote the invitation.

[[story]]
Micah True, known as Caballo Blanco, is one of the best-known names in Christopher McDougall's *Born to Run*. In November 2003 he organised a trail marathon in Mexico's Copper Canyon, from Batopilas to Urique. Seven runners entered: five Raramuri, plus True and Mel Downs of Colorado. Participants were required to do a two-day practice hike along the route, and the Raramuri runners registered at True's house the day before.

The course crossed the Sierra Madre. It began with a steep descent of about 3,000 feet to the Los Alisos aid station, followed by roughly nine miles of trail, a river crossing and a dirt road into Urique. Mel's GPS measured the whole thing at 27 miles.

True went out briskly. At about the three-mile mark, by his own account, he called out "andale huevones" to the runners behind him. The Raramuri runners passed him on the next hill, and he did not see the first five finishers again until Urique.

Manuel Luna won in 5:20, with Martin second in 5:25. Herbalisto, Florencio and Maurilio followed at 5:49, 5:50 and 5:51. True was sixth in 6:35, which beat the 6:41 he knew as the best time by a non-Indigenous runner, his own earlier mark. Mel Downs arrived a few hours later, in 9:59. Because drought followed by monsoon rains had eroded the trail, most times were at least half an hour slower than in the previous race, which had been run in the opposite direction.

In Urique, people in the street applauded the finishers. At the awards ceremony in the Plaza restaurant the Raramuri runners were handed cash prizes. True wrote of the day as a celebration in which everyone involved had won, and said he hoped the Raramuri and others would keep running free.

The result is easy to read once you set the facts side by side. True had chosen the route, taken the entries at his house and started hard. On the first real hill, that pace meant little next to the pace of people who knew the trail, and the first five finishers were out of his sight until the town. Even so, he finished sixth. Owning the race did not make him the best runner on its trail, though he still finished with a time better than any he knew of for a non-Indigenous runner.

[[lessons]]
$$**Enter a new place at the pace of the people who already live there.** Say you start in a workshop where everyone seems slow, and you are tempted to show your speed on day one. Spend the first week watching how long the longest-serving person takes over each job, and you will set your expectations from the real pace instead of from your own. Seeing how fast they are does not shrink you, because they set the measure and you can count your own progress against it. That week of watching tells you where your effort will actually matter.$$

[[open]]
A family eats McDonald's in a car on a California freeway: a cheeseburger, nuggets, fries, a salad, a shake and a soda. Nothing on that list looks like anything else on it. Afterwards the meal goes to a laboratory at Berkeley, where a machine looks for a single marker in every item.

[[story]]
Todd Dawson's mass spectrometer at the University of California, Berkeley, can tell how much of the carbon in a food came from corn. Michael Pollan fed it the first of the four meals in *The Omnivore's Dilemma*, a McDonald's lunch that he, his wife and his son had eaten in the car.

The method rests on a marker. Corn is a C4 plant, and C4 plants take up a higher share of the heavy carbon isotope, carbon-13. The machine compares carbon-13 with carbon-12, and a high ratio points to corn.

The soda came out at 100 percent corn carbon. Next came the milk shake at 78, then the salad dressing at 65, the chicken nuggets at 56, the cheeseburger at 52 and the French fries at 23. Those percentages describe the carbon in each item, so a 56 for the nuggets means a little over half of their carbon came from corn. Pollan's reading was that a meal that looked like a hamburger, nuggets and a salad was made largely from corn.

The reasons are in his other reporting. More than half of the 10 billion bushels of corn grown each year goes to animals, and nearly 10 percent of the calories Americans eat come from corn sweeteners. Carbon from feed ends up in an animal's flesh, so the marker follows corn through the chicken and into the nugget. For many children the sweetener share is closer to 20 percent. Corn reaches the plate unseen, as an animal's feed or a drink's syrup.

The method has a limit: it cannot tell corn from sugar cane, since both are C4 plants. Still, a later study pointed the same way. A. Hope Jahren and Rebecca Kraft, of the University of Hawaii, used a similar approach on 162 hamburgers from McDonald's, Burger King and Wendy's outlets in cities across the country. Only 12 could have come from cattle fed something other than corn, and all of those were from Burger King branches on the West Coast. Every chicken sandwich came from a corn-fed bird.

According to the same study, one hamburger, one chicken sandwich and a small order of fries supplied half of a day's recommended calories for $3.

In the car there were six different items and six different wrappers. The spectrometer traced them largely to one plant.

[[lessons]]
$$**Count where the variety comes from before you count how much of it there is.** A workshop that orders from several suppliers and then finds they all buy from one wholesaler has less variety than its order book suggests. The same happens with an investment mix, a client list or the news sources someone follows: the names differ, the root is shared. Looking at the source shows you how many items one failure could hit at once, and whether the spread on paper is real protection or only appearance. That check takes a phone call or a quick look at the invoices, and it is far cheaper than finding out during a shortage.$$

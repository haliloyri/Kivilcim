In a hotel room in Minneapolis the king-size bed is also the desk and the dinner table. There is no refrigerator, no microwave and no shade on the window. Dinner is a fast-food bag, because there is nowhere to cook. Down the hall, the other rooms hold families.

[[story]]
Start with a budget Barbara Ehrenreich sketched in a university talk about *Nickel and Dimed*. A single mother takes home $1,200 a month. Rent takes $600, child care takes $400, and what remains is $200 for groceries, utilities, transport, car repairs, toiletries and any medical or unexpected cost. Ehrenreich said that both the rent and the child-care figures were unrealistically low.

Her own search in the Twin Cities showed why. The cheapest room with a refrigerator and a microwave cost $800 a month, she said. She rented a room in a residential hotel instead, with neither of those, and in her words "the other rooms in this hotel were occupied by families", so the king-size bed doubled as desk and dining table. With no way to cook, she ate fast food, and she said some of her coworkers lived on Doritos because they rarely had money for better.

Coworkers also taught her something about words. Some slept in their vehicles and did not count themselves as homeless: "If you have a vehicle to sleep in, you don't call yourself homeless anymore." A label can say less about a situation than the person wearing it would like.

Then she looked at the official yardstick. At $7 an hour, she said, federal standards did not classify her as poor. The government's poverty calculation, she explained, rests on food prices, which have stayed relatively stable, rather than on housing and child care, which have risen sharply. The official share of Americans in poverty, she noted, was 12.5%.

The Nation's reviewer adds a second measure from the Economic Policy Institute. It put a living wage at $30,000 a year for one adult and two children, about $14 an hour. By the institute's figures, about 60% of American workers earned less than $14 an hour, and about 30% earned $8 or less in 1998.

Set the budget beside the yardstick and the problem becomes plain. A measure built on the cost of food keeps counting food, while the costs that actually swallow a paycheck, a room and a child-care bill, move somewhere else. The ruler can stay perfectly accurate about what it measures and still miss the thing that decides whether a family gets through the month.

[[lessons]]
$$**Check whether your yardstick still tracks the costs that decide the outcome.** A number built in one year can keep reporting calmly while the thing it was meant to capture has moved. A team that measures its workload by the number of tickets closed, or a household that judges its finances by income alone, can look fine while the real pressure comes from somewhere the measure ignores. Once a year, ask which cost lines have grown fastest, and compare them with what your measure counts before you rely on it again. A number that has never been questioned deserves a second look more than any other.$$

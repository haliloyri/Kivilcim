Whether a prisoner is granted parole should depend on the case. Daniel Pink describes research suggesting it also depends partly on something else: how long it has been since the judge last took a break to eat.

[[story]]
In *When*, Daniel Pink calls the early-to-mid afternoon a kind of hidden danger zone. It's the **trough**, the stretch of the day when mood and alertness dip for most people. And he shows that its effects reach far beyond feeling a bit sleepy.

One example comes from medicine. Researchers at Duke University Medical Center looked at errors related to anesthesia during surgery. They found that such errors were significantly more likely during procedures that began in the mid-afternoon than those that began in the morning. Same hospital, same staff, same procedures. Different hour.

Another example comes from the courtroom. A team of researchers studied parole boards in Israel, analyzing more than a thousand decisions by experienced judges. The judges were far more likely to grant parole early in the day. As hours passed, favorable rulings dropped steeply. Then something striking happened. After the judges took a break for a meal or snack, the rate of favorable rulings jumped back up, before sliding again as the next session wore on.

##The judges weren't getting less fair. They were getting more tired, and tired minds tend to choose the safe default.##

Pink's answer isn't to fight the trough with willpower. It's to take breaks, and to take them seriously.

He describes what makes a **restorative break**. Something beats nothing: even short breaks help. Moving beats sitting: a short walk is better than scrolling at your desk. Social beats solo: a break with another person tends to restore more than one alone, as long as you choose the company. Outside beats inside: time in nature, even briefly, helps. And fully detached beats semi-detached: a break where you still check work messages isn't much of a break.

He also offers a trick for the afternoon slump that he calls the **nappuccino**. Drink a cup of coffee. Then immediately lie down for a short nap of ten to twenty minutes. Caffeine takes roughly that long to reach full effect, so it kicks in just as you wake up. And keeping the nap short prevents you from sinking into deep sleep and waking up groggy.

Pink's broader argument is that breaks aren't a sign of laziness or a luxury. They're a performance tool. For surgeons, judges, and anyone else whose afternoon decisions matter, a well-designed pause may be one of the smartest things they can do.

[[lessons]]
$$**The trough is real, and risky.** Anesthesia errors rose in the mid-afternoon. For most people, early to mid-afternoon is when mistakes become more likely. Knowing that, it makes sense to avoid scheduling your most important, error-sensitive work in that window, or to build extra checks around it.$$

$$**Breaks restore judgment.** The Israeli judges granted parole more often after food breaks, and less often as fatigue built up. Tired minds tend to fall back on the safest, easiest choice. A break doesn't just make you feel better; it can make you decide better.$$

$$**Design your breaks deliberately.** Pink's guidelines are simple: short and frequent, moving, social, outside and fully detached. The nappuccino adds a clever afternoon option. A break planned with the same care as a meeting is far more restorative than a few minutes scrolling at your desk.$$

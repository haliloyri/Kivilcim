Whether a prisoner is granted parole ought to depend on the case. A study of Israeli parole judges found it also depended on something else: how long it had been since the judges last stopped for a meal or a snack.

[[story]]
Daniel Pink's *When* treats the early-to-mid afternoon as a hidden danger zone. He calls that stretch of the day the trough, and he points to two studies that suggest it matters far beyond feeling a little sleepy.

The first comes from medicine. Researchers at Duke University Medical Center studied anesthesia-related errors and found that they were significantly more likely in procedures that began in the mid-afternoon than in those that began in the morning. What differed was the hour at which the procedure began.

The second comes from parole hearings in Israel, where a judge weighs a prisoner's case and decides whether to grant release. A study of the judges' decisions found that favorable rulings were most likely early in the day and right after meal breaks. As time passed without a break, they fell steeply.

##The same judges ruled differently depending on how long it had been since they last stopped.##

Pink's answer is the break, taken seriously. He calls it a restorative break, and he is specific about what that means. It is short and frequent. It works best when it involves movement, other people and time outdoors, and when you detach from work completely instead of checking your phone.

For the afternoon slump he offers a trick he calls the nappuccino. The recipe is simple. Drink a cup of coffee, then lie down immediately for a nap of ten to twenty minutes. The caffeine starts working around the time you wake up, and because the nap is short, you avoid the grogginess a longer one would leave.

Behind all of this sits Pink's larger point, and it applies to doctors, judges and everyone in between. Breaks are not a sign of laziness. They are a performance tool, and for people whose afternoon decisions matter, a well-planned pause may be among the smartest things they can do.

[[lessons]]
$$**Schedule around the dip.** If the trough raises the odds of mistakes, keep your most error-sensitive work out of that window, or add a second check to anything that has to happen then.$$

$$**A break is part of the decision.** The judges' rulings recovered after meal breaks. Treat a pause before a major decision as part of the process, planned like a meeting, not as time lost.$$

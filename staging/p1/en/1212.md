The founder of one of the most famous restaurant chains in the world asks a group of business students a simple question: what business am I in? They answer confidently. They're wrong. At least, that's how Robert Kiyosaki tells it.

[[story]]
In *Rich Dad Poor Dad*, Robert Kiyosaki tells a story he says he heard from a friend who was there.

Ray Kroc, the man who built McDonald's into a global giant, was invited to speak to an MBA class at the University of Texas at Austin. After the talk, some students invited him out for a drink, and he agreed.

At the bar, Kroc asked them a question.

> What business am I in?

The students laughed. It seemed obvious. Hamburgers, of course. McDonald's sold more burgers than anyone.

Kroc shook his head. No, he said. His business was real estate.

##The hamburgers, in this telling, were how McDonald's made its money flow. The land underneath was where it built lasting wealth.##

Kiyosaki explains the idea. Kroc understood that the key to his franchise system was owning the ground each restaurant stood on. Franchisees ran the restaurants and sold the food. But McDonald's often owned or controlled the property, and collected rent. Over the decades, McDonald's came to own some of the most valuable street corners in America and around the world. It's true that the company earns substantial income from rent paid by franchisees, and real estate is a major part of its value.

Kiyosaki uses the story to make one of the central points of his book, in a chapter he calls **"Mind your own business."** Most people, he argues, confuse their profession with their business. Your profession is how you earn a paycheck: nurse, engineer, teacher, manager. Your business, in his sense, is your **asset column**: the things you own that produce income for you, like investments, property or a business that runs without your constant presence.

Many people, he says, spend their whole lives minding other people's businesses: working hard for an employer, paying taxes, paying the bank, and never building assets of their own. Rich people, in his account, keep their jobs if they like, but also steadily build their asset column.

It's fair to note that this is a secondhand story, told by Kiyosaki from a friend's memory, and it's hard to verify exactly as told. But the point it illustrates doesn't depend on the details. What a business appears to sell isn't always what makes it wealthy. And what you do for a living isn't necessarily what builds your wealth.

[[lessons]]
$$**Look past what's on the menu.** Everyone saw McDonald's as a hamburger company. In Kroc's telling, the real long-term value was in the land beneath the restaurants. Asking "where does the lasting value actually come from?" can reveal a completely different picture of a business, including your own.$$

$$**Your job isn't your business.** Kiyosaki separates your profession, what you're paid to do, from your business, the assets you own that pay you. Many hardworking people have a strong profession and no business at all. Seeing that difference is the first step toward changing it.$$

$$**Build your asset column steadily.** "Mind your own business" doesn't mean quitting your job. It means building something alongside it: investments, a side project, property, over years. Small, consistent additions to that column are what eventually give you real choices that your paycheck alone never can.$$

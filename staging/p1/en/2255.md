Eleven to nothing after three innings, with 55,528 people in the stands and a nineteen-game winning streak under the home team. The scoreboard says the evening is a formality, a victory lap before the record. Six innings are still to be played, and the visitors still have their at-bats.

[[story]]
The Oakland Athletics began winning on 13 August 2002, in the season Michael Lewis's *Moneyball* follows, and had not lost since. Early in September Tejada's three-run walk-off homer beat Minnesota. On Labor Day they beat the Kansas City Royals 7-6 and tied the American League record of 19 straight wins, shared by the 1906 White Sox and the 1947 Yankees. On 4 September the Royals were back across from them, with the 20th win at stake.

The game was at Network Associates Coliseum. According to SABR's account the crowd was 55,528, the largest to see Oakland since the club moved from Kansas City in 1967. Tim Hudson started for Oakland and Paul Byrd for the Royals. Oakland scored six runs in the first. Mabry hit a home run in the second, making it 7-0, and four more runs came in the third. 11-0.

In the fourth the Royals scored five, 11-5. Hudson left in the seventh after 104 pitches, following singles by Sweeney and Ibañez. The eighth inning came apart. Tejada's throw home on Ordaz's grounder was short and Mayne scored, 11-6. Beltran's sacrifice fly made it 11-7. Then Sweeney hit a three-run home run, his 20th of the season: 11-10.

In the ninth, Alicea's single scored Pellow and the game was tied at 11-11. An eleven-run lead was gone.

Oakland got its last turn at bat. One out. Scott Hatteberg came up to pinch-hit for Eric Byrnes against Royals reliever Jason Grimsley. The first pitch was a low fastball, the second a high one. Hatteberg sent it into the right-field bleachers. 12-11, and it was his first walk-off home run.

Two days later Oakland lost 6-0 at Minnesota, and the streak ended.

Look at how the Royals' eleven runs were spread across the game: five in the fourth, five in the eighth, one in the ninth. The margin did not collapse at once. It shrank in two bursts, and by the ninth inning the tie was on the board. An eleven-run lead described the game's score at one moment, and it said nothing about what the rest of the game would hold. Hatteberg's swing only mattered because the game was still being played.

[[lessons]]
$$**When you hold a big lead, keep running the routine that built it until the last moment.** A small team that has all but landed a large contract may decide the first offer is so good that preparing for the later meetings can wait, and then an objection from the other side finds them unprepared. A lead gives you confidence, but it does not show that the job is finished, and an unfinished job still needs its checklist from start to end. Keeping the routine means that when part of the lead melts, you know what you still have and can recover in the final minutes.$$

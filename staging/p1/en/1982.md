[[open]]
A contestant stands beside a row of numbered briefcases, each hiding an unknown sum of money, and every case that is opened takes one possible prize off the board. Then the banker calls with an offer: a sure amount to walk away, or keep playing. Economic theory says the cases already opened should not change the answer.

[[story]]
Richard Thaler first met this idea at a smaller scale. With Eric Johnson he asked what happens when someone wins money early in an evening of gambling, and in his Nobel lecture he reports what they found. When people were ahead in the game, they became more willing to take risks on bets that could not wipe out all their earlier winnings. They called it the house money effect.

A footnote in that lecture adds that the hypothesis was later supported at high stakes using choices from the game show *Deal or No Deal*. The study behind it, by Thierry Post, Martijn van den Assem, Guido Baltussen and Thaler, appeared in the *American Economic Review* in 2008. The show began in the Netherlands and later spread around the world.

A game show suited the question. The decisions were simple and well defined, with real money on the line. At each point the contestant had a sure offer on one side and a gamble on the other, which made the choices easy to compare. The authors looked at them on television and in related classroom experiments.

The logic of the test is straightforward. A decision-maker who weighs only what lies ahead would look at the prizes still in play and at the offer, and nothing else. The cases already opened would count only through that remaining list. If the earlier opening also changes what the contestant does, then something besides the remaining prizes is steering the choice.

That is what the authors found. Choices were largely explained by outcomes the contestants had met earlier in the game. After early outcomes that shattered their hopes, contestants became less risk-averse. After early outcomes that exceeded their hopes, they again became less risk-averse. Two opposite histories pushed in the same direction: toward the gamble. The pattern after bad luck fits a break-even effect, and the pattern after good luck fits house money.

The authors conclude that this supports reference-dependent theories such as prospect theory. People judge gains and losses against a reference point, here the expectations built up as the game went on, and that point moves as the cases open. Path dependence, they write, matters even in simple choice problems with large real sums at stake.

[[lessons]]
$$**Judge each decision by what is still on the table.** What has already been won or lost can quietly change how much risk you will take next, though it cannot be recovered or enjoyed twice. A freelancer who loses a big client in the morning may accept a shaky project in the afternoon just to get back to even, and one who has a great morning may agree to anything. Before you decide, set the earlier result aside and ask whether you would take this same option if it were your first choice of the day.$$

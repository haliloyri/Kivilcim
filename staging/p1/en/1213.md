A young founder is working himself into the ground, answering every call and chasing every order. Then he sits down with a spreadsheet and discovers that almost everything he's been exhausting himself over barely matters. Tim Ferriss built part of a bestselling book on what he did next.

[[story]]
In *The 4-Hour Workweek*, Tim Ferriss describes a period when he was running his own company, selling a sports-nutrition supplement. On paper, it was going well. In reality, he was overwhelmed. He was working extremely long hours, constantly putting out fires, answering emails and dealing with customers. He felt trapped by the business he'd built.

Around that time, he came across the work of the economist Vilfredo Pareto and the idea now known as the 80/20 principle: that a small share of causes tends to produce most of the results.

Ferriss decided to test it on his own business. He looked at his wholesale customers, roughly 120 of them, and asked two questions. Which customers were producing most of my revenue? And which were producing most of my problems?

##The answer shocked him. About five of those 120 customers were generating around 95 percent of his revenue.##

At the same time, a small number of other customers were responsible for most of his headaches: constant complaints, demands, negotiations over small orders, late payments. He had been spending huge amounts of time and energy on people who contributed almost nothing to the business, and some who actively drained it.

So he made changes that felt radical at the time. He stopped chasing the low-value customers. For the most demanding and least profitable ones, he politely ended the relationship or changed his terms so the relationship worked for him. And he focused his energy on the handful of customers who mattered most, looking for ways to serve them better and to find more customers like them.

The results were striking. His revenue went up. His working hours went down dramatically. He had more money and far more time.

Ferriss draws a broader lesson from this. Much of what fills our days, he argues, feels productive but isn't. We confuse being busy with being effective. He sums it up in one line: **doing something unimportant well does not make it important.** Answering every email perfectly, serving every customer equally, attending every meeting: none of this matters if the effort goes into things that don't move the needle.

The first step, in his view, is elimination. Before trying to do things faster, stop doing the things that shouldn't be done at all.

[[lessons]]
$$**A few drive almost everything.** Five customers out of 120 produced about 95 percent of Ferriss's revenue. Most businesses and lives have a similar imbalance hiding in them. Simply measuring where results actually come from often reveals that you've been spreading effort evenly across very uneven value.$$

$$**Some customers cost more than they're worth.** A small group caused most of Ferriss's stress while bringing in little money. Letting them go, politely, freed time and energy for the customers who mattered. Saying goodbye to the wrong clients can be just as valuable as winning new ones.$$

$$**Unimportant work done well is still unimportant.** Ferriss's line cuts against the habit of polishing tasks that don't matter. Effort feels productive, but only effort on the right things produces results. Eliminating low-value work comes before trying to do anything faster or more efficiently.$$

[[reflect]]
- Which few clients, tasks or people produce most of your results?
- Which ones produce most of your stress, and how much do they actually contribute?
- What do you do very well that, honestly, doesn't need doing at all?

&&What could you stop doing, or who could you stop serving, to free up your best hours?&&

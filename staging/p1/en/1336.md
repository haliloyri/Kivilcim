Someone across from you is smiling and nodding. Their words say they're happy to keep talking. Meanwhile, under the table, their feet have slowly turned toward the door. Which part of that person should you believe? A former FBI agent has an answer, and it comes with a warning attached.

[[story]]
Joe Navarro spent his career as an FBI agent interviewing suspects, and *What Every BODY Is Saying* is built on what he learned from watching people's nonverbal behavior.

His explanation starts with what he calls the limbic brain. In his model, this is the part of us that reacts to threat with freeze, flight or fight responses before conscious control catches up. Those reactions show up in the body. Navarro offers this as a simplified framework rather than a full map of the brain, but it gives him a way to read what the body does while the words are carefully chosen.

People put real effort into managing their faces. We learn to smile on cue and keep our expressions in line. ##That's why Navarro calls the feet and legs the most honest part of the body: people learn to control their faces, but rarely their feet.## Feet turning toward an exit, for example, can signal a wish to leave, even while the face stays friendly.

The feet aren't the only clue. Navarro describes the shoulders rising and the neck seeming to shrink, a "turtle" effect, when someone is uncomfortable. He also points to touching the neck as a way people soothe themselves.

Then comes the warning, and it matters as much as the cues. These signals show comfort or discomfort. They don't show lying. In Navarro's view there is no single sign of deception, and treating the feet as a lie detector gets the method wrong.

Reading them well takes two more steps. First, compare against the person's baseline: how they normally sit, stand and move when relaxed. A habit isn't a message. Second, look for clusters. One cue on its own means little. Several changing together, at a particular moment in a conversation, are worth noticing.

So, back to the smiling person with feet aimed at the door. The feet may be the more honest signal. But what they're honest about is discomfort, not deceit, and the useful response is a question, not a conclusion.

[[lessons]]
$$**Watch what people don't think to control.** Faces are managed; feet usually aren't. That's Navarro's case for the feet and legs as the most honest part of the body. When the face and the feet disagree, the feet are worth a second look.$$

$$**Discomfort isn't deception.** Navarro insists these cues reveal comfort or discomfort, not lies, and that there's no single sign of deception. Feet pointing at the exit might mean someone is uneasy with the topic or simply wants to leave. Treating them as proof of a lie turns a useful signal into an accusation.$$

$$**Read against a baseline, in clusters.** A single gesture tells you little on its own. Navarro's method is to know how someone normally behaves, then watch for several cues shifting together, like feet turning, shoulders rising and a hand going to the neck at the same moment in a conversation.$$

[[reflect]]
- When did someone's words say yes while their body said no?
- What do your own feet usually do when you want a conversation to end?
- How often do you read a single gesture as a verdict about someone?

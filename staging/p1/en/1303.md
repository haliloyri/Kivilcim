A turkey has been fed every single day for a thousand days. Each morning the food arrives, and each morning the evidence gets stronger. By any measure the turkey can use, the people looking after it have never been kinder. Then comes one particular Wednesday.

[[story]]
The turkey is Nassim Nicholas Taleb's central illustration in *The Black Swan*. It is a thought experiment, not a real event, adapted from a chicken that Bertrand Russell used to make the same point long before.

Follow the turkey's reasoning. On day one, a human brings food. On day two, it happens again. Every feeding is one more data point, and every data point says the same thing: humans are friendly, and they look after the turkey's interests.

By day one hundred, the belief feels solid. By day five hundred, it feels like a law of nature. After a thousand days of evidence, the turkey's confidence is at its highest.

And that is exactly when the risk is greatest. On the Wednesday before Thanksgiving, something unexpected happens to the turkey.

##The turkey's confidence peaks on the very day its danger peaks.##

The point Taleb draws from this is known as the problem of induction. Past observations, however many you collect, cannot prove what will happen next. A thousand friendly feedings do not guarantee a thousand and first. Worse, in the turkey's case, the long record of safety did not merely fail to warn it. It made the turkey more certain at the worst possible moment.

Taleb then adds a detail that changes how the whole story reads. The event is a Black Swan for the turkey. It is not a Black Swan for the butcher.

The butcher knew the date all along. The feeding was never evidence of kindness from the butcher's side; it was part of a plan. The same thousand days that looked like proof of safety to one party looked like preparation to the other. So surprise is not a property of the event alone. It depends on what you know.

Taleb uses "Black Swan" for events with three features: they are rare, they have a large impact, and they seem obvious only in hindsight. Once Thanksgiving has passed, anyone can explain what happened to the turkey. Before it, the turkey's data said the opposite.

Black Swans can also be positive, a rare event that brings an enormous benefit. What they share is not good or bad luck. It is that the past gave little sign of them, and the explanation arrived only afterward.

[[lessons]]
$$**A long safe record can hide rising risk.** On day 1,000 the turkey had its most evidence and its most confidence, and it was in its greatest danger. When something has always worked, the lack of trouble feels like proof. Taleb's turkey shows it can simply mean the trouble hasn't arrived yet.$$

$$**Surprise depends on who's looking.** The same Wednesday is a shock to the turkey and a routine date to the butcher. If an outcome would stun you, it's worth asking whether someone else, with different information, would see it coming. Their view may be the one you're missing.$$

$$**Past data can't prove the future.** Russell's chicken and Taleb's turkey make the same point about induction: counting confirmations doesn't turn a pattern into a guarantee. More observations can make you more confident without making you more right, which is exactly the turkey's position on day 1,000.$$

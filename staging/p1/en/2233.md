In a speeding Johannesburg minibus, the driver is still lecturing the woman sitting in the back seat. She says a few words, nothing remarkable at all, and he realises she speaks Xhosa. His tone changes at once, and ethnic stereotypes start to pour out of him.

[[story]]
Trevor Noah's mother was singled out by a minibus driver because of the language she spoke. The scene appears in the memoir excerpt CBS News published: when the driver heard her speaking Xhosa, he threw ethnic stereotypes about Xhosa women at her. It shows how little it can take, one sentence in a language, for a stranger to pass judgement on a person.

In a radio interview, Noah explained what his mother had taught him about this. She taught him to code-switch, to adjust his words, tone and accent depending on who he was speaking with. He calls it a survival skill and describes language as "a powerful, powerful tool." He speaks six languages himself: English, Afrikaans, Zulu, Xhosa, Tswana and Tsonga, plus conversational German.

Noah argues that accents shape how people judge one another. Hearing someone speak your own language with an accent can feel like an intrusion, and he suggests this is part of what fuels hostility towards immigrants. Language is a signal of belonging before it is a vehicle for meaning, and a listener often senses whether a speaker is "one of us" before understanding a word.

To make code-switching concrete, think of one person who speaks one language at a market, another in an office and a third at home, adjusting not just the words but the pace and the voice each time. That is the skill Noah describes: knowing languages is one thing, knowing which one works in which room is another.

Two conclusions follow, and both come from the same fact. First, if language does not protect you, it can expose you: the driver passed judgement as soon as he heard Xhosa. Second, a person who knows the power of language can use it deliberately. That is what his mother passed on to him: it is not enough to know your own language, you also need to know how to stand in somebody else's.

The striking thing is that a single ability cuts both ways. Being multilingual looks like a privilege, but what Noah describes was learned as a tool for safety. Once language becomes part of staying safe, it stops being a school subject and turns into an everyday skill.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you ask for something, learn to speak in the language and tone the other person trusts.** People place you in a category through your words, your accent and your tone, and that first impression sets the direction of the conversation. Noah's mother taught him he could manage that category. In a meeting, with a client or with a neighbour, first listen to the words the other person uses, then bring your own phrasing closer to theirs, and watch how you are received. The aim is to put the other person at ease, never to play a role, and that small adjustment often opens more doors than a long explanation.$$

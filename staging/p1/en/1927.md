[[open]]
A money manager is certain that the world's banks are about to fall. The crisis he predicted does arrive: a country stops paying its debts. And still the markets climb. Before long he is asking his father for a loan to cover the household bills.

[[story]]
Ray Dalio's *Principles: Life and Work* grew out of one failure, and this story is about that failure. Dalio had founded his firm, Bridgewater, in 1975. Around 1980 or 1981 he reached a conclusion few people around him shared: American banks had lent so much money abroad that it could never be repaid. He expected a major debt crisis and placed his bets accordingly.

In 1982 Mexico defaulted, which looked exactly like the start of what he had predicted. Other countries ran into trouble with their debts as well. But the collapse he was waiting for did not follow. The stock market found its bottom, monetary policy was eased, and prices began to rise. Dalio has said he could not have been more wrong, and the bet cost him dearly.

The damage was personal. By his own account he was so broke that he borrowed 4,000 dollars from his father to cover household costs. According to Stanford's business school, he also let go of his entire Bridgewater team.

Look at the shape of the mistake. Dalio had not misread the facts, because Mexico really did default. What he got wrong was what those facts would cause. He was also wrong in the way people are wrong when they feel certain: nobody in the room had the job of doubting him. Later he said he had been arrogant before that, and that the failure gave him the humility he needed to balance his audacity.

What turned a terrible stretch into a method was his answer to it. He began writing down the criteria behind his decisions, so that he could test them against what actually happened. Afterwards he hired people who would disagree with him. Those written criteria grew, over the years, into the principles Bridgewater is built on and into the book that carries their name.

Notice the order of events. First came the certainty, then the loss, then the rule that certainty alone is never a reason to act. A loss that costs you your staff teaches faster than any advice, but the lesson only lasts if it is written down and someone in the room is free to push back.

[[lessons]]
$$**Be most careful exactly when you feel most certain.** Certainty tells you how strongly you believe something and says nothing about whether it is true, so a conviction that cannot be questioned is a risk, whether it concerns a budget, a hire or a family decision. Check the stakes as well: the larger the bet, the more doubt it deserves. Write down why you believe what you believe, and name one person whose job is to find the hole in it. Then, when the facts arrive and surprise you, you have a record to learn from instead of a feeling to defend, and the cost of being wrong stays small enough to survive.$$

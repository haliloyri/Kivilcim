In western Kenya, on the slope of a mountain, a cave mouth opens in the rock. The inside is dark, and a great many bats live in the ceiling. The people who walk in notice nothing that day. Days later an illness will begin, and the question of where it came from will stay unanswered for years.

[[story]]
For a place that most readers will never see, Kitum Cave has an outsized reputation. It lies inside Mount Elgon National Park in Kenya, and it features in Richard Preston's *The Hot Zone*. In the medical literature the same cave appears twice, seven years apart, as the place where a serious illness began, and in both cases the disease was Marburg virus.

The first case came in 1980. The man whom Preston calls Charles Monet had entered Kitum Cave about two weeks before his symptoms began. The case was traced afterwards, in a retrospective investigation. A doctor who tried to resuscitate the patient fell ill himself and recovered. The reports also stress that the cave holds a large population of bats.

The second case came in 1987, this time to a 15-year-old Danish boy. He too had visited Kitum Cave, and he died of the illness. A new Marburg virus, later known as Ravn virus, was isolated from the fatal case. Investigators suspected contact with bats or exposure to bat droppings. Samples were taken from several animal species in and around the cave, and the analysis found no filovirus.

The striking part is that the answer stayed incomplete. Two serious cases pointed to the same cave, yet the samples did not turn up the virus. That did not mean nothing was in the cave. It meant only that the samples taken so far had not answered the question. Scientists had two clear clues and no evidence joining them.

In a situation like this there are two traps. One is to say "we found nothing, so it is not there" and close the file. The other is to say "it is certainly the bats" without evidence. The right path runs between them: write down what was found, what was ruled out and which question remains open, then keep looking.

What the two cases at Kitum teach is that an unanswered question is itself a piece of knowledge. Someone who knows which samples were taken and which were not starts the next investigation one step ahead, not from zero. A question left on record becomes a map for whoever eventually finds the answer.

There is one more point here. Two cases pointing at the same cave were not proof on their own. Two cases gave a pattern, and the pattern gave a hypothesis. A hypothesis that has not been confirmed has not been refuted either, and scientists had to carry that uncertainty honestly.

[[lessons]]
$$**When an investigation turns up nothing, write down what you ruled out and leave the question open.** At Kitum two cases pointed to the same cave, yet the samples showed no virus, and that was a reason to search more carefully, not to close the file. The same holds when you cannot find the cause of a customer complaint or of a fault that keeps returning. Writing "we tried this, we found nothing, the question is still open" saves the next person from starting at zero and keeps you from a false certainty. An honest 'we do not know' is always worth more than a false 'we know'.$$

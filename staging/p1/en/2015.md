[[open]]
In a German laboratory one summer, a clear liquid falls drop by drop into a vessel at about 125 millilitres an hour. Its raw material is air. The rate is slow enough that you could count the drops, and the sight of it gives no hint of fields or factories.

[[story]]
Michael Pollan observes that the fields of Iowa look bare and black from October to April, a landscape he puts down to cheap oil. What those fields run on goes back to a laboratory in the summer of 1909, where the German chemist Fritz Haber and his assistant Robert Le Rossignol made ammonia from air. Published accounts put the rate at about 125 millilitres an hour. *The Omnivore's Dilemma* returns to Haber when it explains where a cornfield's nitrogen comes from.

Crops need fixed nitrogen, and until then fields had taken most of theirs from soil bacteria living on the roots of legumes. BASF bought the process and put Carl Bosch to work scaling it from a tabletop rig to a factory. He succeeded in 1910. The first industrial plant, at Oppau, began production in 1913 and reached 20 tonnes a day in 1914.

War arrived. The Allied blockade cut off Germany's supply of nitrate, and synthetic ammonia became the raw material for explosives. Haber won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for 1918, awarded in 1919, and Bosch won his in 1931.

The same chemistry went back to the field. In his "Farmer in Chief" article, Pollan writes that after the Second World War the American government encouraged munitions plants to convert to fertilizer, since ammonium nitrate is the main ingredient in both bombs and chemical fertilizer. Nitrogen fertilizer today is made from natural gas, and corn, which Pollan calls the greediest of plants, demands more of it than any other crop. Pollan also writes that pesticides are made from oil, so a cornfield draws on the fossil-fuel supply twice over.

Pollan gives the arithmetic. In 1940 the food system produced 2.3 calories of food energy for every calorie of fossil-fuel energy it used. Today it takes 10 calories of fossil-fuel energy to produce one calorie of supermarket food, and the food system uses 19 percent of the economy's fossil fuel, second only to cars.

The typical corn-belt farmer, he adds, feeds 140 people. Corn and soy dominate American fields on subsidies paid by the bushel. A trickle of 125 millilitres an hour in one lab is where that dependency begins. Pollan adds that 40 percent of the world's grain output is fed to animals.

[[lessons]]
$$**Write down what an efficiency gain runs on.** A workshop can double its output with an automatic machine, yet if the machine works only with one spare part, one supplier and uninterrupted power, the gain depends on all three. Those dependencies stay invisible in daily work, and they are the part that never appears on the invoice, because nobody looks at them while things go well. Listing them in advance shows which item would stop the work if it went missing, and it lets you weigh the price of speed before you pay it and prepare a solid backup route while the machine still runs.$$

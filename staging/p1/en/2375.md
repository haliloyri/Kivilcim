[[open]]
On a pig farm in northern Malaysia, workers who spend their days among the animals begin to collapse with fever, headaches and brain inflammation. Doctors see a disease they know well and treat it that way. More workers keep falling ill, and the name on the chart never quite explains why.

[[story]]
The pig farms of Perak state were where it started, in 1998. Farm workers developed encephalitis, an inflammation of the brain, and the cases were first diagnosed as Japanese encephalitis, a mosquito-borne disease well known in the region. By late 1998 about ten workers from villages near the city of Ipoh had died, and a Malaysian task force was formed.

The illness kept moving with the pigs. In December 1998 infected animals carried it to Sikamat, about 60 kilometres south of Kuala Lumpur. In March 1999 it reached Bukit Pelandok, a major pig-producing area in the south of the country, and the number of human cases rose sharply. Pigs were also sick, and some horses, dogs, cats and goats were affected as well.

In March 1999, Dr Chua Kaw Bing of the University of Malaya isolated the virus. It was not the Japanese encephalitis virus. It was a new one, and the earlier human cases were recognised as Nipah only in hindsight. The virus was named after Sungai Nipah, one of the villages where people had fallen ill. A team from the US Centers for Disease Control was working in Malaysia, and scientists from CSIRO in Australia, Dr Peter Daniels and Dr John White, travelled to join them.

Between 28 February and 26 April 1999, more than 900,000 pigs from the affected areas were culled. Over the whole outbreak, which ran from September 1998 to May 1999, 265 people developed encephalitis and 105 of them died, according to the figures published by CSIRO and the CDC.

How the virus reached the pigs in the first place is something researchers later proposed an explanation for. Malaysian pathologists Looi and Chua suggested in 2007 that fruit bats, flying foxes, passed it to pigs in late 1997 or early 1998 by contaminating pig swill, after drought and fires in Indonesia disrupted the bats' food supply and drove them to orchards and pig farms.

The first diagnosis was reasonable, because Japanese encephalitis was a real disease in the region. What settled the matter was a scientist isolating the virus from patients instead of trusting the name already on the file.

[[lessons]]
$$**Reopen the first diagnosis when the pattern keeps breaking it.** A mechanic who has replaced the same part twice and still hears the same noise has a choice between a third part and a fresh look at what is really wrong. A label saves effort only while the facts fit it, and each case that does not fit is evidence for a different explanation. The same goes for a forecast that keeps missing by the same margin. Write down what the label predicted and what actually happened, and when the two keep drifting apart, test the label itself.$$

Employees are handed a single question on a page: out of all your knowledge, skills, talent and insight, how much of it is your boss actually getting? The answer is a percentage. It can be low, it can be high, and a few people write a number above one hundred.

[[story]]
The question came out of Liz Wiseman's years at Oracle, where she had seen brilliant leaders either fill a room with intelligence or drain it out. To find out how common each type was, she and Greg McKeown studied more than 150 leaders. Stanford's business school describes the project this way, and sources that repeat Wiseman's own account say the leaders came from about 35 companies on four continents.

The people who worked for those leaders gave a number. According to Wiseman, employees of the ones she calls Multipliers said their boss got about 95 percent of their intelligence and capability. Employees of the ones she calls Diminishers said about 48 percent. A few respondents went further and said they gave a Multiplier 120 percent, because they grew from working for that person.

The gap is close to twofold, and Wiseman calls it a 2x effect. Divide 95 by 48 and you get roughly 1.98. It means that two managers with equal budgets and equally capable people could draw very different amounts from them.

Wiseman reports the same pattern outside business. In schools, she says, principals and superintendents received about 40 percent of teachers' capability. She also says the diminishing effect was deeper in regions with strong hierarchy, such as Asia, Latin America and the Middle East.

The numbers have a limit that is worth stating plainly. They are estimates people gave about their own boss, not output measured by anyone else. That does not make them meaningless, because a person is well placed to know how much of themselves they bring to work. It does mean the 48 and the 95 describe how much effort people believe they give, not a stopwatch reading of what they produced.

Even so, the result has a hard edge for anyone who manages people. Salaries are the same whether a team gives half of its ability or nearly all of it. The only visible difference sits in the manager's behavior, and Wiseman's research points to five disciplines that separate the two groups, which she argues can be learned.

The finding, in the end, is a question rather than a verdict. Wiseman did not ask who the smartest leaders were. She asked the people around them how much of themselves they were using.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask the people around you how much of their ability you are using.** Most managers judge their team by results and never ask what stayed unused. One honest question in a one-on-one, such as "what are you holding back in our meetings?", gives you a number you cannot get from a dashboard. Answers will be estimates, but they show where you stand and where to start, because a team's unused capacity is usually the cheapest improvement available. Ask once, then listen without defending yourself, and the next answer will be franker than the first. Do it before a project starts, not after it stalls.$$

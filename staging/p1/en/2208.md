[[open]]
A weekly science journal prints three papers on DNA in a row. The first runs barely two pages and proposes a structure. The other two hold the X-ray evidence, and one of them is by a woman who has just moved to a different college.

[[story]]
On 25 April 1953, volume 171 of *Nature* carried the announcement that James Watson recounts in *The Double Helix*, his 1968 memoir. Watson and Francis Crick's paper, "Molecular Structure of Nucleic Acids: A Structure for Deoxyribose Nucleic Acid," filled pages 737 and 738. It proposed the double helix, and it ended with a restrained line: "the specific pairing we have postulated immediately suggests a possible copying mechanism for the genetic material."

Two further papers followed in the same issue. Maurice Wilkins, Alexander Stokes and Herbert Wilson published "Molecular structure of deoxypentose nucleic acids" on pages 738 to 740. Rosalind Franklin and Raymond Gosling published "Molecular configuration in sodium thymonucleate" on pages 740 and 741. Both papers presented X-ray data from King's College London, the evidence the structure had to agree with.

Watson and Crick said in their paper that they had been stimulated by the unpublished results and ideas of Wilkins, Franklin and their colleagues at King's. Their acknowledgement is often described as terse. According to one biography of Crick, Wilkins had declined an offer to be a co-author.

Franklin's own position had changed by then. She left King's for Birkbeck in the middle of March 1953. Among her papers, Aaron Klug later found a draft dated 17 March 1953, shortly before the Cambridge news reached King's, which Klug read as showing that she had already worked out the double-helical form of B-DNA. So the published record has her as the author of one of two papers that gave the structure its experimental footing.

She died of cancer on 16 April 1958, aged 37. The 1962 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine went to Crick, Watson and Wilkins. Watson's book appeared in 1968, ten years after her death, and the same year Klug wrote a reply arguing that the book gave an incomplete picture of her work.

What can be said without taking sides is narrow but firm. Three papers appeared together, her data was in one of them, and the structure was credited to two people in a third. How much each contribution weighed is argued over, and part of what is known about the people comes from Watson's own account, which others have disputed.

[[lessons]]
$$**Name what each person contributed, not only that they helped.** A general thank-you tells your audience that someone was involved, and it leaves out which piece of the result came from them. When you present a team result, a line such as "the pricing model is hers, and the customer interviews are his" lets listeners see the real shape of the work, and each person hears their own part named. Written credit also outlasts the meeting and gets repeated by people who were never there. Specific credit costs one sentence, and it is the version people can quote accurately later on.$$

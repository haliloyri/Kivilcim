[[open]]
Picture someone keeping every door open. Three half-started projects, two possible careers, a few options held just in case. Nothing has been ruled out, and that feels like freedom. But ask a simple question: in any one of those paths, how far have they actually been able to go?

[[story]]
Mark Manson takes aim at that feeling in Everything Is F*cked: A Book About Hope. He draws a line between two kinds of freedom. One is the freedom of endless options, of never having to rule anything out. He treats this as a shallow, even fake, version. The other is deeper, and it comes from choosing what to give up.

The shallow version is easy to mistake for the real thing. Keeping every option open feels like keeping your life flexible. Nobody can tell you that you've made the wrong choice, because you haven't really made one. Every possibility is still technically available.

The problem, Manson argues, is what that does to depth. If you keep every option open, you can't go deep in any single relationship, career or pursuit. Depth takes sustained attention, and attention spread across every door gets thin in all of them. You end up with many beginnings and few middles.

So commitment has to involve loss. Choosing one path means closing off others, really closing them, not just setting them aside for later. That's why commitment can feel bad at the moment you make it. Something you could have had is now gone.

##In Manson's argument, that loss isn't a flaw in commitment; it's the price of depth and meaning.## You don't get the second without paying the first. A relationship, a craft or a career develops only when you stop treating it as one option among many.

This turns the usual picture of freedom around. Freedom in the shallow sense is how many doors you have. In the deeper sense Manson describes, it's being able to limit yourself: to decide which doors you're willing to close so you can actually walk through one.

The alternative isn't a life without losses. It's a life where the loss shows up somewhere less visible: in the depth you never reach.

[[lessons]]
$$**Options can pose as freedom.** Keeping every door open feels flexible and safe, because nothing has been ruled out. Manson calls this a shallow kind of freedom. It protects you from choosing wrong, but it also stops any single relationship, career or pursuit from getting your full attention long enough to grow deep. Having many doors is not the same as being free to walk through one.$$

$$**The loss is what you pay for depth.** Commitment closes off alternatives, and that stings, because something you could have had is gone. In Manson's argument that loss isn't a sign you chose badly. It's the cost of going deep in one place, and no version of depth avoids paying it. Accepting the loss up front is part of the deeper freedom he describes: choosing your limits instead of refusing to have any.$$

A physicist sits down to write a book for ordinary readers about the universe, from the Big Bang to black holes. Someone warns him that every equation he includes will cut the sales in half. He decides to put in none at all. In the end, one gets through.

[[story]]
The physicist is Stephen Hawking, and the book is *A Brief History of Time*. In the acknowledgments he tells the story himself: someone had told him that each equation in the book would halve its sales, so he resolved not to have any equations at all. In the end, he writes, he did put in one, Einstein's famous E = mc². Morgan Housel cites the episode in *Same as Ever*, a book about how little human nature changes.

Note first what the warning really says. It does not claim that equations are bad physics. It says that each one costs readers, and that the cost compounds. A reader who meets a first equation may carry on. A reader who meets five has quite possibly closed the book at the second.

Hawking took the warning seriously enough to adopt a rule: zero. A rule that strict has a practical advantage. A rule like that spares a writer from arguing, equation by equation, over whether each one is worth it, because the answer is already decided. It also means everything has to be carried by words, which is a demanding standard when the subject is the universe.

Then he broke his own rule once. The point of interest is that the exception was deliberate, one single equation in a whole book about the physical universe. Not two, and not "a few for the keen readers." When you allow exactly one, you must pick it carefully, and the one he picked is among the most recognisable formulas in physics, a line many people who never studied the subject have already seen.

The story is small, and it would be wrong to pretend it proves more than it does. It does not show that simple books always sell or that complexity is always a mistake. It shows a writer counting the price of difficulty before adding any, and then paying that price only where it bought something.

Housel's broader theme is that complexity tends to feel clever to the person who produces it, while the person who receives it pays for it with attention. Hawking, at least in this one decision, treated the reader's attention as a budget that he had no right to spend carelessly.

[[lessons]]
$$**Put a price on each complication before you add it, and keep only the ones that earn their place.** Every extra slide, clause, feature or step in a process asks something of the person on the other end, and the cost compounds quickly. Setting a hard limit, such as one chart per page or three points per email, saves you from arguing about each addition separately. When you do break your own limit, do it once and on purpose, for the piece that carries the most weight. What remains is easier to read, easier to remember and far easier to act on.$$

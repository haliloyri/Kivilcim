[[open]]
A red double-decker waits at a London stop. The conductor swings up the stairs to collect fares, then down again, then up. The driver stays in his seat with both hands on the wheel. Same bus, same route, same day. By evening one of them has climbed those stairs dozens of times, and the other has barely stood up.

[[story]]
After the Second World War, heart disease was climbing in Britain, and the epidemiologist Jerry Morris wanted to know whether a person's job had anything to do with it. Published accounts say he picked London's transport workers because their jobs differed so sharply and because full-time employees left good medical records behind.

The comparison was almost too tidy. Drivers of double-decker buses sat for the whole shift. Their conductors walked the aisles and climbed the stairs again and again. Morris and his colleagues went through sickness absences, doctors' and hospital records, and death certificates to see who developed coronary heart disease, and when.

In November 1953 the Lancet published the results under the title "Coronary heart-disease and physical activity of work." Conductors had less coronary heart disease than drivers, and it showed up later in life. When conductors did fall ill, it was mostly with angina, the milder form, and fewer of them died early. By one published account, immediate deaths from heart attack were more than twice as common among drivers.

Morris then went looking for a second pair. Postmen who walked their rounds had less coronary heart disease than the telephonists and other office staff who sat all day. Two different workplaces, the same direction.

The study has limits, and later writers have named them. It measured activity at work only. The groups were not perfectly matched on things like social class, smoking and diet, and some researchers have argued that the gap may reflect how much the drivers sat as much as how much the conductors moved.

Nobody assigned these men to sitting or climbing. They were already in their jobs, so what came out was an observation rather than an experiment, and it called for a careful reading rather than a victory lap. Whatever the explanation, the pairing of the two jobs on one vehicle gave the question a fair shape.

Still, the logic of the design is hard to shake. One bus, one set of passengers, one big difference in what the body did all day. Morris did not discover a treatment. He found a pattern: the jobs with more movement sat next to less heart disease and a later start. Daniel Lieberman's *Exercised* takes up the question behind that pattern, why a modern body that sits so much runs into trouble.

[[lessons]]
$$**When you want to understand a difference, put two cases side by side where only one thing changes.** Morris could point at sitting versus climbing because both groups worked on the same buses, so the usual excuses had less room. You can borrow the idea at work: compare two teams, two processes or two of your own weeks that share almost everything except one factor. The cleaner the pair, the more your conclusion deserves trust, and you are less likely to be fooled by a result you were hoping for. Even a clean pair needs honesty about what it left out, as Morris's critics showed.$$

At a bank counter, a customer who has come to open an account is handed a thin booklet. The cover has no chart and no table of rates. Inside, merchants of a city thousands of years gone talk about gold and the purse. The man who wrote it spent his working life drawing maps, not counting money.

[[story]]
George S. Clason was born in Missouri in 1874. As a young man he served in the US Army during the Spanish-American War, and in 1900 he moved to Denver. There he started the Clason Map Company and a publishing house. Biographies say the map company produced the first road atlas of the United States and Canada. His trade, in other words, was showing people the way.

The map company did not survive the Great Depression. That detail should not be skipped. A man who is now remembered for advice on saving saw his own business go under in the economic collapse. What he is remembered for is a different line of work altogether.

Clason wrote short pieces on thrift and financial success and told every one of them through parables set in ancient Babylon. According to published accounts, banks and insurance companies handed these booklets out. Look at the logic of that. The reader met the idea in the very place where the decision was made, at the bank where the money would go in or the desk where the policy would be signed. The text did not read like a balance sheet, either. It was built from short stories, and a story is the oldest way people have of keeping a rule in their heads. The pieces were gathered into a book in 1926 under the title *The Richest Man in Babylon*, and in 1930 Clason also published an illustrated hardback edition.

The best-known advice in the book is to set aside a tenth of what you earn. The line is quoted like this: "For every ten coins thou placest within thy purse take out for use but nine." Clason is also credited with coining the phrase "pay yourself first."

What strikes you is how small the rule is. A tenth is a ratio you can work out without a calculator. A child can do it, and a shop assistant can remember it. Clason's chosen form fits: one sentence with stories circling around it.

A map serves one day and one road, and it goes out of date once new roads are built. A saving rule stays the same whatever the roads do: part of the income is set aside first. Clason died in Napa, California, in 1957. The map company was gone by then, and what he left was a book that is still in print.

[[lessons]]
$$**To pass a money habit on to someone, fit it into one memorable sentence and a short story, then put it next to the moment of decision.** Clason chose Babylonian parables over a long financial plan, and he sent them out from places people already visited: the bank and the insurance office. If you want to talk to your child or your team about saving, a short rule and a small story will outlive a long explanation. Give the advice at the desk where the money decision is made, and it has a better chance of being used. Then test it with one question: could the listener repeat the sentence tomorrow?$$

[[open]]
Hundreds of millions of tweets, posted by people in many countries who were only saying what was on their minds. None of them meant to take part in a study. Yet together they left a record of the same thing happening to their mood, at roughly the same times, every day. And that daily rhythm turns out to matter for which kind of work you should do when.

[[story]]
Daniel Pink starts from this data in *When*. Researchers analysed the tweets for signs of positive mood and plotted them against the time of day.

The pattern was strikingly regular. Positive mood rose in the morning. It dipped in the afternoon. Then it rose again in the evening.

Pink sums up the day in three parts: a peak, a trough and a recovery. For most people, that's the order. For night owls, the order runs roughly in reverse.

The natural next question is which part of the day is best for work. Pink's answer is that it depends on the work.

During the peak, people are best at analytical tasks. These are the jobs that need focus and the ability to ignore distractions: checking figures, writing a careful argument, working through a detailed problem. The mind is alert and it stays on track.

But Pink points to a study by Mareike Wieth and Rose Zacks that complicates the picture. They gave people insight problems, the kind of puzzle that isn't solved by grinding through steps but by suddenly seeing it a different way. People solved these better at their non-optimal time of day.

At first that sounds backwards. Why would you think better when you're less sharp?

##The slight lack of focus was the advantage: a less vigilant mind let unexpected connections through.##

At your peak, you are good at filtering things out, and that is exactly what an insight problem doesn't need. When your focus loosens a little, stray ideas get in, and sometimes one of them is the answer.

So Pink's advice is not simply to find your most productive hour. First, work out your chronotype, whether you run more like a lark or an owl. Then match the type of task to the time. Put analytical work in your peak. Put insight work, the open-ended and creative problems, in your recovery period. And where you can, keep important work out of the trough.

The result is a more useful question than the one most people ask. Instead of when you are at your best, it becomes: at your best for what? The same person can be sharpest for a spreadsheet at nine in the morning and better placed to crack an open problem in the early evening.

[[lessons]]
$$**Different work wants different hours.** In Pink's account, analytical tasks go best at the daily peak, while insight problems went better off-peak in the Wieth and Zacks study. Asking when you're most productive misses this. A better question is which kind of task each part of your day suits.$$

$$**A slightly foggy mind has its uses.** People solved insight problems better when they were less alert, because a loosened focus let unexpected connections in. The hours you think of as second-rate may be the right ones for a problem that needs a new angle rather than more effort.$$

$$**Your rhythm may not be everyone's.** Pink notes that the peak, trough and recovery order runs roughly in reverse for night owls. A team schedule built around the morning may put some people's sharpest thinking in their worst hours. Check which order your own day runs in before borrowing anyone else's routine.$$

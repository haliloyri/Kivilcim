A telegram arrives: "Return to Moscow at once." For a man who has just been promoted, it can mean a reward or a trap. Without knowing what the night holds, he chooses to find out which it is by going there on his own decision.

[[story]]
Oleg Gordievsky, the central figure in Ben Macintyre's *The Spy and the Traitor*, was at the peak of his career in the spring of 1985. In late April he had been named head of the KGB station in London, meaning that at the next step he would take the top post there. At the same time he was working for British intelligence, MI6. The promotion meant both safety and risk for him.

On 16 May a telegram arrived from Moscow, recalling him. It could be a promotion meeting. It could also be suspicion. Gordievsky could not know. The British gave him two options: stay in Britain and defect, or return to Moscow. They left the choice to him.

On 19 May Gordievsky chose to go to Moscow. When he arrived, the KGB was waiting for him at a safe house. They drugged him and questioned him for about five hours. Under the questions they were trying to work out whether he was hiding something and who he was working for. In the end they let him go, but with one message: he would never again work abroad.

The decision was dangerous on one side. On the other, it was the only logical option for Gordievsky. If he had run, the KGB would have been certain of his guilt, and going back was a chance to dispel suspicion. There were also the people he left behind, and his own future.

What is interesting about the decision is that walking toward the danger of his own accord left him options. Going back was not closing the door; it was keeping it ajar. He was still free, he could still make contact with the British, and the escape plan was still in place. A few months later, in July, that plan was put into action.

Notice how the order of events matters. A man who has fled has only one story left to tell, and it is the one his pursuers have already written. A man who goes back and answers the questions is still the author of his own account, even when he is frightened.

[[lessons]]
$$**Facing danger can keep your options open rather than shut them, as long as you make the decision and do not let the decision make you.** Avoiding a hard meeting, hiding a mistake or skipping a crisis call grows suspicion and shrinks the options you hold. Going of your own accord lets you own the story and choose the next step. First pin down what you are risking and which fallback plan stands behind you, and then go. Keeping a door ajar is often worth more than either bolting it or walking out through it.$$

Try sitting on a stool with only two legs. You can manage for a while if you keep shifting your weight and never quite relax. Sooner or later, you go over. Arianna Huffington thinks that's how most of us have built our idea of success, and she has a clear view of which two legs we picked.

[[story]]
The two legs are money and power. They're what people usually point to when they say someone is doing well: the income, the title, the influence. Huffington's argument isn't that these don't count. They are, after all, legs of the stool. Her point is that two legs can't hold anyone up for long. A stool with two legs isn't a slightly weaker stool. Sooner or later, you fall off.

So she adds a third. She calls it the third metric of success, and it's made up of four elements: well-being, wisdom, wonder and giving. The image and the idea come mainly from her earlier book, *Thrive*. In *The Sleep Revolution* she carries the argument forward and puts sleep near the bottom of it.

Sleep, in her account, is a foundation of well-being. That gives it a different status from a lifestyle preference. When people sacrifice sleep to push the first two legs higher, she argues, they undermine the judgment and health that success depends on in the first place. The trade looks like investment. It works more like sawing at the thing you're sitting on.

##Rest, in this view, isn't the reward you collect once you've succeeded. It's part of what keeps success standing.##

She takes the argument beyond private life. In *The Sleep Revolution* she describes a shift in which some companies began to treat their employees' sleep as a business concern rather than a purely personal matter. That's a real change of category. Sleep stops being something you sort out on your own time and becomes something that affects how well a whole organization thinks and decides.

What makes the stool stick in the mind is how easy it is to test on yourself. Most people could name their two legs without hesitating. They know roughly what they earn and where they stand. Far fewer could say what their third leg is, how much time it actually gets, or whether they've been quietly cutting into it to prop up the other two.

The four elements also widen what counts as a good year. Well-being, wisdom, wonder and giving don't show up on a payslip or an org chart. In Huffington's framing, that's exactly why they're easy to neglect, and exactly why a life built without them tips.

[[lessons]]
$$**Two legs will tip.** A definition of success that rests only on money and power is structurally unstable. The stool image makes this concrete: it isn't that two legs are weak, it's that they can't balance. Adding more height to the same two legs doesn't fix the design.$$

$$**Success has more than one scale.** Huffington's third metric adds well-being, wisdom, wonder and giving. None of them appears in a salary figure or a job title, which is why they're so easy to overlook when you're keeping score. Naming them gives you something else to measure.$$

$$**Rest holds the rest up.** In *The Sleep Revolution*, sleep sits at the foundation of well-being. Cutting it to push harder on money or power erodes the judgment and health those goals depend on. Treating sleep as a reward for later gets the order backwards.$$

[[reflect]]
- If you judged last month only by money and status, how would it look? And with well-being, wisdom, wonder and giving added?
- Which of the four elements gets the least of your time right now?
- Where have you traded sleep to push one of the first two legs higher?

&&Which one of the four, well-being, wisdom, wonder or giving, could get a real slot in your calendar this week, and would it change how you judge the week?&&

On a Saturday afternoon in a San Francisco park, a woman looks sideways at her husband's phone. On the screen is a search result about how common cancer is in people in their thirties and forties. He is lying a few inches away in the sun, and he has not said a word about it.

[[story]]
In the prologue of *When Breath Becomes Air*, Paul Kalanithi describes the months before his diagnosis, and the park is where they come to a head. He and his wife, Lucy, were lying in the sun in Dolores Park, waiting to meet her sister, when Lucy glimpsed his phone. The medical database results on the screen were for the frequency of cancers in thirty- to forty-year-olds.

He had been losing weight and had severe back pain, and by then it had been going on for months. Early on he had seen a doctor. He asked for an MRI to be sure, and she answered that they should get X-rays first. X-rays were not perfectly sensitive, she said, but it made sense to start there. Kalanithi was a neurosurgery resident, and half of his training had been about disorders of the spine, so he knew the back better than most patients ever will.

As he tells it, the X-rays showed nothing alarming, and the pain was put down to overwork and ordinary aging. A few weeks later it was worse. He developed chest pain, night sweats and a cough that would not go away, and his weight kept dropping, from 175 pounds to 145.

He describes how he caught himself running the case like an exam. A thirty-five-year-old with unexplained weight loss and new back pain: on a medical boards question, the obvious answer would be cancer. Yet when he sat in the doctor's office he was, in his words, so authoritative in a surgeon's coat and so meek in a patient's gown. He deferred to the doctor on a subject he knew well, and he wondered whether he was only frightening himself.

So he kept the thought to himself, until a phone screen in a park gave it away. Lucy saw the search. He turned the phone off and said, "Let's get some ice cream."

The suggestion is almost funny, and it is a familiar picture of how people behave around a fear they have not yet said aloud. A man had a suspicion, his wife had seen the screen, and the next thing on the agenda was dessert.

At that point he was 36, about fifteen months from finishing a decade of training, and nobody had yet said the word out loud.

[[lessons]]
$$**Tell the person closest to you what you suspect before they find it on your screen.** A private worry grows quietly when you check it alone, whether it is about a symptom, a debt or a job that is going badly. Saying it in one plain sentence to someone who cares about you takes less strength than carrying it all by yourself, and it lets them help you decide what to do next, instead of guessing. It also keeps the worry from becoming a topic that both of you quietly steer around, the way a dessert can be suggested in its place.$$

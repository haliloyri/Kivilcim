For the first month in Seoul, two friends mostly stayed quiet. They had promised each other not to speak English, and they did not yet know enough Korean to say anything else. Ordering food and chatting with strangers had turned into things they simply avoided.

[[story]]
Scott Young, the author of Ultralearning, announced the project in August 2013. He and his friend Vat Jaiswal would spend a year without speaking English while trying to learn four languages: Spanish, Portuguese, Mandarin Chinese and Korean. They would live in Spain, Brazil, China and Korea, and they would start on 5 September in Valencia. Vat had never learned a language as an adult before.

The rule only covered speaking. Young's work is in English, so reading and writing in English stayed allowed, along with calls to his parents and business calls that could not be done any other way. Vat's parents speak Hindi, so he needed no exception for them.

How well the rule held depended on the country, and Young's own review a year later is frank about it. In Spain it came close to the ideal. The two spoke no English to each other from the day they arrived, their friends spoke Spanish, and Young switched most of his media to Spanish too. Brazil began more roughly, because they had done no Portuguese preparation, so they used English for the first couple of days and then stayed close to the goal.

China was mixed. Young had prepared more and was speaking only Chinese within a few days, while Vat alternated between the two languages. Korea was the hardest. Without enough preparation, they avoided speaking at all for the first month, and when they did start, they often slipped back into English. They made local friends through language exchanges, which Young admits bent the rules.

By his own assessment, Spanish came out best: he can talk about any topic, read books and follow films and television with attention. Portuguese and Chinese landed in the middle, and Korean was the weakest, since he can hold a conversation but over a narrower range and with more requests for clarification. Chinese was the only language he took a formal exam in, and he passed HSK 4. He adds that the exam body recommends two years of study before it.

His verdict was practical. Asian languages are hard, and full immersion without enough preparation usually does not work. He would not recommend the approach for anything shorter than three months, unless you will return to the country or have already studied a lot. He also describes his year as one story among many.

[[lessons]]
$$**Match the depth of the plunge to the preparation you bring to it.** Full immersion pays off when you can already say enough to take part, and it turns into silence when you cannot. The same project gave Young his best result in Spain and a month of avoided conversations in Korea, which shows how much the starting point matters. Before you commit to a hard rule, whether it is a new language, a new tool at work or a new role, check how much you can already do inside it, and add preparation where the gap is wide, so the first month goes to learning.$$

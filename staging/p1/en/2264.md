[[open]]
A forecasting question asks whether a laboratory will find polonium in a dead man's remains. Plenty of people who read it answer a different question, the one they already hold an opinion about. The score goes to the answer to the question as written, whatever the forecaster thinks about the story behind it.

[[story]]
Yasser Arafat died in 2004, and years later French and Swiss teams examined his remains. One of the questions in the IARPA tournament, as Philip Tetlock has described it, asked forecasters whether the French or Swiss investigators would find evidence of polonium. Tetlock and Dan Gardner's *Superforecasting* is built on questions like this one, with real answers to check against.

According to Tetlock, many forecasters read the question as "Did Israel kill Arafat?" and, deciding it had not, gave a low probability. The best superforecasters took it apart. They listed the ways polonium could turn up in a body, and an Israeli assassination was only one of them. That put them, in Tetlock's words, on the higher side of "maybe."

The tournament asked for a probability rather than a verdict, so even a "maybe" had to become a number that could be scored later. His takeaway was that a strong emotional or ideological reaction to the question hurt. The sentence on the page asked about a laboratory result. Many people answered a verdict about who was to blame.

The facts that followed fit the careful reading. In November 2013 the Swiss laboratory in Lausanne reported that its results "moderately support" the proposition that polonium-210 killed Arafat, while saying the evidence was not definitive. Press reports said the French team found similar levels of polonium but attributed them to radon in the tomb. A question about whether investigators would find polonium needed neither view to be true.

Look at what the wording did, because it set the terms of the bet. It said "French or Swiss," so a positive result from either team was enough. And polonium-210 loses half its radioactivity roughly every 138 days, so an honest forecaster had to ask whether anything measurable could survive years in a grave. Neither question needs a view on who was guilty.

Taking the question apart is the whole technique. One big question about guilt or politics turns into several smaller ones that each have an answer. The forecaster does not have to settle the argument first. A short list of checkable pieces can move the number a long way before any opinion gets involved.

[[lessons]]
$$**Rewrite a charged question into the smaller, checkable pieces underneath it.** Someone who asks "Will the new manager ruin our department?" has packed fear, history and loyalty into one sentence that cannot be answered. Split it into what you can look at: who might leave, what the budget does, how the manager handled the previous team. Each piece can be examined calmly, one at a time, with a source you can name for each. You may also notice that your gut answer was really written by the one piece you disliked from the start, which is a useful thing to catch before you say it out loud in a meeting.$$

You go through the list again. And again. By the tenth round the words come out without a pause, smooth and effortless, and you feel ready. That ease is the signal most of us trust. According to *Make It Stick*, it is also one of the most misleading signals in learning, and the effortful, stumbling version you would rather avoid is the one worth having.

[[story]]
Peter C. Brown, Henry L. Roediger III and Mark A. McDaniel built *Make It Stick*, published in 2014, around the gap between how learning feels and how well it lasts. One of their clearest examples is the difference between two ways of practising.

The first is massed practice. You repeat the same thing again and again in a single session. It is what cramming looks like: one long sitting, the same material over and over, until it flows.

The second is spaced practice. You work on the material, then leave it. When you come back after some time has passed, you practise it again.

Massed practice has an obvious appeal. It produces quick gains. Within one sitting you can feel yourself improving, and by the end the material seems to be firmly in place. It feels productive, and in the moment it looks productive. But the authors say those gains fade quickly.

Spaced practice feels worse. When you return after a gap, some of what you learned has slipped. You hesitate. You get things wrong. Pulling the answer up takes real effort, and it can feel like proof that the first session was wasted.

##The authors argue that this effort is exactly what strengthens the memory and makes it more durable.##

Letting a little forgetting set in is not the price of spacing; it is how spacing works. Because retrieval now takes more effort, the act of recalling does more to fix the memory in place than an easy repetition ever could.

The book borrows a term for obstacles like this: **desirable difficulties**. Not every difficulty helps, but some, like having to work to remember after a gap, make learning stick. The catch is that they do not feel desirable at all. They feel like struggling.

That leads to the book's warning. Learners tend to misjudge which methods actually work, because the easier method feels more successful. Smooth repetition gives a strong sense of progress. Effortful recall gives a sense of falling behind. If you go by feel, you will keep choosing the method that fades.

So the lesson is not simply "practise more." It is to stop using ease as your measure. The session that flows might be building very little that lasts, and the clumsy one after a few days away might be doing the real work.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ease is a poor guide.** Massed practice gives quick gains and a satisfying sense of progress, and the authors say those gains fade quickly. How smooth a session feels tells you little about what will still be there next month. If you judge your study by how fluent it feels, you will tend to pick the method that lasts least. A better check is whether you can still bring it up after a few days away.$$

$$**Let some forgetting happen first.** Spacing works because a gap makes you forget a little, so recalling takes effort. That effort is what the book calls a desirable difficulty, and it makes the memory more durable. The practical move is to resist reviewing something again while it is still fresh, and come back when bringing it to mind is a bit of a struggle.$$

In most companies, the quickest way to a bigger salary is to collect people who report to you. The more heads you manage, the more you are paid. One engineering leader wrote a rule that cuts that link on purpose, and it sits among thirteen others he wanted obeyed to the letter.

[[story]]
Kelly Johnson, the founder of Lockheed's Skunk Works, put his way of running a secret aircraft program into fourteen rules of operation. Ben Rich, who took over the group after Johnson, wrote about the place with Leo Janos in *Skunk Works*. The wording varies a little between published versions, but the content is stable.

The first rule gives the Skunk Works manager near-total control of his program, and the manager reports to a division president or higher. Nobody stands between the person running the project and the person who can say yes. Another rule holds the team small and made of top people, with only a fraction of the staff a conventional program would use.

Paperwork gets the same treatment. Johnson asked for few reports, but he wanted the important work documented thoroughly. Money gets a monthly review of what has been spent, what is committed and what the program will cost by the end. A further rule restricts outside access to the project and its people with strict security, because the programs were secret.

Then comes the fourteenth. Because only a few people are used in engineering and in most other areas, the rule says, a way must be found to reward good performance with pay, and that pay should not depend on the number of people a person supervises. In most organizations a salary grows by adding subordinates, and that gives the best engineer a reason to stop engineering and start managing. Johnson closed that exit.

Lockheed's own profile of Johnson records his motto: be quick, be quiet, be on time. It also says he tolerated errors made in pursuit of a breakthrough, but only if each error was made once.

Read together, the rules form one design. A small team, a short chain to the decision maker, light reporting and pay that follows the work all push in the same direction. They remove the reasons for a group to grow, and a group that does not grow keeps the speed it started with.

The rules do not promise anything about the people. They describe conditions in which a small number of capable people can finish hard things without waiting, and each of them aims at the same target, which is less waiting.

[[lessons]]
$$**Pay and promote people for the quality of their work, not for how many people report to them.** In a small bakery or a software team, the best craftsperson is often offered a manager's title as the only way to a raise, and the shop loses its best baker to a spreadsheet. A path of growth that stays at the bench keeps the skill where the work is done, and it tells the next person that craft is a real way to rise. When you design a role or a raise, ask what behavior it quietly rewards, and move the reward toward the output you actually want.$$

Plastic eggs sit on a supermarket shelf, a pair of pantyhose folded inside each one. Carolyn Lynch takes a pair home and tells her husband they are good. He picks stocks for a living, and he spends the following weeks checking whether she is right.

[[story]]
Peter Lynch, whose *One Up on Wall Street* tells ordinary investors to trust what they see around them, described this episode himself in an interview with PBS's *Frontline*. It happened in the 1970s. His wife Carolyn had brought home L'eggs, a brand of pantyhose from Hanes sold in egg-shaped plastic packages at the supermarket, and she told him they were great.

Lynch checked before he bought. He looked at how women shopped. By his account, the average woman went to a supermarket or a drugstore every week, but to a department store or a specialty shop only about once every six weeks. Good pantyhose had been sold in department stores until then. L'eggs offered the same quality in the place women already walked through all the time.

The product itself had appeared around 1970, and according to one marketing history it held 27 percent of the pantyhose market in grocery stores by 1976, nearly double its closest competitor. Lynch went further than the sales figures, though. When Kaiser-Roth brought out its own supermarket line called No Nonsense, he bought 48 pairs in different sizes and colors and had colleagues try them.

They reported back that No Nonsense was not as good. He kept his Hanes. "That's what fundamental research is," he said.

Hanes grew into the largest position in his Magellan fund, according to a profile in Wharton Magazine. Lynch's word for a stock that rises tenfold is a tenbagger, and he says Hanes was one. He adds that it might have been a thirty-bagger if Consolidated Foods had not bought the company out.

Look at the order of events. Carolyn's remark was a lead that anyone married to her could have heard. The weekly and six-weekly shopping habits turned it into a reason, and the 48 pairs checked the one weakness a rival could have exposed. Buying across sizes and colors meant his colleagues' verdict covered the range a customer would meet on the shelf, and no single pair could carry it. None of that needed a visit to company headquarters or a friend on the inside. A shopper with a free afternoon and some curiosity could have taken every one of those steps.

[[lessons]]
$$**Turn a hunch from daily life into a small test before you act on it.** A baker who hears her customers rave about a rival's sourdough can buy a loaf, taste it next to her own and ask a few regulars which one they would pick. The hunch tells you where to look, and the test tells you whether it is worth your money, your time or a change of plan. Once you have checked one thing a stranger could not know from the outside, you can act with more than a feeling behind you, and you will find out quickly when the hunch was wrong.$$

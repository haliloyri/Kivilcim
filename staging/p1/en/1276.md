[[open]]
Tesla. LinkedIn. YouTube. Yelp. Yammer. Palantir. Six companies that have little to do with one another. What they share is a line on some of their founders' résumés: the same payments company, a few years earlier. How does one young company send out that many founders?

[[story]]
Peter Thiel tells this story in *Zero to One*. The payments company was PayPal. In 2002 it was sold to eBay for $1.5 billion.

For most companies, a sale like that would be the end of the story. For PayPal, it was more like a starting point. Many of its early employees and founders went on to start other major companies, including Tesla, LinkedIn, YouTube, Yelp, Yammer and Palantir. The press gave the group a name: the "PayPal Mafia."

It's tempting to explain that with luck, or timing, or the product. Thiel points somewhere else. In his account, the reason lies in how the original team was put together.

PayPal recruited people who genuinely liked working together and who shared a similar outlook. Thiel describes the early team as all the same kind of nerd, bound by shared interests. Because of that, they didn't need long stretches of time to build trust with one another. The trust was there from early on, and it didn't depend on the company existing. When PayPal was sold, the bonds lasted beyond it.

The second half of his explanation is about how the work was organized. Thiel describes a simple management rule. Each person was made responsible for one clearly defined thing, and each person was evaluated only on that one thing.

##Giving everyone one clear thing to own cut down the internal fights over who was responsible for what.##

In many young companies, roles overlap, and people end up competing over the same territory. At PayPal, the lines were drawn so that there was less to fight about.

Thiel's conclusion is not that PayPal had a clever slogan about culture. It is almost the opposite. A startup's early culture, in his view, is a deliberate choice about which people you bring in and what each of them is responsible for.

That choice produced a company worth $1.5 billion to eBay. But in Thiel's telling, the more lasting result was the group of people who came out of it. The product was sold. The team, scattered across new companies, kept building.

[[lessons]]
$$**The people can outlast the product.** PayPal was sold in 2002, but its early team went on to start Tesla, LinkedIn, YouTube and more. The bonds formed on that team carried on long after the company itself changed hands.$$

$$**A shared outlook makes trust faster.** Thiel says the early team were all the same kind of nerd, so they didn't need long periods to build trust. When you're hiring or forming a team, whether people actually want to work together is a practical question, not a soft one.$$

$$**One clear owner means less friction.** Each PayPal employee was responsible for one defined thing and judged only on it. That cut down the fights that come from overlapping roles. Friction between capable people is often a sign that their responsibilities overlap, not that the people are the problem. Before blaming a clash on personalities, check who owns what.$$

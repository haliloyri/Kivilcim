Your manager doesn't look at you once during a meeting. Within seconds, you know exactly why: they're unhappy with your work, maybe even planning to replace you. By the time the meeting ends, you're anxious and a little angry. But notice what just happened. You never actually found out anything.

[[story]]
The authors of *Crucial Conversations* noticed that many difficult conversations go wrong before anyone says a word. They go wrong inside our heads, in a few seconds most of us never examine.

They describe it as a **Path to Action**. First, we **see and hear** something: a manager who doesn't make eye contact, a short email, a partner who's late again. Second, we **tell a story** about it. We add meaning, motives, intentions. Third, that story creates a **feeling**: hurt, anger, fear. And fourth, the feeling drives what we **do**.

The step that causes the trouble is the second one. We tell the story so quickly that we don't experience it as a story at all. It feels like part of what we saw.

##Two people can sit through the same meeting and leave with completely different stories.##

Imagine two colleagues both notice the manager didn't address them. The first thinks: *she's shutting me out; she's probably planning to let me go.* The second thinks: *she looked stressed; something must be going on.* Same facts. Different stories. One walks out afraid and resentful. The other barely thinks about it again.

The authors make a striking claim: it isn't mainly what other people do that creates our emotions. It's the stories we tell about what they do. That's actually good news. If the story is ours, we can change it.

Their advice is to **retrace your path**. When you notice a strong feeling, go back step by step. What story am I telling that's creating this feeling? And what exactly did I see or hear? Separate the observable facts, things a camera could record, from the meaning you've added. Then ask whether a reasonable, decent person could have done the same thing for a different reason.

When you do need to speak up, they offer a sequence they call **STATE**. **Share your facts**. **Tell your story**, but as your interpretation, not the truth. **Ask for others' paths**, their facts and their story. **Talk tentatively**, so the other person can disagree. And **encourage testing**, making it genuinely safe for them to say you've got it wrong.

Opening with facts matters. "In the meeting, you didn't ask for my update" is hard to argue with. "You're trying to push me out" starts a fight.

[[lessons]]
$$**Feelings follow stories.** In the Path to Action, emotion comes from the story, not directly from the event. The anxious colleague and the relaxed one saw the same meeting. Recognizing that your feelings are built on an interpretation gives you somewhere to intervene, because interpretations can be checked and changed.$$

$$**Pull the fact apart from the story.** Asking "what did I actually see or hear?" is a simple discipline with a big effect. A camera would record that the manager didn't ask for your update. It wouldn't record "she's shutting me out." Keeping those two apart stops a guess from hardening into a grievance.$$

$$**Lead with facts, hold your story loosely.** STATE starts with facts because they're the least controversial part of any conversation. Then you offer your story tentatively and invite the other person's view. You still say what you think, but you leave room to discover that you were wrong.$$

[[reflect]]
- What story have you told yourself this week that you've been treating as a fact?
- What did you actually see or hear, stripped of any interpretation?
- What other story, a kinder or more ordinary one, could explain exactly the same facts?

&&If you only told the facts, what would you actually be able to say?&&

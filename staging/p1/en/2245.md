At a conference, the talks are spoken to whoever sits in the hall that day, and most of them stay there. Even the best presentation soon shrinks to a memory held by the people who were there. The owner of one such conference decided to put its most highly rated talks where anyone could watch them.

[[story]]
According to published accounts, TED began in 1984 as a conference conceived by Richard Saul Wurman, and became an annual event from 1990. A company called Future bought it in 2000 for $14 million. In November 2001 Chris Anderson's non-profit Sapling Foundation took it over from Future for £6 million.

What Anderson held was a conference that filled a hall each year. In June 2006 the team posted a selection of its highest-rated talks online. Only a handful went up at first. By January 2007 there were 44 talks, and together they had been viewed more than three million times.

In April 2007 a new TED.com, designed by the firm Method, went live. The talks were now within reach of anyone who landed on the site, not just those in the hall. The view counts grew fast: 50 million by January 2009 and 500 million by June 2011. That is roughly a tenfold rise in 29 months.

These figures need careful reading. A view is a play, not a person, and one viewer can watch several talks or the same one more than once. Even so, the gap between the two points is plain: three million at the start of 2007, five hundred million by mid-2011. The audience in the hall is nowhere near that scale.

What was done is plain. An organisation took its most valuable product, its best talks, released a small curated dose, and watched what happened. The first data point was more than three million views in about seven months. That number was not a plan, but it was a signal that justified the next step, rebuilding the site.

The sequence matters more than the size of the numbers. Nothing in these figures says the online release left the conference untouched or hurt it, since they report only plays and say nothing about the conference's own attendance.

A small selection came first, the response was measured, and only then was the site enlarged. The conference did not disappear; the meeting in the hall carried on, and the online release added a second audience around it. TED's present shape came less from one large decision than from a chain of small steps, each taken after looking at the result of the one before.

[[lessons]]
$$**Release your best work in a small, controlled dose and measure what the demand says.** A product, a course or a report that reaches only a few people in one room never shows how far it could travel. A small selection carries little risk and gives you a real signal. If the signal is strong it decides the next step, and if it is weak it decides the next question. So you test how far your work might spread, instead of waiting for a perfect launch or betting everything on a big claim, with a loss that stays small if the idea fails.$$

Picture a man climbing down from a freight car in New Jersey. He has no ticket because he couldn't afford one. He has no letter of introduction because he doesn't know anyone who could write one. He's walking toward Thomas Edison's laboratory, and when he gets there he isn't going to ask for a job. He's going to tell Edison he has come to be his partner.

[[story]]
Napoleon Hill tells this one early in *Think and Grow Rich*, in the chapter he called "Thoughts Are Things." The man is Edwin C. Barnes, and what he wanted was oddly specific. He didn't want to work for Thomas A. Edison. Plenty of people wanted that. Barnes wanted to go into business *with* him, as an associate and a partner.

The trouble was everything else. Barnes had never met Edison. He had no connection who could get him through the door. He didn't even have enough money for the railroad fare to get there. So he went anyway, riding a freight train to Edison's laboratory in West Orange, New Jersey.

He presented himself to the most famous inventor alive and said it plainly:

> He had come to go into business with Edison.

Years later Edison remembered that first meeting. Barnes, he said, looked like an ordinary tramp. But there was something in his expression that Edison couldn't ignore. It was the look of a man who had made up his mind and meant to get what he'd come for.

##Edison read the decision in Barnes's face before Barnes had proven a single thing.##

He didn't get his partnership that day. What he got was a job in Edison's offices, doing unimportant work at a very small wage. It was nowhere near what he had announced. A lot of people would have felt humiliated by that. Some would have taken the job and quietly let the big talk fade, telling themselves it had been a young man's fantasy.

Barnes took the job and kept the goal.

Then came the long part of the story, the part that usually gets skipped in the retelling. For about five years he did work that had nothing to do with becoming Edison's associate. From the outside, nothing changed. No promotion toward partnership, no sign that the plan was working. He was simply a man in a minor role in someone else's company. But he held on to the desire as sharply as the day he climbed off that freight car.

The opening came in a form nobody was fighting over. Edison had invented a dictating machine, the device later sold as the Ediphone. His salesmen weren't excited about it. They didn't think it would sell.

Barnes did. He said he could sell it.

He got the chance, and he sold it so well that Edison gave him a contract to distribute and market the machine across the whole country. That contract was the business association Barnes had walked in asking for five years earlier. The tramp from the freight train had become Edison's partner.

For Hill, the point was what came first. Barnes had a definite purpose and he'd made a decision with no way back. Hill called it burning your bridges. In Hill's telling, a thought held like that becomes a reality.

[[lessons]]
$$**The decision was the capital.** Barnes arrived with nothing you could put on a balance sheet: no fare, no introduction, no credentials. What he had was an exact target. He wanted to be Edison's associate, not just his employee. That precision gave him a reason to board the freight train at all. The money and the contract came later, after he'd already committed.$$

$$**Take the door that's open, and keep your eyes on the one you want.** The job Barnes was offered was small, low-paid and unrelated to partnership. He accepted it without shrinking his ambition to fit. The minor role put him in the building, near Edison and near the products, which is where the real opening would eventually appear.$$

$$**Invisible years are what let you spot the opening.** For about five years nothing seemed to happen. But because Barnes never stopped looking for his chance, he recognized it when it showed up disguised as a dictating machine Edison's own salesmen didn't believe in. Other people saw a product nobody wanted. He saw the partnership he'd been waiting for.$$

[[reflect]]
- Which goal have you put on a shelf because you're waiting to have "enough" first, whether that's money, contacts or qualifications?
- Barnes's first step was a freight car, not a first-class ticket. What's the rough, unglamorous version of your first step?
- Is there a modest position close to your goal that you've been too proud to take?
- Look around your work right now. What project are others shrugging at that you could say "I can do that" about?

&&If you said your goal out loud as plainly as Barnes did, what would your freight-train step be this week?&&

Patients who have just had a hip replaced are asked how they picture their recovery. The researchers write down how positive each picture is. Then comes the waiting: a couple of weeks later, therapists will judge how well each person is actually recovering, and the two sets of notes will be laid side by side.

[[story]]
Two weeks later, physical therapists rated how well each patient was recovering, and the pattern ran against common sense. In the study by psychologist Gabriele Oettingen and her co-author Mayer, published in 2002, those whose fantasies of recovery had been more positive were judged to be recovering less well. The same study found that positive fantasies went with lower grades in an upcoming exam and with less success in starting a romantic relationship.

An earlier piece of Oettingen's work, with Thomas Wadden in 1991, had pointed the same way. It followed overweight women in a behavioral weight-loss program. According to later reviews, the more positively they fantasized about losing weight, the less weight they lost, three months and a year on. The sample was small, so this was a hint, not a proof.

Oettingen stresses a distinction that is easy to miss. A fantasy is a vivid picture you build in your mind. An expectation is your estimate of how likely something is. In these studies optimistic expectations predicted success, while positive fantasies predicted the opposite. Believing a goal is within reach and savoring the finished scene are different mental acts. The people who did well were hopeful in their estimates, just not dreamy in their pictures.

Why would a pleasant picture do harm? Her account is that dwelling on a positive future lets you enjoy it now, in the present, which can reduce the push to make it real. Kappes and Oettingen tested this in four experiments published in 2011. Where people were led to indulge positive fantasies, they showed lower energization and put in less effort. Energization was measured by self-report and by objective signs, including systolic blood pressure.

She does not tell people to give up on dreams. Her remedy is called mental contrasting: first you picture the wished-for outcome vividly, then you picture, just as vividly, the obstacle that stands between you and it. The dream supplies the direction, and the obstacle supplies the reason to plan.

Oliver Burkeman's *The Antidote* is built on a similar suspicion of mandatory positivity. Some of these studies were observational, so they do not prove that fantasies cause poor results. Still, the direction repeats from study to study: seeing only the happy ending is no substitute for the work that leads to it.

[[lessons]]
$$**After you picture the goal, picture the most likely obstacle just as clearly, and write one "if this, then I will" plan for it.** Looking at the happy ending feels good, and that feeling can pass for part of the work already done, which softens your effort. Meeting the obstacle in your mind creates the need for a plan, something concrete like "if I'm tired tonight, I'll still walk for ten minutes before dinner". With that sentence ready, the picture of success pushes you toward action instead of paying you in advance, and the obstacle turns from a surprise into a scheduled event.$$

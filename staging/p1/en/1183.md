Imagine you could ask fifty of the world's most successful investors for one piece of advice for ordinary people. You might expect secret strategies or clever stock tips. Tony Robbins tried something like that. The first step he arrived at is almost disappointingly simple.

[[story]]
For *Money: Master the Game*, Tony Robbins spent years interviewing about fifty leading financial experts and investors, among them Ray Dalio, founder of the world's largest hedge fund, and John Bogle, founder of Vanguard and pioneer of low-cost index funds. He wanted to know how ordinary people, not just the rich, could build financial freedom.

From those conversations, he built a plan of seven simple steps. And the first step is the foundation for all the others. He calls it making the most important financial decision of your life.

##Decide to save a fixed part of every dollar you earn, and invest it, before you spend anything.##

Robbins points out that most people do the opposite. They get paid, pay the bills, spend on everything else, and then plan to save whatever is left at the end of the month. The trouble is that there's usually nothing left. Spending expands to fill the money available. Good intentions don't survive the month.

So step one flips the order. You pay yourself first. A set percentage comes out of your income before you have a chance to spend it, and goes into investments that can grow over time.

The next part is what makes it work. Robbins insists you **automate** it. Don't rely on remembering or on willpower. Set up a transfer so the money moves on payday, before you ever see it in your account. If you never see it, you're far less likely to miss it, and you never have to make the decision again.

He also addresses a common objection: *I can't afford to save right now.* Here he draws on the work of economist Shlomo Benartzi and a program called **Save More Tomorrow**. The idea is that people find it painful to cut today's spending, but much easier to commit to saving part of a *future* raise. You decide now that when your salary goes up, a portion of the increase goes straight to savings. You never feel the loss, because you never had that money to spend in the first place.

Robbins's argument is that without this step, nothing else in the book matters. Clever investment strategies can't grow money you never set aside. With it, even modest incomes can slowly turn into real security, because time and compounding do the rest.

[[lessons]]
$$**Save before you spend.** Most people save "whatever's left," and usually nothing is. Robbins's first step reverses the order: a fixed percentage comes out first. It's a small change in sequence that makes a huge difference, because it stops spending from quietly swallowing every good intention.$$

$$**Automation beats willpower.** A transfer that runs on payday doesn't depend on discipline, mood or memory. You make the decision once, and it keeps happening. Removing the monthly choice is often the single most effective thing you can do to make saving stick.$$

$$**Commit your future raises.** Save More Tomorrow works because it avoids the pain of cutting current spending. Pledging part of a future raise feels easy now, and the money never shows up in your lifestyle. It's a clever way to raise your savings rate without ever feeling poorer along the way.$$

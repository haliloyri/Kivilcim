On a January evening, engineers dial into a conference call to argue against a launch. They have a number: do not fly below about 53 degrees Fahrenheit. The forecast for the night is far colder. By the next morning, the spacecraft is still on the pad and the launch is going ahead.

[[story]]
On the evening of 27 January 1986, engineers from Morton Thiokol, the company that built the solid rocket boosters, joined a teleconference with NASA before the next morning's launch of the space shuttle Challenger. Two of them, Roger Boisjoly and Thompson, recommended not launching if the temperature fell below about 53 degrees Fahrenheit, which is 12 degrees Celsius.

Their concern was the O-rings, rubber seals in the joints of the boosters. Cold makes rubber stiff and brittle, and a stiff seal may not close a gap quickly enough. The forecast for the overnight hours was around 30 degrees Fahrenheit, far below the figure the engineers wanted to respect. The gap between the limit they proposed and the weather that was coming was not small or subtle; it could be read off a thermometer.

The Challenger launched the next day, 28 January, and broke apart in flight. Afterwards the Rogers Commission, the panel appointed to investigate the accident, found that the decision-making process had been flawed. Concerns raised by engineers had not been passed up to the people who needed to hear them.

The popular version of this story casts Boisjoly as a lone hero who saw the danger while everyone else looked away. That version is disputed, and the more accurate picture is less comfortable. The engineers did speak, and they gave a threshold. What failed was the way the organisation received that warning and carried it upward, from the engineers on the call to the people who had the authority to say stop.

Look at what the number did and did not do. It gave the engineers a specific, testable position, which is much stronger than a general worry. But a recommendation is only half of a decision. The other half is a person with authority who treats an objection as something to be answered with evidence, rather than as an obstacle to the schedule.

Two jobs follow from this, one for each side of the table. Whoever holds the data must say, in advance and in plain figures, where their line is. Whoever leads the meeting must ask for the objection and make sure it travels to the people who decide.

[[lessons]]
$$**State your concern as a clear threshold with data behind it, and if you lead, ask for the objection out loud.** A vague worry is easy to wave away, while a number such as "not below 12 degrees" forces an answer. If you chair the decision, ask directly who sees a reason not to go ahead and what they would need to be convinced. Check afterwards that the warning reached the person who decides, not just the room. A good process does not rely on one brave person; it makes the warning travel by default.$$

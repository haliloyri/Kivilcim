[[open]]
A telephone line, a telegraph wire, a radio beam and a television signal look like four unrelated problems. Each has its own engineers, its own equipment and its own folklore about what goes wrong. Someone had to ask whether a single yardstick could measure what all four carry.

[[story]]
Claude Shannon, a mathematician at Bell Labs, supplied that yardstick, and Jon Gertner's The Idea Factory gives him a prominent place among the Labs' stars. His paper "A Mathematical Theory of Communication" appeared in the Bell System Technical Journal in two parts, in July and October 1948. It is now treated as the founding text of information theory.

The central move was to measure information without asking what a message means. For the engineering problem, Shannon argued, the content of a message does not matter; what matters is how many possible messages the sender could have chosen from. A coin toss, a letter of the alphabet, a line of a poem all become quantities that can be counted. The unit he used was the binary digit, and the paper credits the shortened word "bit" to his Bell Labs colleague John Tukey.

Once information had a unit, a second question became precise: how much of it can a channel carry? Shannon showed that every channel, however noisy, has a limit, and that below that limit messages can be sent with as few errors as you like, if they are encoded cleverly enough. Before this, engineers fighting noise mostly made signals louder. Shannon's result said that the right code could do what extra power could not.

The paper did not build a gadget. It did something quieter and more durable: it gave a name and a number to the thing that every communication system was actually moving. A telephone call and a television picture could now be compared, compressed and protected on common terms, because both were streams of bits. The counting came first, and the engineering that followed in later decades stood on it.

Shannon worked inside a company that built the systems he was describing, so his questions were about real wires and real noise. His theory is abstract, but it grew out of a place that needed answers from it.

What lasts from this story is a method, and it is easy to miss because the equations are so famous. Before anyone could improve communication, someone had to decide what exactly was being communicated and how to count it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you try to improve something, decide what you are measuring and in which unit.** A vague goal such as "better communication" or "more engagement" invites endless argument, because everyone pictures something different, while a number you can count lets everyone test the same idea. Look at the project in front of you: if two people on your team would give different answers to "how will we know it got better?", the unit is still missing. Once you have it, unrelated problems become comparable, and you can see which fix really moves the number rather than which one merely sounds convincing.$$

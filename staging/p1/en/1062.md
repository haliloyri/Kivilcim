[[open]]
Picture a busy hospital cafeteria at lunchtime. Doctors, nurses and visitors grab a tray, move along the line, and reach for a drink on the way to pay. Most of them reach for soda. One physician looked at that scene and made an unusual choice. She didn't design a health campaign or plan a single lecture. She decided to move some bottles around.

[[story]]
The physician was Anne Thorndike, a primary care doctor at Massachusetts General Hospital in Boston. She wanted the hospital's staff and visitors to eat and drink better. She also knew how that usually goes. You tell people what's good for them, they nod, and then they do what they always did. So she set herself a harder test. Could she change their habits without relying on their willpower or motivation at all?

Her approach had a slightly dry name: changing the "choice architecture" of the cafeteria. In plain terms, that meant changing where the drinks sat and how they were displayed. People would still make their own choices. The room would just be arranged differently.

Look at what the cafeteria was like before. The refrigerators right next to the cash registers held only soda. Those fridges are the last thing you pass before you pay, at the exact moment you think, *oh, I should grab something to drink.* Whatever sat there was the easy choice.

So Thorndike and her colleagues stocked water in those fridges beside the registers. They didn't stop there. They also set baskets of bottled water at food stations throughout the cafeteria, on top of what was already in the main drink area. Wherever you stood in that room, water was now in view and within arm's reach.

The detail that makes this story so striking is that soda was still available. Nobody banned it or hid it, and the price didn't change. There were no posters about sugar and no one scolded anybody at the register. The only difference was that water had become more visible and easier to grab.

Then they watched the sales. Over the next three months, soda sales fell by about 11 percent. Bottled water sales rose by about 26 percent.

##No one had been persuaded of anything, yet the choices changed anyway.##

James Clear tells this story in *Atomic Habits* to make a point that sounds almost too simple. People often choose products because of *where* they are, not *what* they are. The obvious cues in our surroundings shape what we do more than our good intentions do. Clear treats environment as an invisible hand. We rarely notice it pushing us, but it pushes all day long.

He applies this in his own home. He leaves a guitar stand in the middle of the living room so that he practices more. He fills water bottles and places them around the house. He keeps fruit in a bowl where he can see it rather than tucked away in a drawer. None of it takes discipline. It's the same move Thorndike made in the cafeteria, carried into an ordinary home.

[[lessons]]
$$**You pick what you see.** Before the change, the fridges by the registers offered one option, soda, right where people made their last-second decision. Once water appeared in those same fridges, people started reaching for it. The drink itself hadn't changed. Its position had. The cue you see most often, at the moment you decide, tends to become the thing you choose.$$

$$**Moving things can beat arguing.** Soda fell about 11 percent and water rose about 26 percent in three months, and nobody was convinced of anything along the way. There were no lectures, price changes or campaigns. If you've spent months trying to talk someone, or yourself, into a better habit, the cafeteria suggests a different question. Instead of asking what to say, ask what to move.$$

$$**Make the good cue obvious and the bad one quiet.** Clear's guitar sits in the middle of the living room, where he can't miss it. That is the same idea as the water baskets at every food station. To build a habit, put its cue where you'll bump into it. To weaken one, take its cue out of your line of sight. Out of sight tends to mean out of habit.$$

[[reflect]]
- Look around the room you're in. Which objects are quietly voting for a habit you'd like to quit?
- Think of the "register moment" in your day, the point where you decide on autopilot. What's sitting there right now?
- Pick one habit you want. Where could its cue go so it's the first thing you see in the morning or when you walk in the door?
- Notice where you've been blaming willpower for a problem that might really be about placement.

&&If your surroundings made every choice for you today, what would they choose, and which single object would you move to change that?&&

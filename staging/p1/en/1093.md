Imagine there's one thing on your list today that you really don't want to do. You already know it has to happen. You get to pick exactly one detail: does it happen at 9 in the morning, or does it sit on your shoulder until 6 in the evening? Most of us think we're choosing *when* to do it. We're actually choosing how long we'll carry it.

[[story]]
This one is simple, and I want to take it slowly. In *The Power of Focus*, Jack Canfield, writing with Mark Victor Hansen and Les Hewitt, keeps returning to one theme. Your results come from where your attention goes, and your best attention belongs to your top priorities. It doesn't belong to whatever happens to be convenient or urgent.

People often use a vivid image for one way of living that out. They call it "eating the frog." You'll hear it credited to Mark Twain, but that attribution is doubtful, so let's not lean on it. The picture carries the idea on its own. If you have to eat a frog today, don't sit there looking at it all afternoon. Eat it first.

The frog is the one task you most need to do and most want to avoid. The rule is to do it before the easier work.

So let's run the same day twice.

In the first version, you sit down at 9 and open your email. It feels productive. You reply, you sort, you knock out a few quick jobs that are easy to finish and satisfying to check off. Somewhere behind all that activity, a quiet voice keeps running:

> *I still have to do that.*

You don't say it out loud. You barely notice it. But it's there during lunch, and it's there in the middle of a meeting. It's there when you're halfway through something else and your focus slips for a second. The hard task isn't getting done, but it isn't leaving you alone either. By 6 your energy is low. That is exactly when the hardest thing on your list is finally waiting for you, and it gets your worst hour. Or it doesn't get done at all and moves to tomorrow, still running in the background.

##The task you avoid doesn't wait quietly in a drawer. It follows you around all day.##

In the second version, you sit down at 9 and don't open email. You pick up the thing you've been dreading and do it while your energy is at its highest. It's uncomfortable. It might take an hour, and it might be a bad hour. Then it's done.

Now look at the rest of that day. The emails are still there, and so are the small tasks. They move faster, because nothing is pulling at the back of your mind anymore. The loop has stopped. Each easy thing is simply easy, with no weight sitting underneath it.

That's the claim at the heart of this idea. An avoided task keeps running in the back of your mind. It drains energy and scatters focus until you finish it. So the real comparison isn't "hard task now" against "hard task later." It's one tough hour set against a whole day of low-grade drag, plus the tough hour anyway, done when you have the least to give it.

The common habit is to start with email and easy wins and leave the hard thing for the evening. It feels reasonable. But it quietly puts your best hours into your least important work, and it makes the task that matters most the one you carry longest.

[[lessons]]
$$**Avoiding a task still costs you.** In the first version of the day, the hard task never got touched, yet it was present in every hour. That silent *I still have to do that* loop showed up at lunch and in meetings and kept breaking your focus. Putting a task off doesn't pause its cost. You pay a little all day in scattered attention, and then you still have to do the work.$$

$$**Order decides which energy each task gets.** Morning email felt productive, and that's the trap. Your energy and willpower are usually highest early. Spending them on replies and quick checkmarks means the hardest item gets the 6 p.m. version of you. That version is tired and short on patience, and the most likely to push it to tomorrow. Choosing what comes first is really choosing which task gets your best hours.$$

$$**One hard hour can make the whole day lighter.** In the second version, the uncomfortable hour came first, and after that the small tasks flowed. They hadn't changed. The weight underneath them was gone. Clearing the biggest thing early doesn't just finish one job. It changes how every job after it feels.$$

[[reflect]]
- Look at your list right now. Which task has been sitting there longest, and what exactly makes you step around it?
- Think about the first thing you did this morning. Was it your most important work, or just the most convenient?
- Notice when the *I still have to do that* voice turns up during your day. What is it pulling you away from?
- Picture tomorrow at 6 p.m. Do you want to be starting the hard thing then, or already done with it?

&&If you did your most-avoided task first thing tomorrow, how would the rest of your day feel?&&

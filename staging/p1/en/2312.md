[[open]]
A new computer is unveiled at a ceremony in a big city, and the people who built it are already being sent in different directions. The man who led the work is about to be sent abroad, and not one machine has been sold yet, not a single one.

[[story]]
Data General announced the Eclipse MV/8000 in April 1980, at a ceremony in New York City. It was the machine that Tracy Kidder's *The Soul of a New Machine* had followed from a basement in Westborough, where it was code-named Eagle. According to Wired's twenty-year retrospective, the Eagle group was broken up and Tom West was sent to the company's office in Japan, and all of it happened before a single MV/8000 had been sold.

The machine went on to sell briskly for several years. By the time West came back from Japan, after about a year, it was a success by then, one the whole company could point to. The man who led the project had missed the stretch when it began to pay off.

The engineers scattered too. Steve Wallach left Data General in 1980 and co-founded Convex in Dallas, which Hewlett-Packard bought in 1995. When Wallach invited West to join Convex, he found West "just burnt", in Wallach's word. Ken Holberger and Chuck Holland left in the early 1980s and founded Epoch Systems, which EMC bought in 1992. Carl Alsing joined Tandem in late 1980.

Jonathan Blau left high technology after burning out at a later company, Alliant, and became a junk bond analyst at Donaldson, Lufkin & Jenrette in New York. Wired found him at forty-four in that new job. Tom West, for his part, stayed at Data General until 1998, when he retired as chief technologist. He lived in Westport, Massachusetts, sailing and fishing, and died in 2011.

Put these pieces side by side and a pattern shows. The sprint that produced the machine was followed by dispersal, not by a pause: the group was broken up, the leader was sent abroad, and several engineers left within a few years to start something new. Wallach's word for West is the plainest sign of what the sprint had cost, and the company's move for the leader was a transfer rather than a rest.

None of this takes away from what the engineers built. It simply shows that a launch date is the end of one kind of work and the start of another, and that the second kind is easy to leave unplanned.

[[lessons]]
$$**Plan the weeks after a big push as carefully as the push itself.** A restaurant team that has just survived a holiday rush may be exhausted precisely when the owner starts talking about the next big thing, and the owner who sees that can give them a quiet week first. Rest, recognition and a clear next assignment are all cheaper to arrange before the crunch ends than to repair after people have left. Put the date of the first day off, or the first small thank-you, on the calendar at the moment you set the deadline, so that the rest is not left to chance.$$

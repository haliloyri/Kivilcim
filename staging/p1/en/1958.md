A man steps into a boat on a lazy, glassy sea, under clouds that look ready to break. It is only a few miles. Before the crossing is over he will be asking the pilot to put him ashore, and then he will lower himself into the water in his cloak without waiting for the anchor.

[[story]]
Seneca's fifty-third letter to Lucilius opens with a rueful admission: he was talked into travelling by water, and it went badly. He had wanted to get from Puteoli to Naples, a few miles across the bay. The sea was lazily smooth that day, though the sky hung heavy with the kind of clouds that break into rain or squalls. Seneca decided the distance could be run off quickly anyway, and to be quicker still he steered straight out toward the islet of Nesis, cutting across every inlet.

By the time he was far enough out that going back or going on made little difference, the calm that had lured him was gone. The storm was yet to start, but the swell had, and the waves were coming faster and faster. He asked the pilot to land him anywhere. The pilot answered that the coast was rough and a bad place to come ashore, and that in a storm a lee shore was what he feared most.

Seneca was in no state to weigh that. A sluggish seasickness was racking him, the kind that churns the stomach and never lets it clear. So he laid down the law and made the pilot head for land whether he liked it or not. As they closed in, he did not wait for the proper routine, with the bow turned seaward or the anchor dropped from the front. He remembered that he was a devoted cold-water man and went over the side in his cloak, as a cold-water man should.

Then came the rocks. He scrambled over them, hunting for a path or making one for himself, and learned that sailors have good reason to fear the land. It is hard to believe, he writes, what he put up with when he could not put up with himself. He adds a joke: Ulysses came to grief at every turn, he suggests, because he got seasick, whatever the sea god's grudge. If Seneca ever has to go anywhere by water again, he expects to arrive in the twentieth year.

When his stomach finally settled and a rubdown had restored his body, he began to think about how we treat our faults. A slight chill fools us until a real fever starts to burn and even a tough man admits he is ill. A sore foot gets called a sprain or too much exercise, and only when the ankles swell does anyone say the word gout. Diseases of the mind run the other way, he says: the worse one is, the less one notices it.

His picture for this is sleep. A light sleeper chases dreams and sometimes knows he is dreaming, but a deep sleeper loses even the dreams. That is why nobody confesses a fault while it still has him: only the man who is awake can tell his dream, and owning up to a failing is already a sign of a sound mind.

[[lessons]]
$$**Admitting a fault out loud means you have started to wake up.** A team lead might spend months blaming the season for the same late deliveries, and as long as the cause sits outside her, nothing can be changed. The day she says that her own planning habit is the cause, the problem becomes something she can measure, test and ask others for help with. Left unnamed, a fault keeps working quietly, the way a deep sleeper never learns what he dreamed. Naming it costs a moment of embarrassment instead of another quarter of excuses, and the help you then ask for can be specific.$$

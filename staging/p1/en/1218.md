Ask yourself whether a particular app has any benefit, and the answer will almost always be yes. It keeps you informed, connects you with someone, entertains you for a moment. Cal Newport thinks that's exactly the problem. We're asking the wrong question.

[[story]]
In *Digital Minimalism*, Cal Newport describes a habit so common most of us never notice it. He calls it the **any-benefit mindset**. We adopt a new app, service or platform if we can identify any benefit at all. A social feed helps you stay in touch with old classmates. A news app keeps you informed. A video site teaches you things. Each one, taken alone, can be justified.

The trouble is that this test ignores the costs. Every tool takes some of your time and attention, and many are designed to take as much as possible. Add enough of them together, each with a small benefit, and you can end up with a life that feels fragmented and exhausted, even though every individual choice made sense.

So Newport proposes a different philosophy, which gives the book its title. **Digital minimalism**, as he defines it, means focusing your online time on a small number of carefully selected and optimized activities that strongly support things you value, and then happily missing out on everything else.

##The question isn't "does this have any benefit?" It's "is this the best way to support something I deeply value?"##

That second question is much harder to pass. Maybe you value staying close to old friends. Is scrolling a feed of their updates the best way to do that? Or would a monthly phone call do far more? Maybe you value being informed. Is checking news dozens of times a day the best way, or would reading a few thoughtful sources once a day serve you better?

Newport offers an unexpected example of this kind of thinking: the Amish. The common stereotype is that the Amish reject all modern technology. In fact, many Amish communities do adopt some technologies. What they don't do is adopt them automatically. When a new tool appears, they ask whether it will strengthen or weaken the things they value most, such as family and community life. Sometimes they accept a tool with restrictions. Sometimes they reject it. The key is that the decision is deliberate.

Newport isn't asking readers to become Amish. He's pointing out that their approach, starting with values and then deciding which tools serve them, is the reverse of how most of us operate. We start with the tools and hope our values survive.

Digital minimalists are willing to miss out on small benefits. In exchange, they protect their time and attention for what matters most.

[[lessons]]
$$**"Some benefit" isn't enough.** Nearly every tool offers something, which is why the any-benefit test lets almost anything in. Newport's point is that small benefits come with real costs in attention and time. Judging tools only by what they give, and never by what they take, is how digital clutter builds up.$$

$$**Ask whether it's the best way to serve a value.** Starting from what you deeply value, and then asking which tools serve it best, changes everything. A feed may keep you vaguely in touch, but a phone call might serve friendship far better. The stronger test leads to fewer, better tools.$$

$$**Decide deliberately, like the Amish.** The Amish don't reject all technology; they evaluate it against their values before adopting it. That deliberate step is what most of us skip. Choosing tools on purpose, sometimes with limits, is the heart of digital minimalism, whatever tools you end up keeping.$$

On a flat track of about twenty-five metres in Kenya's Rift Valley, children from a rural primary school run past a recording camera. None of them is wearing shoes, and the school's teachers say that none of its pupils has ever owned a pair. The researchers watch how each foot meets the ground.

[[story]]
The modern running shoe was not invented until the 1970s. For millions of years before that, people ran long distances, and they usually ran barefoot or in thin-soled footwear such as sandals or moccasins. Daniel Lieberman of Harvard and his colleagues asked how those runners coped with the jolt of the foot hitting the ground when there was no cushioned heel to take it.

They compared five groups. Group one was amateur and collegiate athletes around Harvard, all shod since early childhood. Group two was Kalenjin athletes from Kenya, all habitually shod, though 75 percent had not started wearing shoes and training in them until late adolescence. Group three was American runners recruited online who ran barefoot or in minimal shoes. Groups four and five were Kenyan children aged 11 to 16: 16 from a rural school where no child had ever worn shoes, and 16 from an urban school in Eldoret where every child had been shod since early childhood.

Habitually barefoot runners often landed on the front of the foot first and then brought the heel down. Sometimes they landed flat, and less often on the heel. Habitually shod runners mostly struck with the heel. The Kenyan children followed the same split: those who grew up unshod mostly landed on the front of the foot, while the shod children mostly landed on the heel when running in shoes.

The team measured collision forces only in the American groups, because there were no force plates on the Kenyan tracks. For heel-strikers the peak of the landing impact was about 1.9 times body weight barefoot and about 1.7 times in shoes. For barefoot runners who landed on the front of the foot it was about 0.6 times body weight, roughly a third. The authors credited a more plantarflexed foot at landing and a more compliant ankle, which lowered the effective mass of the body that collides with the ground.

They wrote that landing on the forefoot or midfoot was probably more common when humans ran barefoot or in minimal shoes, and that it may protect against some impact-related injuries. Yet the study did not measure injuries. It measured how hard the foot hit the ground, not how often runners got hurt.

So the result reads in two layers. What is settled is that different habits produced different landings and very different collision forces. What remains open is what that does to injury rates, a question this study left to others.

[[lessons]]
$$**To see what a tool changes, look at people who never used it.** Lieberman's team did not simply argue about shoes. They watched children who grew up without them and set them against children who grew up in them, which showed what the cushioned heel makes easy. You can use this at work with a new piece of software, a meeting format or a habit: ask how the job was done before it, because only then does the tool's own contribution show. Keep apart what you measured and what you only hope follows, as this study did when it measured impact and not injury.$$

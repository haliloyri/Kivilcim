[[open]]
On a big screen, slides change one after another. There are 114 of them, and every one is about the products sold by the companies sitting in the room. The people watching are the ones who could change those products with a single decision, and the man clicking through the deck works for one of them.

[[story]]
Michael Moss describes a meeting. In 1999, the chief executives of some of America's largest food and beverage companies gathered in Minneapolis, at Pillsbury, where an executive named James Behnke was the host. Moss says they had come to be shown the emerging obesity crisis.

The first speaker was one of their own. Michael Mudd, a vice president at Kraft, stood up with 114 slides. In Moss's account he laid the problem at the feet of the processed food industry. He asked the executives to look at their main-line products and at the total amounts of salt, sugar, fat and calories in them, and he asked them to act together instead of one company at a time.

The reason for acting together is easy to see. A company that quietly cut the sugar in its cereal risked handing its customers to a rival who did not.

The room answered with defence. According to Moss, the CEOs said they were already as conscious of nutrition as they were of convenience and low prices, and that shoppers had choices in the grocery store. The head of General Mills did not see a need to reformulate all the products on the shelves. Moss adds that the executives feared lost sales if healthier versions turned out to be less appealing than the originals.

Afterward, in Moss's words, they basically went back to what they do.

Both sides of that room had a logic. Mudd was making a coordination argument: if nobody wants to move first, everybody should move at the same moment. The executives were making the other half of the same argument. Each one answered to shareholders, and a product that sold worse was a cost the competitor down the table would not share.

It helps to notice what Mudd did not ask for. He did not ask one company to give up sales on its own; he asked for a standard that would bind all of them at once. Seen that way, the request was less a moral appeal than a proposal to change the rules of the game, so that being first to cut sugar would stop being a disadvantage. It rested on the assumption that the people in the room could trust one another to hold the line, and a deck of slides cannot supply that trust.

So the meeting ended where the problem began. Everyone present was right about their own incentive, and together they were stuck. The 114 slides described the problem in full, but none of them gave a single person in the room a safe way to go first.

[[lessons]]
$$**A shared problem gets solved when someone makes it safe to move first.** Mudd described the problem thoroughly, yet every executive heard only what it would cost their own company. You meet the same wall whenever you bring a problem to peers who each pay for the fix from a separate budget. Before the meeting, ask each person what going first would cost them, and then propose a step that nobody has to take alone. In practice that means your first slide should be the cost to the listener, not the size of the problem, because an argument only starts to work once it answers that question.$$

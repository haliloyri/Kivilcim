At the roadside on the far edge of Johannesburg, a woman stands alone and waves at passing cars. Behind her, a small boy crouches in the bushes, listening. When a car stops, she will whistle once, and he will run out to it from the leaves.

[[story]]
Trevor Noah was five when he and his mother moved to Eden Park, a neighbourhood on the outskirts of Johannesburg, next to several Black townships. His mother saw it as a suitable place for "half-colored and half-black" people like them. It was just the two of them, and Noah describes the move as a shared adventure in which they were a team.

Eden Park was called a suburb, but it sat on the edge of civilization, the kind of development sold to people of modest means with a house and a yard, far from everything else. To live there you needed a car. His mother bought a secondhand, tangerine Volkswagen Beetle, and paid more for it than it was worth. It was unreliable and failed to start about one time in five. It had no air conditioning, and its fan blew leaves and dust onto him.

When the car broke down, they rode minibuses or hitchhiked, and for hitchhiking his mother had a method. Drivers would stop for a woman alone, but not for a woman with a child. So she hid Noah in the bushes at the roadside. She would wave down a car, and when it stopped, she whistled, and Noah ran out to it. Noah recalls the drivers' disappointment at the moment they realised what they had stopped for.

Look at what she did not try to change. A driver who will not stop for a woman with a child is a fact. Rather than arguing with it, she arranged what the driver saw first and when, so that the car had already stopped by the time the child appeared. The logic is simple: once a driver has pulled over, turning down the ride means turning away a mother and her son face to face, which is harder than driving past.

Noah's part in it mattered too: hide, wait for the whistle, run at the right moment. In his telling it was a small, repeatable routine that worked once it was set up.

What stands out is how cheap the solution was. It took a whistle, a bush and good timing, and nothing else. His mother recognised where the problem lay, the refusal that comes at first glance, and put it in a different order.

[[lessons]]
$$**When you notice that something draws a "no" at first glance, think about the order in which people see it.** Noah's mother could not change her child, but she could change which piece of information the driver saw first. In an offer, a promotion talk or a job posting, a constraint placed on the first line can end the conversation before it begins. Lead with the real benefit, meaning what the other person gains, state the constraint openly right after, and do not leave the other person deciding with half the picture. The goal is to order the facts, never to bury them.$$

[[open]]
The description of a private trading venue makes two promises. The system watches order flow all the time, and a surveillance report is run every week. Subscribers can choose to keep the most aggressive traders away from their orders, and the venue says it will honour that choice.

[[story]]
Dark pools, private venues where banks match orders away from public exchanges, belong to the market Michael Lewis describes in *Flash Boys* (2014). One of them was LX, run by Barclays. Its marketing leaned on a feature called Liquidity Profiling, which was supposed to sort traders by how aggressively they behaved and let subscribers block the most aggressive ones.

On January 31, 2016, the SEC announced a settlement, and the New York Attorney General's office announced parallel actions. The SEC's findings were specific. Barclays had said Liquidity Profiling would continuously police order flow in LX and that it ran weekly surveillance reports. According to the SEC it did neither as described.

There was more. Barclays sometimes moved subscribers out of the most aggressive categories without adequately telling anyone, so a subscriber who had chosen to block aggressive traders still traded against them. It also misrepresented the market data feeds it used to calculate the national best bid and offer, using slower feeds alongside direct ones.

Barclays admitted wrongdoing, which in a settlement is not a given. It paid $70 million, split evenly between the SEC and the New York Attorney General at $35 million each. The orders also included a censure, a cease-and-desist order and a third-party consultant to review LX's marketing, its compliance with the Market Access Rule and certain Regulation ATS requirements.

The same day brought a second settlement, with Credit Suisse, in which the bank consented to the orders without admitting or denying the findings. It paid $84.3 million. The SEC found that Credit Suisse had called the scoring in its Crossfinder pool objective and transparent when it had significant subjective elements. It also found that the system accepted and executed more than 117 million illegal sub-penny orders, and that a feature called Crosslink alerted two high-frequency trading firms to customer orders without disclosure.

Look at where each of these findings sits. They concern the distance between a sentence in the marketing and what the machine actually did. Every promise could have been tested, whether it was about weekly reports or about a category a subscriber had chosen. From outside, though, a customer had only the sentence.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask how a promise of protection is checked, and how often.** A gym that advertises daily cleaning, a repair shop that promises tested parts or a vendor that guarantees a backup each night all give you words rather than evidence. Ask what record exists, who looks at it and when you can see it yourself, and ask this before the first problem rather than after it. A vendor who can show you the record in two minutes tells you something, and one who cannot tells you something as well. Either answer is more useful than a second reading of the brochure.$$

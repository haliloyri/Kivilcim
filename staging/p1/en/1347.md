A psychology professor walks into a clothing store with a simple errand. He wants a pair of jeans. Regular jeans, the kind he has always worn. Instead, he is asked which fit he means. Which cut. Which wash. Which style. There used to be just "jeans." Now there is a menu, and by the time he leaves, he will have something better than he came in for and a feeling he did not expect.

[[story]]
The professor is Barry Schwartz, and he opens *The Paradox of Choice*, published in 2004, with this errand. His point is not that shopping is tiresome. It is what happened after the shopping was over.

Once, he recalls, buying jeans meant buying jeans. There was one kind, and you found your size. Now he was being asked which fit, which cut, which wash, which style. So he did what a sensible person does with that many options. He tried them. Many of them.

And it worked, in a way. He walked out with jeans that fit better than any pair he had ever owned.

Then comes the part that makes the story worth telling. He felt worse about the purchase. Not relieved, not pleased with the better fit. Worse.

His explanation is simple once you hear it. All those options had quietly raised his expectations. When there is only one kind of jeans, you expect nothing in particular, and a decent pair is a pleasant surprise. When there are dozens, you start to believe one of them must be perfect. Against that imagined perfect pair, even the best jeans he had ever owned fell short.

##The jeans had improved. What he expected had risen faster.##

And there was a second sting. With so many options on offer, a less-than-perfect result no longer felt like bad luck. It felt like his own mistake. He blamed himself for choosing wrongly, even though he had chosen better than ever.

Schwartz sets this small scene against a much bigger contrast. People in affluent societies have more material comfort and more choice than any generation before them. Yet, he argues, depression has risen, and reported happiness has not kept pace with all that abundance. The jeans are a miniature of the larger puzzle: more options, better results, and no matching gain in how people feel.

To explain who suffers most from this, he draws a line between two kinds of decision makers. **Maximizers** look for the best possible option. They need to know they have checked enough alternatives to be sure. **Satisficers** decide what good enough means by their own standard, and they stop when something meets it. They are not careless; they simply have a finish line. Schwartz reports that maximizers tend to be less satisfied with their choices, even when those choices turn out well.

His remedies follow from the jeans. Satisfice more often, especially where the stakes are low. Keep your expectations under deliberate control instead of letting the size of the menu set them. And pay less attention to what other people have, because every comparison adds another imagined better option to measure yourself against.

None of this means choice is bad. It means that past a certain point, more options can improve the result and shrink the satisfaction at the same time.

[[lessons]]
$$**Satisfaction is a gap, not a score.** The jeans were objectively the best Schwartz had ever owned, and he still felt worse. How a result feels depends on what you expected going in, not only on the result itself. When a wall of options makes you expect perfection, a very good outcome can register as a small failure.$$

$$**A finish line protects you.** The satisficer is not settling for less. They have decided in advance what good enough means and they stop once it is met. The maximizer has no finish line, so every new option reopens the question. That is why maximizers, in Schwartz's account, tend to be less content with choices that work out fine.$$

$$**Pick your comparison point on purpose.** Every alternative you look at, including what friends or neighbours bought, becomes a yardstick for your own choice. Schwartz's advice to pay less attention to what others have is a way of limiting how many imagined better options you carry around. An ordinary result feels different when you stop measuring it against everyone else's.$$

Most sensible advice about money points you toward the middle: not too safe, not too risky, a balanced portfolio. Nassim Nicholas Taleb's example does the opposite. Almost everything goes into the safest place possible, a small slice goes into the riskiest bets available, and nothing sits in the sensible-looking middle.

[[story]]
The idea comes from *The Black Swan*, and it starts from a problem Taleb considers unsolvable. Black Swans, the rare and high-impact events that seem obvious only afterward, cannot be predicted. No model, expert, or forecast will reliably tell you when the next one arrives.

So Taleb changes the question. If you can't know when a shock is coming, the useful thing to ask is how exposed you are to it.

He points out that environments differ. In some, a single rare event can wipe you out. In others, surprises can actually help you. His advice is to arrange things so you limit your exposure to negative Black Swans while staying open to positive ones.

His practical example is what he calls a barbell strategy for investing. Picture the shape of a barbell: heavy weights at each end, nothing in the center.

On one side, you put roughly 85 to 90 percent of your money in extremely safe instruments. On the other, you put the rest in very speculative bets. And you avoid the positions in between, the ones usually described as "medium risk."

##The middle that looks prudent is exactly where Taleb says the hidden exposure to disaster sits.##

The logic runs like this. With the barbell, the worst case is limited and known. If every speculative bet goes to zero, you lose the small slice and nothing else, because the large portion was never at risk. You can see the floor before you start.

The other end works differently. Because those bets are speculative, most may come to nothing. But a rare positive surprise can pay off a great deal. The small slice is where you stay open to a lucky Black Swan.

The middle offers neither advantage. A position labelled "medium risk" may look moderate, but its worst case is harder to see. It can be exposed to exactly the kind of rare shock nobody predicted, without the upside that justifies the danger.

Taleb kept developing these ideas. The second edition of *The Black Swan*, published in 2010, added a postscript essay on robustness and fragility. He later expanded them into a separate book, *Antifragile*.

[[lessons]]
$$**Choose exposure, not forecasts.** Taleb's starting point is that Black Swans can't be predicted. What you can control is how much a shock would hurt you. Limiting your exposure to the bad kind, while leaving room for the good kind, is a decision you can make today without knowing what's coming.$$

$$**Cap the downside first.** In the barbell, roughly 85 to 90 percent sits in extremely safe instruments. That is what makes the speculative slice affordable: its worst case is known and survivable. The boring side is what gives you permission to take a real bet on the other.$$

$$**The comfortable middle can mislead.** "Medium risk" sounds prudent. Taleb's warning is that it can hide exposure to disaster behind a moderate label. A position whose worst case you can't see is harder to protect than one that is openly safe or openly speculative.$$

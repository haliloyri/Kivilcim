In the House of Commons, the Chancellor of the Exchequer rises with a bill in his hand and describes the river that runs past the building. It has, he says, become a Stygian pool, reeking with ineffable and intolerable horrors. Then he gets to the part Members care about most: who pays.

[[story]]
Steven Johnson's *The Ghost Map* is about the Soho cholera outbreak of 1854 and the changes in how cities deal with disease. This story is about the sewers London built a few years later. Their story starts with a speech on 15 July 1858, in the middle of the summer that Londoners came to call the Great Stink. Reports say the heat was so heavy that curtains on the river side of Parliament were soaked in chloride of lime to keep out the smell.

For years the river had received the waste of a city that kept growing. In a hot summer the smell rose from the water and into the windows of the people who ran the country. Whatever else it did, it made the problem impossible to ignore.

Look closely at what the Chancellor, Benjamin Disraeli, proposed, because it is more than an expression of disgust. The bill widened the powers of the Metropolitan Board of Works to purify the Thames and finish the main drainage of London, and it came with the money attached. That was the sharp part. A special sewage rate of no more than threepence in the pound would be collected for forty years, and he put the yield at about £140,000 a year. On top of that, the government would guarantee loans of up to £3,000,000 at no more than four per cent. The Chancellor said the works could hardly be done for much less than £3,000,000, and he expected them finished by the end of 1863. The Board would no longer need approval for each construction from the First Commissioner of Works or from Parliament.

The bill became law on 2 August 1858. Joseph Bazalgette, who had been chief engineer of the Board since 1856, could now build, and construction began in 1859. The scheme included about 82 miles of large intercepting sewers and 1,100 miles of street sewers feeding them. Pumping stations followed at Deptford, Crossness and Abbey Mills, and the Prince of Wales opened Crossness in April 1865. The whole project took about ten years more.

The estimate of 1863 proved too hopeful, as such estimates tend to. Even so, the plan survived because the money for it had been settled on day one.

[[lessons]]
$$**Bring a way to pay for the fix along with the argument for it.** Disraeli's speech stated the problem vividly, but the bill passed because it also named a rate, a time span and a loan guarantee. A decision-maker who must find the funds himself usually postpones. In your own proposal, whether it is a new tool, a repair or a hire, put the cost, the source of the money and the date by which it ends on the first page. People can say yes much faster to a plan that already answers what it costs and who bears it.$$

[[open]]
Every organization pays a tax that isn't on any budget. There's no invoice, no line in the accounts, no one who signs it off. Yet it slows almost everything down. Stephen M. R. Covey gave it a name, and once you've heard it, you start seeing it everywhere.

[[story]]
Most people in business carry around a simple formula for success, even if they never write it down. Get the strategy right. Execute it well. Results follow.

In *The Speed of Trust*, Covey says this formula is missing something. He rewrites it like this:

> (Strategy x Execution) x Trust = Results

Trust isn't just one more ingredient. It's a multiplier. A great strategy, executed by hard-working people, can still produce poor results if trust is low, because everything takes longer and costs more than it should.

Covey calls that extra cost a **trust tax**. You pay it whenever people don't believe each other enough to move quickly. And the frustrating part is that it's invisible. No accountant ever records it.

Picture a manager who doesn't quite trust their team. Every decision needs sign-off. Every important email gets read twice. Meetings grow an extra layer of checking. Nobody is lazy. Everyone is working hard. But a surprising share of that work goes into getting permission rather than getting the job done.

##The team stops solving the real problem and starts solving a different one: how to satisfy the boss.##

Covey lists the forms this tax takes inside organizations. There's **redundancy**, where the same work is checked or done twice. **Bureaucracy**, with its layers of rules and approvals. **Politics**, where energy goes into positioning instead of results. **Disengagement**, where people stay but stop really caring. **Turnover** of good people who get tired of not being trusted. **Churn** among customers and partners who don't trust you either. And at the far end, **fraud**, the outright dishonesty that makes all the other checks feel necessary.

The same logic works in reverse. When trust is high, Covey says, you earn a **trust dividend**. Decisions need fewer people. Bad news gets shared early, because sharing it doesn't feel dangerous. Mistakes surface while they're still cheap to fix. Collaboration becomes faster because nobody is building a case against anyone else.

Covey's claim is that this happens in every relationship, whether anyone measures it or not. You are always either paying the tax or collecting the dividend.

[[lessons]]
$$**Trust multiplies everything else.** Covey's formula, (Strategy x Execution) x Trust, explains why good plans often underperform. If trust is low, even excellent work gets divided by the time spent double-checking, justifying and protecting. Improving trust can do more for results than another round of strategy.$$

$$**The tax hides in normal-looking habits.** Redundancy, bureaucracy and politics rarely look like mistrust. They look like process, caution or just "how things work here." The manager who reads every email thinks they're being careful. Covey asks you to see these habits as payments, and to ask what they're paying for.$$

$$**High trust pays you back.** The trust dividend shows up as speed and candor. When people aren't afraid to report problems early, small issues stay small. That's not a soft benefit. It's time and money you get to keep, every single week.$$

[[reflect]]
- Which step in your weekly routine exists mainly so that someone can feel reassured?
- Look at Covey's list of taxes. Which one does your team pay most often?
- Where are you the one imposing the tax, checking work that no longer needs checking?

&&Which relationship at work, if trust went up, would give you back the most time?&&

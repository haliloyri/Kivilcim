On a Sunday afternoon in London, one executive tells another over the phone that his bank is out. On the other side of the Atlantic, officials who have spent two days building a rescue now have a few hours left. What stopped the deal was a vote that nobody could hold before Monday morning.

[[story]]
Andrew Ross Sorkin's *Too Big to Fail* follows the weekend of 13 and 14 September 2008, when Wall Street tried to find someone to take over Lehman Brothers. Bank of America had pulled out after deciding it could not do a deal without government backing. That left one serious bidder, the British bank Barclays.

On Friday, Treasury Secretary Henry Paulson had called Wall Street's chiefs to Manhattan, and Bob Diamond of Barclays flew in. By Saturday it was clear that Wall Street would not pay for a lifeboat. The British authorities said they would accept a Barclays bid only with a guarantee behind it, from the private sector or from the US Treasury. Barclays' own plan was to split Lehman's hard-to-sell assets into a "bad bank" and buy the rest.

The Treasury refused to give a guarantee. A second obstacle sat in Barclays' own rules: a deal of this size needed its shareholders' approval, and that would have taken more than a month. The Financial Services Authority, Britain's regulator, declined to waive the requirement. Alistair Darling, the Chancellor, spoke to Paulson by phone and ended the call without the assurances he wanted.

At about 2 p.m. in London, Barclays' chief executive, John Varley, told the FSA's Hector Sants that the bank was pulling out. Paulson and the British officials have since given different accounts of who closed the door. Nobody disputes the calendar.

That evening Lehman's chief operating officer, Bart McDade, called Diamond and asked whether Barclays would bid for Lehman out of Chapter 11. Diamond said he would sleep on it. At about 2 a.m. on Monday 15 September, Lehman's parent company filed for bankruptcy, and markets fell sharply when they opened. Barclays did later buy much of Lehman's US business, once regulators on both sides had approved.

Look at how the weekend was built. The people in the room wanted a yes, and several of them had the power to make one. The piece that could still say no was a shareholder vote and a regulator, and neither was at the table or could move faster than its own process. A month-long approval met a deadline measured in hours.

[[lessons]]
$$**List who can say no before you celebrate who said yes.** Agreement among the people in the room says little about the people who are not in it, such as a regulator, a board or a committee with its own calendar. A team lead who promises a client a Friday launch after everyone in the meeting nodded may later find that the security review that can block it sits in another department and takes weeks. Naming those gatekeepers on day one lets you ask them early how long they need and what they will want to see, while the date can still move.$$

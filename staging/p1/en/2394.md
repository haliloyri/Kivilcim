[[open]]
A metal sphere covered in solar cells, about the weight of a heavy adult, circles the Earth. On the ground, a huge horn-shaped antenna in the woods of Maine swings toward it. Thousands of kilometres away, a second antenna waits on the coast of France for a picture that has never crossed an ocean live.

[[story]]
The sphere was Telstar 1, built by Bell Labs and launched from Cape Canaveral on 10 July 1962 aboard a Thor-Delta rocket. It weighed 77 kilograms. Jon Gertner's The Idea Factory counts Telstar among the Labs' wonders, and it was a rare project in which a private company, not a government, paid for the launch: roughly three million dollars, according to IEEE Spectrum's account.

The ground work mattered as much as the satellite. Bell Labs had built a ground station at Andover, Maine, and there was a second at Pleumeur-Bodou in France. Satellite and stations had to find each other, track a fast-moving object, and exchange signals strong enough to turn into pictures. After test signals in the days following the launch, the public broadcast took place on 23 July 1962.

That day, networks in North America and Europe carried live pictures across the Atlantic. The programme included part of a major-league baseball game and remarks from President John F. Kennedy. European audiences watched through Eurovision, and in North America NBC, CBS, ABC and the CBC took the feed. For the first time, a television picture crossed an ocean in the same moment it was made.

Then the satellite began to fail. Radiation from high-altitude nuclear tests carried out by the United States and the Soviet Union damaged its electronics, and Telstar went offline in February 1963. It remains in orbit today. It had worked for months rather than years, and that turned out to be enough.

Why that was enough is the lesson of the project. Telstar was never meant to be permanent. Its job was to prove that live television could be relayed across the Atlantic by a satellite. It proved that in July 1962 in front of an audience, and every later communications satellite that stayed up for years could build on a demonstration nobody could argue with. A doubter could no longer say that it might work in theory.

Notice also how little the experiment tried to do, and how much of the effort went into making one result unmistakable. It carried one kind of signal between two chosen stations, and it was designed so that one clear result, live pictures across the sea, would be visible to everyone at once.

[[lessons]]
$$**Build the prototype to answer one question in public, and accept that it may not last.** A short-lived demonstration can still settle a debate for good, because people trust what they saw happen live far more than what they read in a proposal. Telstar's few months were enough to show that the idea worked, and permanent versions followed. In your own work, pick the one claim that the doubters would believe only if they saw it, design the smallest test that shows it clearly, and do not spend the budget on durability before the answer is in and everyone has seen it.$$

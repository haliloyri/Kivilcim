[[open]]
Two groups of young pea aphids sit in hot air for four hours. Afterwards one group goes on reproducing as before, while the other barely reproduces at all. The difference between them is a single missing letter of DNA, and it is found in the bacteria inside the aphids, not in the aphids themselves.

[[story]]
In Ed Yong's *I Contain Multitudes*, the pea aphid carries a bacterium called *Buchnera aphidicola*, which supplies the amino acids the insect cannot get from plant sap. In return the aphid gives the bacterium energy, carbon and shelter inside specialised cells. This story is about what a small change in that bacterium did to the whole insect, in a 2007 study by Dunbar, Wilson, Ferguson and Moran, published in *PLoS Biology*.

The team looked at the heat-shock response, the way a cell protects itself when temperatures climb. When *Buchnera* gets too warm, a promoter switches on a gene for a heat-shock protein, and the promoter contains a run of adenine letters. Some laboratory lines of the bacterium were missing one adenine from that run. With the shorter promoter, the heat-shock gene switched on less across temperatures from 15 to 35 degrees Celsius.

The cost showed up at high temperature. After four hours at 35 or 38 degrees, young aphids carrying the short-promoter bacteria rarely reproduced, while aphids carrying the normal bacteria had no trouble. As adults, the short-promoter aphids also weighed less, and they held far fewer *Buchnera* than the others. The researchers compared lines that differed only in this one mutation, so the loss of offspring traced back to the bacterium and not to the insect's own genes.

You might expect such a mutation to vanish. It does not, and the reason is cold. At 15 and 20 degrees, aphids with the short-promoter bacteria produced offspring faster than normal aphids did. The authors suggest this advantage in cool conditions helps explain why the mutation persists. Read the other way round, the aphids with the normal promoter buy their heat tolerance at the price of slower reproduction in the cold, so neither version is simply better.

The mutation looks less like a defect than a trade. It persists in the wild, too. Bacteria with the short promoter turned up in about 21 percent of aphids collected in Wisconsin and 13 percent of those from New York, and not in wild populations from Arizona or Utah. The mutation had also arisen and become fixed twice in laboratory stocks. The researchers found similar stretches of repeated adenines near heat-shock genes in the *Buchnera* of other aphid species.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask in which conditions a strength pays and in which it costs.** A feature that helps you under one set of conditions can quietly become a weakness under another, even though nothing about the feature itself has changed. A bakery that runs on a very lean morning shift may earn well in a calm month and fall apart in the week before a holiday. Name the conditions your own strong points were built for, and plan for the moment those conditions shift, so a surprise does not catch you with no slack, and so you notice early when the old advantage has turned into a cost.$$

Tom Waits is driving on a busy Los Angeles freeway when a melody suddenly arrives in his head, the beginning of a song. He has no paper, no pen, no way to record it. Every artist knows the feeling that follows. Then Waits does something unexpected: he starts talking to the song.

[[story]]
Elizabeth Gilbert tells this story in *Big Magic*, as part of her argument that creative people can have a lighter, less tortured relationship with inspiration.

As she tells it, Waits had spent much of his career feeling the pressure every artist knows. Ideas would arrive, often at inconvenient moments, and he would feel a surge of anxiety. What if he lost it? What if this was the one great song he'd never get back? Creativity, in those moments, felt less like a gift and more like a demanding master.

That day on the freeway, the familiar panic began. He could hear the fragment clearly, but he was driving, with no way to capture it. He could feel it slipping away.

And then, instead of panicking, he looked up at the sky and spoke to the song directly.

##Excuse me, he said in effect, can't you see I'm driving?##

If the song really wanted to exist, he told it, it could come back at a better time, when he was sitting at the piano. Or, if it couldn't wait, it could go and bother somebody else. Leonard Cohen, perhaps.

Gilbert says that something shifted for Waits after that. His anxiety eased. He realized the creative process didn't have to be a torment he carried entirely alone. It could be something stranger and more playful: a kind of collaboration between him and whatever force brought the songs. He was a partner in the work, not its prisoner.

For Gilbert, this fits her broader view of creativity. She likes to imagine ideas as a kind of independent energy, looking for someone to work with. Whether or not you believe that literally, the attitude is useful. It takes some of the crushing pressure off the individual artist. You don't have to catch every idea perfectly or live in fear of losing it. You can show up, do your part, and trust that good ideas often return.

Waits's joke about Leonard Cohen is also a reminder that humor helps. Creative work is serious, and it matters. But treating it with a bit of lightness, and even talking back to it, can make the whole process far less frightening, and often more productive.

[[lessons]]
$$**You don't have to carry it alone.** Waits's panic came from feeling that every idea depended entirely on him. Speaking to the song turned the process into a collaboration. Believing that creativity is partly a partnership, however you understand it, can lift an enormous weight off anyone who makes things.$$

$$**Lightness lowers anxiety.** Telling a song to go bother Leonard Cohen is funny, and that's part of why it worked. Humor loosens fear's grip. Taking your work seriously doesn't require taking every moment of it with dread, and a little playfulness often makes the ideas flow more easily.$$

$$**You can set terms with inspiration.** "Come back when I'm at the piano" is a boundary. Ideas don't have to rule your life or arrive only on their schedule. You can capture what you can, trust that strong ideas return, and choose when you'll give them your full attention.$$

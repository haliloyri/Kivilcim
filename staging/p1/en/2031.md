In an insurance company's vault sits a stack of about $300 million in bonds from a firm that makes crackers, cookies and cigarettes. The paper says one thing: this money will be repaid. If the borrower takes on a new debt, the value of that promise can change.

[[story]]
Bryan Burrough and John Helyar's *Barbarians at the Gate* tells the RJR Nabisco takeover through the bankers and executives, but the people who had lent money to the company have a place in it too. This story is about their side. According to Time, RJR's revenues in 1987 were $15.8 billion. Johnson's management group planned to borrow about $17 billion to buy the shares.

Time's issue of 5 December 1988 reported that RJR's roughly $5 billion of bonds had lost about 20 percent of their value in the process. Twenty percent can sound modest. Twenty percent of a $5 billion stack is about a billion dollars. The New York Review of Books noted that Metropolitan Life held some $300 million of RJR Nabisco's existing bonds.

Metropolitan Life and ITT sued, Time reported. Shareholders did not sit still either, and eleven separate class-action suits were filed against the company. The bondholders had nothing in hand but the promise itself. The paper was the same, the interest rate was the same, the maturity date was the same. The one thing that had changed was the load carried by the company behind the paper.

That was part of the larger scene of those years. Time said the value of leveraged buyouts had risen from $13.4 billion in 1984 to $76.8 billion in 1988 up to that point. Buying with borrowed money had stopped being a new idea and had become a movement. The same article recalled that KKR had called Beatrice the "deal of the century" and was left holding businesses that had not yet found buyers. The Brookings Institution estimated that a new recession could push 10 percent of major companies into bankruptcy. The weight of debt was something nobody noticed while business went well.

What teaches here is not whether the bondholders were wronged. They had received a promise, but the promise carried no protection against decisions the promising party might take later. The lawsuit route was open, yet a lawsuit comes after the loss. Preventing the loss is a matter of a clause that can be written before the paper is signed.

[[lessons]]
$$**When the value of an agreement depends on what the other side decides later, have the protection written in before you sign.** If you lend a friend money, you can talk in advance about whether they are about to take on another large debt and how that would affect your repayment. Even if the other side means to keep their word, the strength behind the word changes when their situation does. Asking for such a clause before the agreement is signed does not spoil a good relationship, because both sides know from the start what can change and when, and nobody is surprised later. Ask first, sign second.$$

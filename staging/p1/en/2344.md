[[open]]
On an island in the Canadian Arctic, tree trunks lie in the ground that look as if the sea had washed them up last week. They are about 45 million years old. A geochemist picked up a piece, and the first thing she compared it to was driftwood.

[[story]]
The island is Axel Heiberg, off the northern coast of Canada, and Hope Jahren, the geobiologist who later wrote *Lab Girl*, studied its fossil wood while she worked at Johns Hopkins University. Around 45 million years ago redwood forests grew there. At some sites the wood is so well preserved that, as she told *Science News*, "It's like driftwood." Drying out and slight squashing from the sediment above were the only changes.

That condition made a chemical question possible. A tree builds cellulose out of the water it takes in, and the ratios of oxygen and hydrogen isotopes in that cellulose depend on the air the tree grew in. Jahren and a co-author measured those ratios in the fossil wood to estimate humidity. The result, published in *Geology* in 2003, was that the forest's air held about twice the water vapor of the region's air today, on average.

For temperature the team turned to different samples. In some of the wood a carbonate mineral had seeped in, and the isotope ratios in that mineral gave an estimate of the average annual temperature, about 13 degrees Celsius. That is warm for a place at that latitude, and *Science News* compared the conditions to the spring climate of Oregon's coastal forests today.

Then there is the dark. At that latitude winter means 24 hours of darkness, with no sun to warm the ground. The extra humidity matters because water vapor is a greenhouse gas, and the researchers proposed that it trapped the heat radiating from the ground through those long winter nights. A forest of redwoods in an Arctic winter could survive, in this reading, because the air itself was acting as a blanket.

What stands out is how little the method needed. There was no instrument on the island 45 million years ago, no thermometer, no weather station. There was only wood, and the wood had kept its chemistry. Jahren read the record the trees had written in the only language they had, and the numbers she got described a place that is now ice and tundra.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask what the leftovers of a process can tell you about the conditions around it.** When no one measured something at the time, the answer often survives in the things that were made while it was happening. A café owner trying to understand why a slow season felt so strange can read the stock the kitchen kept, the staff rota and the till rolls, rather than trusting memory alone. Treating those leftovers as evidence lets you reconstruct a past you cannot ask anyone about, and it shows you which records are worth keeping from now on.$$

In a San Diego weight-loss clinic, a doctor notices something that makes no sense. Some of the patients who lose the most weight quit the program and put it all back on. He could keep tracking the numbers. Instead, he starts asking his patients one question more than usual.

[[story]]
Bruce Perry and Oprah Winfrey built *What Happened to You?* around a change of question: from "what is wrong with you?" to "what happened to you?" One of the best-known studies behind that idea began in that clinic.

The doctor was Vincent Felitti, a specialist in preventive medicine at Kaiser Permanente in San Diego, who ran a program for severely obese patients. The program worked on the scale. Yet some who lost a great deal of weight dropped out and regained it. When Felitti asked about their lives, several told him about hard things from childhood, abuse among them. He kept asking, and the pattern did not go away.

He teamed up with Robert Anda, an epidemiologist then at the CDC, and together they turned the hunch into a questionnaire. It asked adults about the years before 18: abuse, violence against their mother, a household member with a drinking or drug problem, a mental illness, thoughts of suicide, or time in prison. The paper they published in 1998 counted seven such categories of adverse childhood experiences.

The form went by mail to 13,494 adults who had already had a medical evaluation at Kaiser. A total of 9,508 sent it back, which is 70.5 percent. More than half reported at least one category. Such events turned out to be common among ordinary members of a health plan, far from a few unusual families.

Then came the counting. The more categories a person had marked, the more likely adult illness became, from heart disease and cancer to chronic lung and liver disease. In one journalist's summary, people with four or more categories had roughly twice the heart disease risk of people with none. The relationship was graded, like a dose.

The researchers' own colleagues add a caution, and it matters. These are associations, not proof of cause. A score is one risk factor among many, alongside genes, diet, smoking and drinking, and it cannot tell you what will happen to any single person.

What the study did was turn a private history into something a doctor could see. A question that once belonged to a few awkward conversations in one clinic became a way of reading thousands of lives, and it began with one doctor who was willing to ask.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask what happened before you decide what is wrong.** Behaviour that looks like a habit, a mood or weak willpower often has a history, and Felitti only saw it because one extra question changed what he was looking at. The same applies to a colleague who goes quiet in meetings or a teenager who shuts the bedroom door: "what happened?" leaves room for their own answer, where a verdict leaves none. The question makes no diagnosis and demands no confession; it keeps you curious a little longer, and that is where understanding starts. It costs you one sentence and a few minutes of listening.$$

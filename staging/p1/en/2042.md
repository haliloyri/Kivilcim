Thirty-three ballots had passed and nobody had won. Two of the Republican Party's biggest names each held hundreds of delegates, and neither could pull a single bloc away from the other. Then an Ohio delegate, who had come to Chicago only to speak for a friend, heard his own name read out.

[[story]]
Candice Millard's *Destiny of the Republic* follows James Garfield from an unlikely nomination to a death that shook the country. This episode is the nomination itself, at the Republican convention in Chicago, which opened on 2 June 1880. Garfield had not come to win it.

Nearly two-thirds of the delegates were pledged to Ulysses S. Grant or James G. Blaine, yet neither man could reach the 370 votes a majority required. On the first ballot Grant had 304, Blaine 284, and Garfield's fellow Ohioan John Sherman 93. Over the next 33 ballots Grant's total never rose above 309 and Blaine's never fell below 275. For the better part of a week-long fight the blocs hardly shifted.

Garfield was an Ohio delegate who had come mainly to support Sherman, and he gave the speech that put Sherman's name forward. According to published accounts, it was an emotional appeal for party unity that barely mentioned Sherman himself. Sherman's best showing was 120 votes, on the 13th ballot. He never climbed any higher than that.

Then, on the 34th ballot, sixteen Wisconsin delegates voted for Garfield, and he protested against the use of his name. Indiana followed on the next ballot, and on the 36th Garfield won with 399 votes while Grant's bloc held at 306. The man who had objected to his own candidacy was now the party's candidate.

For running mate he chose Chester Arthur, who won that vote on the first ballot, 468 to 103 over Washburne. 

Look at the arithmetic. Garfield's 399 cleared the 370-vote bar by 29, while Grant's bloc ended the 36th ballot at 306, within two votes of where it had begun at 304. The winning votes therefore came from outside the largest bloc, from delegates who had been scattered among other names for days.

The logic of the deadlock deserves a second look. Two camps were strong enough to block each other and too weak to win, and the third candidate could not grow past 120. In a standoff like that, a majority does not come from pushing harder; it comes from a name that neither camp has spent thirty ballots fighting. Garfield's speech had argued for unity rather than for a single man, and when Wisconsin broke, two states moved to him within two ballots. Sequence is not proof of cause, but the picture fits.

[[lessons]]
$$**When a group is deadlocked, the person who argues for the whole group's success often becomes the way out.** In a meeting where two camps have dug in, the fifteenth restatement of your own position moves nobody. What does move people is someone who names what everyone wants and asks for nothing for themselves, because both sides can follow that person without conceding anything to the other. You can be that person on an ordinary Tuesday: say what both sides are trying to protect, then propose the one step that protects both. If neither camp has to lose face to follow you, the deadlock has somewhere to go.$$

Picture France in 1900. There are only a few thousand cars in the entire country, commonly estimated at around 3,000. You make tires for a living. Your market is tiny. Most people have never sat behind a wheel. What do you do? Most of us would reach for an ad. Two brothers reached for something else, and it became one of the most famous names in food.

[[story]]
Around 1900, André and Édouard Michelin ran a tire company in France. It was an awkward time to be in the tire business. Cars existed, but barely. The usual estimate is about 3,000 on French roads, and for most people a car was still a rare sight rather than part of daily life.

The brothers understood something simple about their business. They didn't make money when someone bought a car. They made money when someone *drove* it. Every mile wore rubber down, and every worn-out tire needed replacing. So the real question wasn't "How do we get drivers to choose our tires?" It was "How do we get people to drive more?"

The obvious move would have been to advertise. Put the Michelin name in front of the small group of motorists and tell them the tires were good. That might have won a slightly bigger slice of a very small pie.

Instead, in 1900 they published a free guide for motorists. It had nothing to do with tire specifications. It told drivers practical things they actually needed on the road: where to find a mechanic, where to get fuel, which hotels to stay in, where to eat. For someone setting out on a long drive in those early years, that was valuable information. It made a trip feel possible.

Think about what that guide did. It didn't sell tires directly. It gave people reasons and confidence to take the car out and go somewhere. And every one of those trips ended with a little less tread.

Then the guide took on a life of its own. Over the following decades, its restaurant ratings, and later its star system, grew into one of the most respected references in fine dining. Chefs came to see earning a Michelin star as a career-defining achievement. Restaurants built their reputations on it.

##A tire company ended up deciding which restaurants the world's food lovers would travel to.##

And through all of it, the guide never directly advertised tires. It didn't need to. The Michelin name came to stand for something far beyond rubber: judgment, quality, a standard worth trusting.

This story is often told alongside Seth Godin's thinking in *Purple Cow*. It isn't clearly one of the book's core case studies, which lean on examples like the Aeron chair, Krispy Kreme and JetBlue. But it fits his argument closely. Godin's point is that being remarkable, something worth talking about, beats being loud. The Michelin guide was worth talking about. A tire ad would not have been.

[[lessons]]
$$**Useful beats promotional.** The brothers could have printed an ad saying their tires were excellent. Drivers would have glanced at it and moved on. They printed a guide full of mechanics, fuel stops, hotels and meals, something a motorist would keep in the car and pull out on the road. The most effective marketing often doesn't look like marketing. It looks like help, and people choose to hold onto it.$$

$$**Grow the need, not just your share.** With only around 3,000 cars in France, fighting for a bigger slice of existing tire sales had a low ceiling. Michelin went after the thing that created demand in the first place, which was driving. More trips meant more worn tires. When your market is small, ask what makes people need you at all, and make more of that happen.$$

$$**Remarkable outlasts loud.** No one outside the trade remembers tire ads from that era. Yet Michelin stars became a byword for excellence in kitchens around the world. People attach meaning to what surprises and serves them. A tire maker judging restaurants was surprising, and the guide was genuinely useful. Together those two qualities built a reputation no slogan could buy.$$

[[reflect]]
- Michelin's customers needed confidence to take a trip. What does the person you want to reach need that sits one step away from what you sell or do?
- Look at your last few posts, emails or pitches. Would anyone keep them the way drivers kept that guide, or would they scroll past?
- Is your growth stuck fighting over a small pie? What would make more people need what you offer in the first place?

&&What could you offer your customers, followers or circle that is genuinely useful even if they never buy anything from you?&&

A car dealer watches sales tick up and decides to order more cars. It looks like good management: notice the change, respond quickly. But those cars will not show up today. They arrive later, into a situation that has already moved on. In Donella Meadows's version of this story, the dealer who reacts fastest is the one who makes things worst.

[[story]]
In *Thinking in Systems: A Primer*, Donella H. Meadows makes a point that sounds simple and turns out to be slippery. In a feedback loop, information arrives with a delay. So a system is always responding to the past, not the present.

Her main example is a car dealership. The dealer wants to keep a steady inventory of cars on the lot: enough to sell, not so many that they pile up.

Between the dealer and reality sit three delays. The first is a perception delay. The dealer averages sales over a stretch of time before believing a change is real, rather than jumping at one good day. The second is a response delay. Instead of placing one huge order, the dealer spreads orders out over time. The third is a delivery delay. The factory takes time to ship the cars.

Now Meadows runs a simple test. Demand rises slightly and then stays at the new level. Nothing dramatic. You might expect inventory to dip a little and then settle.

It does not settle smoothly. Because of the delays, inventory swings up and down. The dealer sees the lot thinning and orders more. The extra cars are still on their way, so the lot keeps thinning, and the dealer orders more again. Then all those orders arrive, and the lot is overfull. The dealer cuts back, and the cycle turns the other way.

Here is the part that runs against instinct. The obvious fix is to react faster: shorten the perception delay, respond to sales more quickly.

##When the dealer reacts faster, the swings get worse; when the dealer reacts more slowly, they shrink.##

Faster reaction means acting harder on information that is already out of date, while earlier orders are still in the pipeline. Slower, steadier reaction gives those orders time to land before the next correction.

Meadows's conclusion reaches well beyond car lots. Delays strongly shape how a system behaves. Changing the length of a delay, or changing how we respond to one, can change the whole pattern.

[[lessons]]
$$**You are always acting on old news.** The dealer's view of the lot passes through three delays: perceiving the change, responding to it, and waiting for delivery. By the time a decision takes effect, the situation it was meant for has shifted. Any system with a delay works this way, even when it feels immediate.$$

$$**Faster is not always better.** In Meadows's model, reacting faster made the inventory swings worse, and reacting more slowly calmed them. Speed feels responsible, but on a delayed signal it can mean stacking new corrections on top of ones that have not arrived yet.$$

$$**Know the delay before judging the action.** The factory's delivery delay means orders show up long after they are placed. If you judge a decision before its effect can possibly arrive, you will see "no result" and correct again. Knowing how long the answer takes is part of reading whether an action is working.$$

[[reflect]]
- Where did you change course recently before the effect of your first decision could have arrived?
- Which of your decisions take the longest to show results, and do you usually wait that long?
- Is there something in your life that swings between too much and too little, the way the dealer's lot did?

&&What is one change you already made that you should leave alone until its effect has had time to show?&&

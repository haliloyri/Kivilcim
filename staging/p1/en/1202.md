[[open]]
Peter Thiel has a favorite question he asks people in job interviews. It sounds simple. Most people struggle to answer it well. Try it yourself: what important truth do very few people agree with you on?

[[story]]
Thiel opens *Zero to One* with this question, and he admits it's hard. To answer it well, you need knowledge, because you have to see something most people miss. And you need courage, because you have to say something unpopular out loud, to someone who's judging you.

Most people, he says, give weak answers. They name something that sounds bold but that many people already believe, or they name something that's just a matter of taste. A good answer takes the form: *most people believe X, but the truth is the opposite of X.*

The reason Thiel cares so much about this question is that he believes every great business is built around what he calls a **secret**: an important truth that others haven't recognized yet.

He sorts knowledge into three categories. There are **conventions**: easy truths that everyone already knows. There are **mysteries**: things that are impossible to know. And in between, there are **secrets**: truths that are hard to find, but not impossible.

##If you believe there are no secrets left, you won't look for them. And if you don't look, you won't find any.##

Thiel argues that many people have stopped believing secrets exist. They assume that anything important has already been discovered, and anything worth doing has already been done. He blames several forces: incrementalism, the habit of only making small, safe improvements; risk aversion, the fear of being wrong in public; complacency, the comfort of already being successful; and a sense that the world is so connected and competitive that any good idea would already be taken.

He disagrees. He points out that some of the most successful companies of recent years were built on secrets hiding in plain sight. Airbnb saw that millions of spare rooms sat empty while travelers paid high prices for hotels. Ride-sharing companies like Uber and Lyft saw that countless cars sat idle while people struggled to find rides. These weren't secret technologies. They were overlooked truths about resources everyone could see.

The lesson isn't that every contrarian opinion is right. Most aren't. It's that the biggest opportunities are usually found where others aren't looking, in truths that seem unlikely right up until someone proves them.

[[lessons]]
$$**Opportunities hide in unpopular truths.** Thiel's question forces you to find something important that most people don't see yet. That's where great businesses start, because if an opportunity were widely recognized, it would already be taken. Being willing to believe something unpopular, carefully, is where new value comes from.$$

$$**Secrets are hard, not impossible.** Between easy conventions and unknowable mysteries lies the space of secrets. Thiel argues that people stop searching because they assume everything worth knowing is known. Simply believing that undiscovered truths still exist is enough to make you start looking for them.$$

$$**Some secrets are in plain sight.** Airbnb and ride-sharing companies built on spare rooms and idle cars that everyone could see. Their secret wasn't hidden information. It was a way of seeing ordinary things differently. Looking at what's underused around you can be as powerful as looking for something completely new.$$

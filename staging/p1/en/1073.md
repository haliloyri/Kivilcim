[[open]]
Say you look up a bestselling author online. You expect the usual trail: a profile, a feed, maybe an old account nobody deleted. You find nothing. No profile of any kind. Now say this author writes about work, attention and careers, the very topics people usually build big followings around. Is he hiding something? Or did he just decide, a long time ago, that the thing everyone calls essential wasn't?

[[story]]
The author is Cal Newport, and the book is *Deep Work*, published in 2016. Partway through it, Newport mentions something that surprises a lot of readers: he has never had a social media account. Not Facebook, not Twitter, nothing.

That sounds like a quirk until you look at the rest of his life. Newport is a computer science professor at Georgetown University. He's also a writer whose books reach a wide audience. For many people, those two achievements, an academic career and a readership, are exactly why you need social media. You network, you promote, you stay visible. The usual thinking goes something like this:

> If you're not on it, you're invisible.

Newport points out that he built both without a single account. His career is his evidence. The necessity everyone takes for granted turned out, in his case, to be an assumption.

The book's larger argument explains why this matters. Newport says the ability to focus without distraction on cognitively demanding work is getting rarer, and because it's getting rarer, it's getting more valuable. Anything that breaks that focus has a real cost, even when it feels free.

So how do people end up with so many tools that break it? Newport gives the habit a name: the **any-benefit approach**. You adopt a network tool because it offers some possible benefit, or because you worry you'll miss something without it. Under that rule almost anything qualifies. Every app can point to *some* upside. Every platform might help you find an article, a contact, an opportunity. And so the tools pile up.

He proposes a different standard, which he calls the **craftsman approach**. A craftsman doesn't keep a tool just because it could come in handy. First you identify the core factors behind your professional and personal success and happiness, the handful of things that actually move your life forward. Then you adopt a tool only if its positive impact on those factors substantially outweighs its negative impact.

##The question stops being "Could this help me?" and becomes "Does it help enough to be worth what it takes from me?"##

Put that way, a lot of tools fail. Not because they're useless, but because the benefit is small and the cost to your attention isn't.

That's why one of the book's rules is titled "Quit Social Media." Newport doesn't frame it as a rejection of technology. He's a computer scientist, after all. He presents it as a deliberate choice to protect his attention. For him, the platforms didn't clear the craftsman's bar. His career, built without them, suggests he wasn't missing what everyone assumed he'd miss.

[[lessons]]
$$**"Some benefit" is a trap.** The any-benefit approach is how most of us fill our phones: a tool might help, or we might miss something, so we sign up. Newport's point is that nearly every tool passes that test, which makes it no test at all. The real question is whether the benefit clearly outweighs what the tool costs your attention. Small upside plus steady distraction is a loss, even when it doesn't feel like one.$$

$$**Start with your list, not everyone else's.** The craftsman approach begins before you look at any tool. First you name the few things that actually drive your success and happiness. Only then do you judge each app or platform against that list. Newport assumed nothing about what a professor or author "has to" use. He measured against his own core factors, and social media didn't earn a place.$$

$$**Protected focus is an edge.** Newport argues that deep, undistracted work is becoming rare, and that rarity is exactly what makes it valuable. He built a Georgetown professorship and a wide readership without the accounts people treat as mandatory. When most people's attention is scattered, being able to hold yours still is an advantage in its own right.$$

[[reflect]]
- Think of three apps you use daily. Did you adopt each because it offered a clear, large benefit, or just "some benefit"?
- Write down the two or three things that most drive your work. Which apps directly serve them, and which only sit near them?
- Where have you told yourself "everyone in my field does this" without checking whether it's true for you?
- What would a week of the attention those tools take actually buy you?

&&If you judged every app on your phone the way a craftsman judges a tool, which ones would you keep?&&

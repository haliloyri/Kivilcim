[[open]]
Four people sit at supper in a villa a few miles outside Rome, and outside the walls soldiers ring the house. A centurion walks in with a message from the emperor, and it is meant for only one of the guests, the best known man at the table.

[[story]]
The man at the table is Seneca, the Stoic who taught the young Nero and later wrote letters on how to face hardship. Ryan Holiday and Stephen Hanselman's *The Daily Stoic* draws on his writing throughout. This story is about how he spent the evening of his last day, in AD 65, as the Roman historian Tacitus tells it.

Seneca had come back from Campania that day. Towards evening a tribune of the praetorian guard, Granius Silvanus, arrived at the villa, about four miles from Rome, and surrounded it with soldiers. Instead of going in himself, he sent a centurion to tell Seneca that he was to die, on the emperor's order. Seneca was at supper with his wife, Pompeia Paulina, and two friends.

He asked to draw up his will, and the centurion refused. Tacitus says Seneca turned to his friends and told them that, since he could not repay their loyalty with a legacy, he would leave them the best thing he had: the example of his life. If they remembered it, he said, they would be known for their steadfast friendship.

Then he did something that is easy to overlook. His friends were weeping, so he comforted them. He reminded them what philosophy teaches about bearing hardship, and he asked them where their philosophical maxims had gone, since nobody had been unaware of Nero's cruelty. His attention went to the people in the room.

Paulina wished to die with him, and Seneca allowed it. He told her they would leave behind an example of the same steadiness, though the glory would be hers alone. His own end came slowly, and while it came he dictated a farewell discourse to his secretaries. Before the end he asked for a warm bath. Tacitus reports that he sprinkled some of the water on the slaves nearest him and said he was pouring a libation to Jupiter the deliverer.

Nero, fearing the public's disgust at a second cruelty, ordered Paulina spared. Soldiers had her slaves and freedmen bind her wounds. She lived on for several years, left very weak, and honoured her husband's memory.

Notice what was left once the will had been refused. With property out of reach and little time for speeches, the options were few. What Seneca still had was his manner, and he decided to use it for the people who loved him, in the hour when it would be hardest to do so.

[[lessons]]
$$**When you cannot give people anything else, give them your steadiness.** A manager laid off alongside her team has no raises or bonuses left to offer, yet can still be the calm voice that answers the questions in the room. A father who loses his job can still sit at the dinner table and show his children how someone handles a bad week. Steadiness cannot be written into a will, yet others pick it up by watching you, and it is often the thing they remember longest. If you decide in advance that this is what you will give, you also have something to hold on to in a bad hour, because there is always one more person in the room who needs it.$$

Put an airline next to Google. One flies enormous numbers of people across the country every day. The other answers search queries typed into a box. Ask which of the two creates more value, and most people point to the planes.

Now ask which one keeps more of what it earns. That answer flips, and the reason behind it is the whole argument of a short, sharp book.

[[story]]
The book is *Zero to One*, by Peter Thiel with Blake Masters, published in 2014. Its most provocative claim runs against almost everything business culture teaches: competition is not a sign of a healthy business. It is a trap.

Thiel's reasoning starts with what economists call perfect competition. When many firms sell roughly the same thing, each one undercuts the others. Prices fall, margins shrink, and over time the rivals compete the profits away. Nobody earns much in the long run, however hard they work.

His example is the US airline industry. Airlines carry huge numbers of passengers. For all those travelers, they create enormous value. Yet the industry keeps only a tiny profit on each passenger trip. The value is real. It just doesn't stay with the airlines.

Then he turns to Google. In the same year, Google brought in far less revenue than the airlines did. But it kept a large share of that revenue as profit. The difference was not effort or intelligence. Google had no close rival in search.

##Being useful and being profitable turn out to be two different things.##

Thiel adds a twist about how companies talk. A firm that dominates its market tends to play that down, describing itself as one small player in some vast industry. Firms stuck in fierce competition do the opposite. They exaggerate how unique they are, because admitting they are interchangeable is uncomfortable.

The deeper idea is his distinction between two kinds of progress. Going from 1 to n is horizontal progress: copying something that already works and spreading it. Going from 0 to 1 is vertical progress: creating something that did not exist before. Copying feels safe, since the model is proven. But it pulls you straight into competition with everyone else copying the same thing. Creating something new is how you can escape that fight.

So his advice to founders is almost the reverse of "go big." Start small. Pick a narrow market you can dominate, rather than fighting for a sliver of a large, crowded one.

[[lessons]]
$$**Value created is not value kept.** Airlines move vast numbers of people and deliver real value to them, yet they keep only a tiny profit per trip. In a crowded market, the value you create leaks out to customers and rivals. Working hard and being useful are not enough on their own; you also need a position where the value can stay with you.$$

$$**Aim for no close comparison.** Google's large profit share did not come from beating other search engines by a nose. It came from having no close rival in search at all. The goal is to build something different enough that a side-by-side comparison stops making sense, rather than winning a head-to-head race.$$

$$**Copying is the comfortable road into the fight.** Going from 1 to n feels like the low-risk choice because someone already proved the idea works. But everyone else can see that proof too, which is exactly why the space fills up. Going from 0 to 1 is harder to imagine, yet it is the path that can lead out of competition instead of into it.$$

[[reflect]]
- When you describe your work, do you mostly compare it to what others do, or explain what only you do?
- Is there a place where you are fighting hard for a small edge in a crowded field?
- If a rival copied your best idea tomorrow, what would still be yours?

&&Next time you plan a project or pitch, can you write one sentence about what it does that nothing nearby does, without naming a competitor?&&

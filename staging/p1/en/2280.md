[[open]]
A federal complaint lists objects instead of crimes: a jet, hotel properties in two American cities, paintings by two famous artists, a stake in a music catalogue, a Hollywood film. Each item sits on the page like a lot in an auction catalogue. Together they were meant to explain where a country's money had gone.

[[story]]
Tom Wright and Bradley Hope's *Billion Dollar Whale* follows the money that disappeared from 1MDB, a Malaysian state fund, and this story is about the day the US Justice Department put that money on paper. On 20 July 2016, Attorney General Loretta Lynch announced civil forfeiture complaints seeking to recover more than $1 billion. The department called it the largest single action ever brought under its Kleptocracy Asset Recovery Initiative.

The Malaysian government had created 1MDB in 2009 to promote economic development through global partnerships and foreign investment. According to the complaints, between 2009 and 2015 more than $3.5 billion of its money was misappropriated by high-level officials and their associates. The United States was after the slice it could reach, more than $1 billion that the DOJ alleged had been laundered through American financial institutions. These were allegations, not verdicts.

The complaints split the alleged theft into three schemes. In 2009, about $1 billion meant for energy concessions allegedly went through shell companies instead. Then more than $1.3 billion raised in two bond offerings in 2012 was allegedly taken, and $1.2 billion following another bond offering in 2013. The money allegedly passed through shell companies with bank accounts in Singapore, Switzerland, Luxembourg and the United States. Each hop was meant to hide where it started.

At the end of that trail stood things you could photograph. High-end real estate and hotel properties in New York and Los Angeles. A $35 million jet aircraft. Works by Vincent Van Gogh and Claude Monet. An interest in the music publishing rights of EMI Music. The production of the 2013 film *The Wolf of Wall Street*. "The Department of Justice will not allow the American financial system to be used as a conduit for corruption," Lynch said at the announcement.

The method of the complaint is worth a second look. It does not begin with a theory of who is guilty. It begins with purchases, a jet, a painting, a hotel, and walks backwards through each transfer toward the fund. A phrase like "billions went missing" becomes a list a clerk could check line by line: which account, which company, which date, which asset. The department's later filings put the alleged total taken from 1MDB at more than $4.5 billion, but the first complaint already showed how to work: start from what was bought.

[[lessons]]
$$**Trace a vague problem down to the things it bought.** When a household budget leaks or a team's spending feels off, the sentence "money keeps disappearing" gives you nothing to act on, and arguing over who is to blame gives you even less. Write down the purchases instead: each subscription, each contract, each repeat order, with its date and the account it left from. A list like that turns a mood into something two people can check together, and it shows you which single line to question first. Whoever asks "what did we buy?" will usually learn more in ten minutes than a week of guessing about motives would teach.$$

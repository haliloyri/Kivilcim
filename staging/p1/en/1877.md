Shortly after leaving Singapore, one engine of an Airbus A380 comes apart in the air. Pieces strike the wing and the fuselage, and hundreds of passengers sit behind them. Everything started in the wall of a pipe that was thinner than it should have been.

[[story]]
On 4 November 2010 a Qantas Airbus A380, registration VH-OQA, took off from Singapore. Over Batam Island in Indonesia, the No. 2 engine, a Rolls-Royce Trent 900, disintegrated. There were 469 people aboard. Nobody died.

Australia's transport safety bureau, the ATSB, published its final report on 27 June 2013, number AO-2010-089. It traced the failure to a short stub pipe in the engine's oil feed line, where a fatigue crack had formed.

Oil escaped through the crack and caught fire. A turbine disc then separated from the engine. Debris from it struck the wing and the fuselage. Rolls-Royce attributed the crack to a thin wall in the pipe, a flaw of manufacturing.

The crew turned the aircraft back toward Singapore and it landed at Changi, the airport it had taken off from. It was a different aircraft from the one that had left, because by then the damage reached beyond one engine, into the wing and the fuselage.

What stands out is the length of the chain. A pipe wall, a crack, oil, a fire, a disc, a wing, a fuselage: each link followed the one before. In a chain like that, the pilots' problem stops being a single failure and becomes a failure that keeps producing new ones. Knowing a line called "engine failure" falls short, because the next link may not be on any list.

The flaw also was not something that appeared in the air that day. The thin wall was a manufacturing fault, so it was already inside the pipe before the aircraft left the ground. A small difference in a measurement pulled an engine, a wing and 469 lives into the same problem.

One thing should be said plainly. The reports do not tell us what went through the crew's minds in those minutes, and we will not make it up. What is on record is this: the chain was long, each link touched another system, the aircraft landed, and all 469 people survived. Someone who knows how an aircraft is connected can guess where the next link might appear. Someone who knows only the checklist has to wait for its turn.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before a surprise arrives, work out in your head how the parts of the job depend on each other.** A restaurant owner who knows which machine hangs on which circuit can guess at a problem that is on no list when the power goes out. A team manager who has already drawn which task rests on which, in case the most experienced colleague goes on leave, recovers faster at the first sign of a gap. A model like that lets you prepare for the next link in the chain instead of learning every failure from scratch.$$

Picture a ship docking in America in 1848. On board is a Scottish boy from a poor family, one of thousands arriving the same way with the same thin prospects. Decades later, that boy is among the richest men on earth. Most of the others are never heard from again. So what separated him from the crowd on the dock? Napoleon Hill thought he knew, and his answer is less flattering to genius than you might expect.

[[story]]
The boy was Andrew Carnegie. The history is well known, though Hill doesn't walk through it step by step. The family left Scotland poor and came to America with no special advantages. Young Andrew went to work in the jobs open to a boy like him. He worked in a cotton mill, then as a telegraph operator, then on the railroad. Nothing in that list points toward a fortune. Plenty of immigrant boys did the same kind of work, and most of them stayed there.

Carnegie didn't stay. He went on to build one of the largest steel businesses of his era. In 1901 his steel interests became part of the formation of United States Steel.

Most people would say he must have been brilliant, or lucky, or secretly well connected. Napoleon Hill looked at it differently, and he had an unusual vantage point. In *Think and Grow Rich*, Hill says Carnegie himself passed on to him the "secret" of achievement. Carnegie then urged Hill to organize it into a philosophy that other people could use. Much of the book grew out of that encouragement.

So when Hill held Carnegie up as a model, he was pointing at two specific things.

The first was what Hill called a **definite major purpose**. Carnegie didn't scatter himself across every opportunity that came along. He fixed on steel and pursued it persistently. There was one aim, and he held to it, year after year.

The second is the part that surprises people. Hill credits much of Carnegie's fortune to what he called a "Master Mind." This was a group of associates working together in harmony toward the same goal. According to Hill, Carnegie didn't lean on his own expertise in making steel. He leaned on theirs.

##The steel king, in Hill's telling, was not the man who knew the most about steel. He was the man with the clearest aim and the right people around it.##

That changes the question you started with. You could keep asking the obvious one:

> What did he know that the others didn't?

Hill's answer points to a different one:

> What did he decide, and who did he bring with him?

Knowledge, in this reading, can be gathered from other people. Carnegie's associates supplied the technical expertise. What Carnegie supplied was the fixed direction that gave all that expertise somewhere to go, along with the persistence to keep it moving. Hill's point is that poverty didn't decide the outcome. Neither did the mill, the telegraph office or the railroad. What decided it was that at some point Carnegie chose one thing and organized people around it.

It's worth being careful here. Popular retellings sometimes shrink the story to "he just had one goal." Hill's version has two parts working together. There is definiteness of purpose, and there is the alliance of people who could carry it out. A goal with no one to help carry it out goes nowhere. A group of talented people with no shared aim tends to drift. Carnegie, as Hill presents him, had both.

[[lessons]]
$$**One aim gathers effort that would otherwise scatter.** Carnegie had seen several lines of work, including the mill, the telegraph and the railroad. He could easily have kept chasing whatever looked promising next. Instead, in Hill's telling, he committed to steel and stayed there. A definite major purpose does more than motivate. It sorts your time. Every hour either moves the one thing forward or doesn't, and you can see which.$$

$$**You don't need to know everything, just who knows it.** This is Hill's Master Mind point, and it's the most practical part of the story. Carnegie didn't rely on his own expertise in making steel. He relied on associates who had that knowledge and worked together in harmony. His job was to hold the aim steady and keep the right people pulling toward it. A gap in your skills is a reason to recruit, not a reason to quit.$$

$$**Where you start is not what decides where you end.** A poor immigrant boy doing ordinary jobs was about the least likely candidate for steel magnate. The humble beginning was real, but it wasn't the deciding factor. What carried him from the telegraph office to United States Steel was a decision he made and then kept making, persistently, over years.$$

[[reflect]]
- If you had to name one definite aim for the next few years, could you say it in a single sentence, the way Carnegie could say "steel"?
- Think of two or three people whose knowledge would most advance that aim. Are you actually working with them, or just admiring them from a distance?
- Where are you treating a lack of expertise as a stop sign, when it might be a sign to build your own small Master Mind?
- Which of your current projects are the equivalent of the cotton mill, useful for now but not where you're headed?

&&What would you have to stop doing so you could give real time to your one main aim?&&

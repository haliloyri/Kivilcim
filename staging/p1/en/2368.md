Somewhere over the United States, a flight attendant begins the safety announcement, the one passengers have heard so many times that they have stopped listening. Halfway through, the announcement takes an unexpected turn and becomes a joke about leaving your lover, told on a Boeing 737.

[[story]]
"There might be 50 ways to leave your lover, but there are only six ways to leave this Boeing 737." That is the kind of line Chip Heath quoted in an interview with Knowledge at Wharton in 2018, when he talked about Southwest Airlines and the safety announcement, the most scripted minutes of any flight. Southwest's version, in Heath's telling, sometimes makes people laugh.

Heath then offered numbers, and they are worth reading with their source in mind, because they are his account of an analysis rather than a published study. According to him, only about 1.5 percent of flights had these funny announcements. He said doubling that to 3 percent, roughly one flight in 30, was estimated to add $138 million to the bottom line. He also said that passengers who experienced one were likely to fly an extra half flight over the following year, with other variables controlled for.

The logic behind the estimate is easy to follow. The share of flights with a memorable announcement was tiny, so even a modest increase meant that many more people would get one. Each of them was reported to come back slightly more often. A small effect per passenger, multiplied by a huge number of passengers, becomes a large sum.

What makes the example useful for the Heaths' argument is the position of the moment. A flight is mostly routine: boarding, waiting, a drink, landing. The safety announcement is the point where everyone expects nothing at all. That is exactly why a joke there lands, because the surprise arrives where the script is strongest. It costs the airline no equipment and no new route. It asks only that a crew member breaks the pattern for thirty seconds.

Heath also mentioned that funny announcement lines are displayed on placards outside the corporate cafeteria. That detail suggests the company treats the jokes as a craft it values, not as an accident, and tells a new crew member that this kind of thing is welcome.

The point is not that every airline should copy a joke. It is that the most forgettable minutes of a customer's day are also the cheapest place to leave an impression.

[[lessons]]
$$**Break the script where your customers or colleagues expect nothing, because a routine moment is the cheapest place to be remembered.** A meeting opening, a welcome email, a goodbye or a handover all follow a pattern that people stop hearing. If you change one sentence in it, with a joke, a real detail or a plain thank-you, that sentence will probably outlast the rest. Choose the part of your routine that everyone sleepwalks through and ask what one honest, surprising line would do there. It takes more attention than money, and people tend to notice the attention.$$

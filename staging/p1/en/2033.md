A clean offer lands on the table before lunch. Take it, and the competing firms can go home without writing a cheque. The deadline is half past noon. By the time it passes, the offer has gone nowhere, and the people who need to rescue a borrower are back at the same table, with less time left than they had in the morning.

[[story]]
The offer arrived on 23 September 1998, the day the Federal Reserve Bank of New York's account of Long-Term Capital Management says things came to a head, and it is one of the turning points of the LTCM story told in Roger Lowenstein's *When Genius Failed*.

Go back two days. According to the President's Working Group report of 1999, by 21 September the fund's liquidity was bleak, and further bad market moves could have pushed it into default as early as Wednesday the 23rd. The firms that had lent to LTCM and traded with it were the ones with the most to lose. On Tuesday the 22nd, four of the most worried counterparties formed a core group and began looking for something better than default.

Their idea was to recapitalize the fund with joint investments from its main counterparties. The New York Fed provided the facilities for the talks and encouraged the firms to find the least disruptive solution they believed was in their own interest. Alan Greenspan later told Congress that no Federal Reserve funds were put at risk and that no individual firm was pressured to take part.

Then, late on the morning of the 23rd, another investor group offered to buy out the fund's partners. The Fed's history of the episode describes it as a group led by Warren Buffett. It looked like a clean answer, and the consortium talks were suspended. But the offer was not accepted by the 12:30 p.m. deadline, because of reported legal issues, and the talks started up again.

Around six that evening, an agreement came together. Fourteen firms put in about 3.6 billion dollars of new equity and received 90 percent of the fund's portfolio along with operational control. The original owners kept 10 percent. The money was repaid, too: LTCM returned the consortium's investment by the end of 1999.

Nobody at that table acted out of charity. The Working Group report points out that the burden stayed with the counterparties who had let LTCM build such huge positions, and each of them was weighing a certain contribution against a larger expected loss in a default. The agreement came only after the firms were convinced that no alternative to default existed.

The Fed's own part was small in money and large in effect: it supplied a room and a deadline, and the rest was arithmetic.

[[lessons]]
$$**When a shared problem is stuck, bring the people who would lose most into one room and put the numbers in front of everyone.** In a project crisis, a family debt or a stalled group plan, each side tends to run its own sums in private and wait for someone else to move first. Around one table, with the same figures, each side can see what waiting would cost it. You do not have to persuade anyone with speeches; you only have to make each person's stake visible. Call the meeting yourself, and write each person's share on the page so nobody has to guess it.$$

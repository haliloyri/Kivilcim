A patient reaches for an object, and when asked why that one, answers at once, clearly and sensibly. The part of the brain that gave the answer never saw what moved the hand. It explains anyway, and it sounds completely sure.

[[story]]
Some people have epilepsy so severe that no drug controls it. For a number of these patients, surgeons cut the corpus callosum, the bundle of fibers that connects the brain's two hemispheres, so that seizures can no longer spread from one side to the other.

You might expect dramatic changes afterward. In everyday life these "split-brain" patients seem largely normal. They walk, they talk, they carry on with their lives.

The difference shows up in the lab, and it depends on how vision is wired. What you see on the left side of your visual field reaches the right hemisphere, and what you see on the right reaches the left. In an intact brain the two sides share everything instantly across the corpus callosum. In a split-brain patient they cannot, because the bridge between them is gone.

So researchers flash an image briefly to one visual field only. When it appears on the left, just the right hemisphere receives it. That hemisphere controls the left hand, and when the patient is asked to pick the matching object, the left hand picks correctly.

Then comes the part that makes the experiment famous: asking the patient why. Speech is handled mainly by the left hemisphere, and the left hemisphere never saw the image. It has no idea what drove the hand. The honest answer would be "I don't know." That is rarely what comes out. ##The talking hemisphere supplies a fluent, plausible reason for the choice, and the reason is false.##

Researchers, notably Michael Gazzaniga, call this tendency of the left hemisphere the interpreter: a system that builds a coherent story from whatever it has, even when the real information is missing.

Neuroscientist David Eagleman uses this research to argue something broader. Much of our behavior, he says, is driven by unconscious processes, and the conscious mind often writes the justification afterward. The hand acts. The voice narrates.

[[lessons]]
$$**The one who explains is not always the one who decided.** The left hand chose correctly because the right hemisphere saw the image. The explanation came from a hemisphere that saw nothing. When you hear yourself give a reason, you are hearing the talking part, and it may not have made the call.$$

$$**A good story beats "I don't know."** Patients rarely admit ignorance. The interpreter closes gaps instead of reporting them, so a confident explanation, yours included, tells you little about whether it is accurate.$$

[[reflect]]
- Think of a recent choice you explained easily. Did the reason come before the decision, or only once someone asked?
- When an explanation arrives instantly and tidy, what would you need to see before you believed it?

&&Is there a habit you defend or a person you avoid where your "why" has always felt a little too neat?&&

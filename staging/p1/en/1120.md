[[open]]
Picture someone reaching for an object without hesitating. You ask why they picked it, and they give you a crisp, sensible answer right away. Now suppose the part of their brain doing the talking never saw what drove that choice. They aren't lying. They believe every word. That is exactly what happens in a certain kind of patient, and it may be happening to you more often than you'd like.

[[story]]
The story starts with a drastic operation. Some people have epilepsy so severe that no drug can control it. For a number of these patients, surgeons cut the corpus callosum, the thick bundle of fibers that connects the brain's two hemispheres. With that bridge gone, a seizure can't spread from one side to the other.

You might expect the results to be catastrophic. They aren't. In everyday life these "split-brain" patients seem largely normal. They walk, they talk, they go back to their jobs and families. If you met one at a party, you probably wouldn't notice anything unusual.

The strangeness only shows up in the lab. Researchers take advantage of how vision is wired. Each eye feeds both hemispheres, but each half of your *visual field* goes to one side. What you see on your left is processed by the right hemisphere, and what you see on your right goes to the left. In a healthy brain the two sides share everything instantly across the corpus callosum. In a split-brain patient they can't.

So the patient stares at a point in the middle of a screen, and an image flashes briefly in the left visual field. Only the right hemisphere receives it. The right hemisphere controls the left hand, and when the patient is asked to pick the matching object, the left hand goes to the right one.

Here is the catch. Speech is handled mainly by the left hemisphere, and the left hemisphere never saw the image. It has no idea why the hand chose what it chose. The honest answer would be simple:

> "I don't know."

That is almost never what comes out. ##Instead, the talking hemisphere produces a fluent, plausible explanation for the choice, and it is completely made up.## The patient isn't hesitating or covering up. The reason arrives smoothly, delivered with the same confidence any of us would use to explain our own behavior.

Researchers, most notably Michael Gazzaniga, gave this tendency of the left hemisphere a name: the **interpreter**. It is a system that builds a coherent story out of whatever it has, even when it is missing the real information. When the facts aren't available, it fills the gap with something that sounds right.

David Eagleman takes up this research in his 2015 book on the brain, published in Turkish as *Beyin Fırtınası*, and he pushes it further. His argument is that the split-brain lab doesn't reveal a quirk of damaged brains. It reveals how all brains work. Much of our behavior is driven by unconscious processes we have no direct access to, and the conscious mind often writes the justification afterward. The hand acts. The voice narrates.

[[lessons]]
$$**The explainer isn't the decider.** In the lab, the left hand chooses correctly because the right hemisphere saw the image. The explanation comes from the left hemisphere, which saw nothing. Two different systems are involved: one had the information and acted on it, and the other had none and talked anyway. When you hear yourself explain a choice, you are hearing the talking part. That doesn't guarantee it's the part that made the call.$$

$$**A good story beats "I don't know."** The most striking detail is what split-brain patients don't say. They almost never admit ignorance. They offer a reason that sounds sensible and is wrong, and they offer it sincerely. The interpreter isn't built to report gaps. It's built to close them. That's why a confident explanation, your own included, tells you very little about whether it's accurate.$$

$$**Everyone carries an interpreter.** Eagleman's point is that the split brain only exposes something that is always running. In an intact brain the stitching is seamless, so you never catch the moment when a reason gets written after the fact. Many of your "because" statements are reconstructions: honest, fluent, and possibly unrelated to what actually moved you.$$

[[reflect]]
- Think of a recent choice you explained easily. Did the reason come before the decision, or did it show up once someone asked?
- How would you know if your stated reason was the real cause? What evidence would change your mind?
- Notice when an explanation shows up almost instantly. Speed and smoothness are exactly what the interpreter produces.
- Is there a pattern in your life, like a person you avoid or a habit you defend, where your "why" has always felt a little too tidy?

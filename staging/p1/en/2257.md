[[open]]
A reporter puts a plain question to a credit analyst at a rating agency, about one of the biggest companies in America: how does it actually make its money? The analyst does not give a number. He gives her one short sentence, and the sentence is an admission.

[[story]]
"If you figure it out, let me know." That was the answer Todd Shipman of Standard & Poor's gave Bethany McLean, then a writer at *Fortune*, when she asked how Enron made its money. At Fitch, Ralph Pellecchia had a reply almost as short: "Do you have a year?" McLean later wrote *The Smartest Guys in the Room* with Peter Elkind, but first she turned the question into the headline of her *Fortune* story of 5 March 2001: "Is Enron Overpriced?"

The numbers made it a fair question. Enron's stock traded at roughly 55 times its trailing earnings, more than twice the multiple of the S&P 500. The company said the shares were worth $126, more than half again above the market price, and 13 of the 18 analysts who followed it rated the stock a buy. Yet its return on invested capital was about 7 percent, not far from what Treasury bonds paid.

The business had changed shape. In 1990 about 80 percent of Enron's revenue came from regulated gas pipelines, and in 2000 the wholesale energy operations produced 95 percent of a revenue figure that had passed $100 billion. Enron marked those contracts to market, so changes in their estimated value flowed into earnings whether or not any cash arrived. In the first nine months of 2000, the article reported, operations had generated only about $100 million in cash, and net debt stood near $13 billion.

The company would not fill the gaps. Fortune quoted chief financial officer Andrew Fastow: "We don't want to tell anyone where we're making money." Details were kept confidential for "competitive reasons." One analyst called Enron "a big black box," and Robert Winters of Bear Stearns said that building a predictable model of the business was "mostly an exercise in futility."

Broadband showed how wide the range of guesses was. That unit reported $408 million in revenue and a $60 million loss for 2000. Enron valued it at $40 a share, some analysts at $25, and Winters at about $5.

The article did not predict a collapse. It laid the figures side by side and asked a question. Over the previous year the shares had returned 89 percent and earnings had risen 25 percent. Enron filed for bankruptcy protection on 2 December 2001, nine months after the piece ran.

[[lessons]]
$$**Count an unanswered "how does this earn money?" as information.** When a friend pitches you a side business and cannot say in two sentences where the income comes from, the missing sentence tells you more than the glossy projection does. The same goes for a supplier whose price looks too good or a project whose benefit nobody on the team can trace. Before you commit time or savings, ask for the plain answer and notice how it arrives: if it comes as confidence, a sketch or a request that you trust the people involved, that tells you what the numbers do not.$$

[[open]]
Picture a quiet room with flowers at the front and soft music playing. People you know are talking in low voices, and some of them have been crying. You walk down the aisle and look into the casket, and the face you see is your own. Four people are about to stand up and talk about your life. You already know what you hope they'll say. The harder question is whether any of them could say it honestly.

[[story]]
That scene isn't a nightmare. It's an exercise Stephen R. Covey asks you to try in *The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People*, published in 1989. It opens his chapter on Habit 2, which he calls "Begin with the End in Mind."

He sets it up carefully. You enter a funeral. You see grieving friends and family. Then you realize the funeral is yours. There's a program, and four people will speak. The first is from your family. The second is a friend. The third is someone from your work or profession. The fourth is from your church or a community organization you've been part of.

Then Covey asks you to stop and think about each speaker in turn. The questions go roughly like this:

> What would you want this person to say about your character?

> What contributions and achievements would you want them to remember?

> What difference would you want them to say you made in their life?

The exercise works because it doesn't let you hide behind a job title or a busy calendar. You have to answer as the people who actually lived alongside you. Covey argues that if you answer honestly, you find your deepest values. And those values often look very different from what fills your daily schedule. Nobody pictures the family speaker praising how fast they cleared their inbox.

That gap is his real subject. Many people work hard at goals they never deliberately chose, and Covey has an image for it. You can climb the ladder of success, rung after rung, faster and faster.

##Then you reach the top and discover the ladder was leaning against the wrong wall the whole time.##

The trouble in that picture isn't laziness. The climber worked hard. The effort just went toward the wrong place.

To explain how you avoid that, Covey states a principle: all things are created twice. First comes the mental creation, then the physical one. A house exists as a plan before anyone pours the foundation. A life works the same way. What you do each day is the physical creation, and it follows some picture of where you're headed. That picture may be one you drew yourself. It may also be one you absorbed from other people's expectations without noticing. Beginning with the end in mind means taking charge of that first creation, so the blueprint is actually yours.

Covey also gives the exercise a practical next step. He recommends writing a personal mission statement. It sets out what you want to be, what you want to do, and the principles those things rest on. Think of it as the funeral speeches turned into a guide you can check against a Tuesday afternoon.

[[lessons]]
$$**The eulogy is a better map than the to-do list.** Covey picks four specific speakers: family, friend, colleague, community member. Each one sees a different side of you, so together they cover your whole life. Your task list only tells you what's urgent. The words you'd want from those four people tell you what you actually value. If the two don't match, the list is the one that needs to change.$$

$$**Speed means nothing if the wall is wrong.** The climber in Covey's image isn't failing. He's succeeding faster and faster, and that's what makes the image uncomfortable. Hard work can hide a bad direction, because every rung feels like progress. Before you ask how to go faster, ask whether you'd be glad to reach the top of this particular ladder.$$

$$**Draw your own blueprint, then write it down.** "All things are created twice" means some mental picture is always steering your days. If you don't create it on purpose, other people's expectations will fill it in. The personal mission statement is Covey's answer. It's a written statement of who you want to be, what you want to do, and the principles behind both, and you can check each choice against it.$$

[[reflect]]
- Go through the four speakers one at a time. Which of them would struggle most to say something true and good about you today?
- Look back at what took up most of your time this past week. Would any of it come up in a eulogy?
- Think about the ladder you're climbing right now. Whose expectations put it against that wall, and are they also your own?
- Try writing the family speaker's first sentence. Notice whether it came easily or whether you had to stretch.

&&If the people who knew you best could say only one sentence about you, what would you want it to be, and does your life today back it up?&&

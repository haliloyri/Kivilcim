[[open]]
A stadium full of graduates in black gowns listens to a man who left college himself. He tells them that the only way to do great work is to love what you do, and the crowd applauds. Years later a reader goes back over the same speech and asks a different question: what did that man love when he was young?

[[story]]
Steve Jobs gave his commencement speech at Stanford on 12 June 2005, and Cal Newport opens *So Good They Can't Ignore You* with it. Jobs told the graduates three stories. In one of them he said he was fired from Apple at thirty, a very public failure, and that the only thing that kept him going was that he still loved what he did. He ended with the advice people quote most: the only way to do great work is to love what you do, and if you have not found it yet, keep looking.

Newport's objection is about the order of events. According to his account, the young Jobs was not burning to build computers. After a short time at Reed College he dropped out, and in those years his passion, Newport argues, was Zen Buddhism. A Jobs who had followed only what he loved at that moment, Newport says, would probably have ended up a Zen monk, not the founder of a technology company.

The speech itself supplies the next fact. Jobs says he started Apple with Steve Wozniak in his parents' garage when he was twenty. Newport reads that as an opportunity that appeared, not a calling that had been waiting. Jobs saw a chance and took it, and the love for the work grew as the company did.

Then look at the numbers Jobs gives. In ten years, he says, Apple went from the two of them in a garage to a company with more than four thousand employees. When he was pushed out at thirty, he had spent about a decade learning how to build a company. The sentence "I still loved what I did" comes from a man with ten years of hard, absorbing work behind him.

That is the reading Newport offers. The speech tells students to find love first and work second. The life it describes often ran the other way: work first, skill second, love as a result. Newport does not say passion is fake. He says it is usually built, and that advice which skips the building leaves graduates waiting for a feeling that may arrive only after the years of effort. A graduating student who takes the speech literally goes looking for a feeling, when the useful move is to pick a craft and start getting good at it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask what you could get good at before you ask what you love.** A speaker's success story is told from the end, where the love and the skill already sit together, and it hides the years in which only the skill was growing. When you choose a project, a role or a field, look for the thing where steady practice will make you rare and valuable, and expect the feeling to follow. Give the choice a fixed stretch, say two years of honest effort, and judge it by how much better you have become. If you wait for a calling to announce itself, you may wait a long time, and meanwhile nothing is being built.$$

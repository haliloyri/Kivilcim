"All day, you gotta do what they say. But lunchtime is all yours." The line was written for children, and it sold a packaged lunch, yet it says nothing about what is inside the box. It promises something else entirely: a few minutes in which a kid is in charge.

[[story]]
Michael Moss traces the Lunchables to Bob Drane, an executive at Oscar Mayer, in the 1980s. The company had a problem. Red meat sales were falling, and a meat company needs people to keep buying meat. Drane's task was to find a new way to sell it.

The first answer was convenience. The product was aimed at working mothers who needed lunch to be quick to pack. Then the target moved to the children themselves, and that is where the idea found its voice. Moss puts it in a sentence: the Lunchables was not about food, it was about empowerment for kids. The slogan about lunchtime being all yours said exactly that.

Moss describes Drane's own motive in modest terms. He was most interested in saving jobs, and he cared a great deal about the company. The product was a way to give a struggling line of business a reason to exist.

It worked as a fix, and it did not stay small. Over time the Lunchables grew into a family of versions, with hot dogs, hamburgers, pancakes and pizza, and with desserts. Moss describes many of them as heavy in fat, sugar and salt.

The shape of the story is worth following. The company began with a sales number, and the number alone pointed nowhere. Nothing in it said what to make. The answer arrived only when the question changed from "how do we sell more meat?" to "who is making this choice at lunchtime, and what do they lack?" A child at school does not lack a sandwich. The child lacks control over a day that other people run.

The motive matters to the logic. Behind the falling number stood real jobs, and Drane's concern for them is the human side of that number. First came a need that parents recognized, then a feeling that children recognized, so the story has two stages. The figure said that sales were dropping, but it did not say whom to sell to, why, or with what feeling.

Notice also how little the slogan constrains the box. A promise about feeling can be kept by almost any contents, which is why the later versions could travel so far from the first one while the line on the package still held. The pitch was about the child, and the child was never asked what was in the tray.

[[lessons]]
$$**Look for the feeling your customer is missing before you try to fix the number in your own report.** Oscar Mayer's problem was falling meat sales, but the way out began with asking what a child lacks at lunchtime. In your own work the matching question is what the person in front of you wants to feel after using what you offer. Treat the number as the start of the question, not the answer. Answer that, and the product, the pitch and the wording often follow from it, but check that the promise still matches what is actually in the box.$$

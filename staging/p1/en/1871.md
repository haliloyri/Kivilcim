A tiny family office in New York buys protection against mortgage products built by the biggest banks in the world. The premium it pays is small. If the bet comes good, the return will be eighty times the money put in.

[[story]]
Cornwall Capital is one of the odder stories in *The Big Short*, because it has neither a Wall Street giant nor a famous fund manager at its centre. Jamie Mai founded Cornwall in 2003 as a family office to manage the capital of his father, Vincent Mai. Charlie Ledley joined him soon afterwards and left in 2009. Ben Hockett arrived in 2005 as head trader. So this was a small outfit in New York that hardly anybody had heard of.

Its job was to find things the market had priced wrongly. Protection against bonds backed by subprime mortgages, the credit default swaps, looked exactly like that to them. It was cheap to buy because the market gave almost no weight to the chance that these bonds would collapse. If they did collapse, the payout would be many times the premium that had been paid.

The logic is simple. What Cornwall could lose was the premium, a small and known amount. What it could win was far larger. In a bet like that, being wrong is fairly ordinary and the price of it was paid at the start. Being right just once covers the cost of many small errors. A small price has another advantage: after a failed attempt there is still capital left to play with and a clear head, so the team can take the next opportunity as well.

When the housing market fell apart, Cornwall's subprime trade returned 80 times the money first put into it. Lewis estimated that only about 20 investors in the world made this bet. In a gigantic market, the number who stood on the right side would not have filled a bus.

What deserves attention is not any claim that Cornwall was cleverer than everyone else. Instead of trying to guess how often a bet would come off, they compared how much they would lose if it failed with how much they would gain if it worked. The asymmetry was on their side. The market's mistake, with everyone assuming collapse was unthinkable and so keeping the price of protection low, was the raw material of the bet. That is a lesson any team can use: you do not need to see the future better than the crowd, only to notice where the price of an outcome has been set too low.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you decide, stop asking "am I right?" and ask "what do I lose if I am wrong, and what do I gain if I am right?"** When you start something new or run a small trial, a low ceiling on the loss and a high ceiling on the gain make being wrong cheap and being right valuable. Cornwall's ordinary-looking premium was exactly that imbalance. Write the price and the payoff side by side whenever you weigh an option. That way you can back something you do not know for certain, at a cost you have capped in advance.$$

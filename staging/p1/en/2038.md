[[open]]
Through a periscope the world is a small bright circle. A submarine commander has been told to go home: fuel is low, the fog is thick, and nothing has been worth a torpedo all morning. Then a long dark shape with four funnels slides into the circle.

[[story]]
Kapitänleutnant Walther Schwieger commanded the German submarine U-20 in the waters south of Ireland, where Erik Larson places the final act of *Dead Wake*. On 7 May 1915 his boat had poor visibility and little fuel, and by published accounts he had already decided to abandon his plan to enter the Irish Sea and head back to Germany. The cruiser Juno was said to have passed him that day, zigzagging, out of reach.

Then came the liner, a long dark shape with four funnels. U-20 had three torpedoes left, and Schwieger's log records that he fired only one at her. The torpedo struck the starboard side near the bridge. What followed surprised him. The National Archives in Washington holds a copy of his diary, and in its translation he writes of an "extraordinarily heavy detonation" and a very large cloud of smoke, and notes that "a second explosion must have followed that of the torpedo." Historians still disagree about what the second explosion was. Schwieger himself could only guess at boilers, coal dust or something else on board.

The ship sank in under twenty minutes. Of the 1,959 people aboard, about 1,195 died, among them many women and children. Published translations of the log differ in wording, but they agree on one point: Schwieger did not fire again, and wrote that he did not want to send a second torpedo into people struggling to save their lives.

U-20 then turned out to sea and went home. Published histories say it kept radio silence until it was almost home. Schwieger went on to sink dozens of ships in all, and on 5 September 1917 he died when his later boat, U-88, was lost, most likely to a mine, with all 43 men aboard.

What this episode shows is the distance built into his job. A periscope shows a hull, a speed and an angle, nothing more. The people inside the hull, the number of lifeboats, how fast water would fill the ship, all stayed outside the circle of glass. Schwieger's own surprise at the second explosion is a small proof of that: even the man who pressed the firing button had not seen what he was about to cause.

[[lessons]]
$$**When you act from a distance, deliberately bring in the picture you cannot see.** A periscope, a dashboard or a spreadsheet shows only what its designer chose to show, and the damage a decision causes usually lies outside the frame. Before you approve a layoff, a cut or a rule change, ask three questions: who is on the other end, what happens after the first effect, and what would I notice if I were standing next to it. Schwieger's log shows how an informed professional can still be surprised by the size of what he started. You cannot see everything, but you can ask what is missing from your view.$$

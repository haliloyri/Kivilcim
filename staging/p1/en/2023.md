Every evening a man on a psychiatric ward writes in a small notebook. The nurses' record for that man reads: "engaged in writing behavior." The man is perfectly well, and nothing he does can show it, because on this ward his notebook is read as a symptom and not as the diary of a healthy person.

[[story]]
The man belonged to a study by David Rosenhan, a Stanford professor, published in *Science* in 1973 as "On Being Sane in Insane Places." It sits in the history of how psychiatry decides who is ill, the history that Jon Ronson's *The Psychopath Test* explores. Rosenhan asked a simple question: could a psychiatric hospital tell a healthy person from a sick one?

He and seven other healthy volunteers sought admission to twelve hospitals in five US states. Each reported one symptom only, a voice that seemed to say "empty," "hollow" or "thud." They gave false names and jobs and were honest about everything else. Once admitted, they stopped mentioning the voice and said they felt fine.

All of them were admitted. All but one were diagnosed with schizophrenia; the exception received a diagnosis of manic-depressive psychosis. Stays ran from 7 to 52 days and averaged 19. Most were discharged with the label "schizophrenia in remission," which treated the illness as quiet rather than absent. No member of staff spotted an impostor.

Other patients did. According to Rosenhan, 35 of the 118 patients on the wards voiced suspicion that the newcomers were not ill. The pseudopatients wrote notes on their days, and a nurse logged that as a symptom. Contact with doctors averaged roughly seven minutes a day.

Rosenhan then ran the test in reverse. A research hospital had said such mistakes could not happen to it, so he told it that over three months he would send in impostors. Staff rated 193 new patients for how likely each was to be one, and 41 were judged impostors. Rosenhan had sent nobody.

Rosenhan's conclusion was that context decides how behavior is read. Once the label was on, everything the pseudopatients did was interpreted from inside it, and note-taking looked like part of the diagnosis rather than a habit.

The study has been questioned since. In 1975 the psychiatrist Robert Spitzer argued that diagnosis rests on what patients say, so walking in with an invented symptom tells you little about whether diagnosis is valid. In 2019 the journalist Susannah Cahalan, in *The Great Pretender*, challenged the accuracy of Rosenhan's account and could trace only two of the eight pseudopatients. The paper changed how people thought about diagnosis, even if its data now carries an asterisk.

[[lessons]]
$$**Write down what would contradict your first impression of someone, before it hardens into a label.** Say you decide a new colleague is unmotivated because of a quiet first week. From then on every silence in a meeting confirms it, and the colleague has little chance of surprising you. If you note on day one which behavior would make you rethink, such as a question asked unprompted or a task finished early, you give yourself something concrete to look for. A month on, you can check the note against what actually happened, not against your memory of it, and see whether the label still fits the person.$$

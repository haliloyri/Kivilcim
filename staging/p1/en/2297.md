A finance professor carries a glass jar into a classroom. It is full of jelly beans. He puts it on the desk, hands out slips of paper, and asks every student to write down how many beans are inside. Nobody may count and nobody may confer.

[[story]]
James Surowiecki describes this classroom exercise in *The Wisdom of Crowds*, and it is usually credited to the finance professor Jack Treynor. The setup is simple enough for anyone to repeat. A jar holds some hundreds of jelly beans. The students look at it and write a number. Then the professor collects the slips and does two things: he averages the guesses, and he checks how close each student came.

In the version usually reported, the jar held 850 beans and the class had 56 students. The group's average guess was 871, only 21 beans too high. Only one of the 56 students, according to the account, guessed closer than the group did. So 55 people, many of whom surely felt sure of their own eye, would have done better to hand their slip to the class average.

This is a classroom story told through secondary retellings, so the exact figures are worth treating as approximate. The pattern is the interesting part, and it shows up whenever people try it: the average of a crowd's independent guesses beats nearly all of the individuals in it.

Look at what this says about confidence. Every student looked at the same jar and formed an impression, but each impression was slightly off. One person's eye overestimates, another's underestimates, one forgets that beans pack tightly, another counts the layers oddly. Their mistakes pointed in different directions, so averaging them cancelled most of the errors. The student who beat the group most likely did not have a better method. In a class of 56, someone's errors will happen to land close to zero by luck.

That is the uncomfortable part. When you are one of the 56, you cannot tell whether you are the lucky student or one of the 55. You feel exactly the same either way. A good guess feels as sure as a bad one, so a feeling of confidence is not evidence that your number beats the group's.

Practical judgment follows from that. The group's average is a strong benchmark to compare yourself against. If your own estimate for the same question is far from what many independent people say, you should ask what you know that they do not, and you should not assume the answer is "a lot".

[[lessons]]
$$**Treat the average of many independent guesses as the number to beat, and ask what you know that justifies departing from it.** Whether you are estimating a sales forecast, a project date or the number of seats you will need, your own gut figure carries personal error that you cannot feel from the inside. If you can quickly collect a few separate estimates from colleagues, compare yours to their average before you commit. When you still choose to differ, be able to name the specific information that the others lack. Confidence alone is not a reason, a small habit that protects you from your own certainty.$$

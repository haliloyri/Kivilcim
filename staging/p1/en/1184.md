Here's a piece of math that surprises most people. If your investments fall by 50 percent, how much do they have to rise to get you back to where you started? Not 50 percent. One hundred percent. The investors Tony Robbins interviewed seemed to build their whole approach around that fact.

[[story]]
When Tony Robbins set out to write *Money: Master the Game*, he expected the world's best investors to talk mostly about how to win big. Instead, again and again, they talked about how not to lose.

Warren Buffett's famous rules sum up the attitude. Rule number one: never lose money. Rule number two: never forget rule number one. Buffett obviously doesn't mean he's never had a losing investment. He means that avoiding large, permanent losses is the foundation of everything else.

Robbins explains why with simple arithmetic. Suppose you have $100,000 and lose half. You're down to $50,000. To get back to $100,000, that $50,000 now has to double. You need a 100 percent gain just to break even. And during all the years it takes to recover, you're not growing at all. Large losses don't just hurt. They steal time, which is the most important ingredient in compounding.

##The best investors, Robbins found, obsess over the downside before they think about the upside.##

One of the clearest examples came from Paul Tudor Jones, a highly successful hedge fund manager. He told Robbins about a principle he calls **asymmetric risk/reward**. Before making an investment, he looks at how much he could lose and how much he could gain. He looks for opportunities where the potential reward is at least five times the risk, a ratio of five to one.

The beauty of this, Jones explained, is that you don't need to be right very often. If you risk one dollar to make five, you can be wrong on four out of five trades and still come out ahead.

Robbins also describes Ray Dalio's **All Weather** approach, built on a similar concern. Instead of betting on one economic scenario, Dalio designed a mix of investments meant to hold up in different conditions, whether growth or recession, inflation or deflation. The aim is to reduce the chance of a devastating loss, so that the portfolio can keep compounding through bad years as well as good.

For ordinary people, Robbins's takeaway is practical. Don't chase the biggest possible win if it exposes you to a loss you can't recover from. Look for decisions where the upside clearly outweighs the downside. And protect yourself from the big hit first.

[[lessons]]
$$**Big losses cost more than they look.** A 50 percent drop needs a 100 percent gain to recover, and the years spent recovering are years without growth. That lopsided math is why Buffett's "never lose money" rule is so central. Avoiding the big hit is worth more than chasing the big win.$$

$$**Look for asymmetric bets.** Paul Tudor Jones seeks opportunities where the possible reward is at least five times the risk. That lets him be wrong most of the time and still succeed. Asking "how much could I lose, and how much could I gain?" before any big decision is a habit worth borrowing.$$

$$**Protect the downside first.** Dalio's All Weather approach spreads risk so no single scenario can wipe you out. The same logic applies beyond investing: before chasing an opportunity, make sure a bad outcome is something you can survive and recover from.$$

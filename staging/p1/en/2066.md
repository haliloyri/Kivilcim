[[open]]
A new hire leaves her first week at the company without having filled in a single retirement form. Her first paycheck still carries a deduction that goes into a retirement account. The company's rules made that choice for her before she arrived, and she can still undo it if she chooses.

[[story]]
Brigitte Madrian and Dennis Shea studied a large US company in the health care and insurance business, one that appeared on the Fortune 500 list. It had run a 401(k) plan since 1985, an American retirement plan that takes a share of an employee's pay. To join, a worker had to fill in an enrollment form or phone the plan's record keeper, pick a contribution rate and choose how the money would be invested.

On 1 April 1998 the rules changed. Employees hired from then on were eligible at once and were enrolled automatically unless they opted out: 3 percent of their pay went into the plan's money market fund. They could change the rate or the fund at any time. The employer's matching contribution and the other terms of the plan stayed as they were.

Madrian and Shea compared the groups. Among employees hired shortly before the change, who had to sign up on their own, 37.4 percent took part on 30 June 1998. Among those hired under automatic enrollment, 85.9 percent took part on 30 June 1999. Because the new rule also let new hires join from the first day, the paper treats that second change separately.

The second finding concerns what people chose. A large share of those hired under automatic enrollment stayed with both the 3 percent rate and the money market fund, a combination very few earlier hires had picked for themselves. The authors put this down to inertia and to many employees treating the default as the company's investment advice.

For the company as a whole, participation had stood at 61 percent before the change. Assuming the new hires' 86 percent would hold, the authors estimated a rise of about 25 percentage points. Their own estimates for a bigger employer match or for financial education came to less than half of that.

There was a catch. In the paper's tables, the average contribution rates of the automatically enrolled group are lower than those of the earlier group. The default raised participation, and it also left many people at 3 percent. The number chosen by whoever wrote the rule became the number in many workers' accounts.

[[lessons]]
$$**Pick the pre-set option as if you were giving advice.** A manager whose calendar app makes every new invitation an hour long can expect most meetings to fill that hour, even when twenty minutes would have done and nobody needed the extra time. When people do nothing, they usually accept whatever outcome that produces, and many read it as a recommendation. Before you send out a form, a rule or a setting, ask what happens when nobody changes it, and you can make that outcome the one you would actually choose for the people who will never open the options.$$

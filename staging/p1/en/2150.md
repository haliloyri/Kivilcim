A young man sits through the last lectures of a famous chemist in London, writing steadily. Nobody employs him to do this, since by day he works in the book trade. When the lectures end he takes his scribbles home to copy them out again, more neatly, into a single volume.

[[story]]
Michael In 1812 Faraday looked like anything but a future scientist. He worked in the bookselling trade, and he wanted out of it. Science was what he wanted, but he had no post in it and no obvious way to get one.

What he did have was a habit of taking notes. According to the Royal Institution's own account, he had already filled four bound notebooks from a series of lectures by a man named Tatum, and he showed them to his employer, Riebau, and to a customer in the shop. That led to a ticket for the last four lectures given by Humphry Davy at the Royal Institution. Faraday later wrote that a gentleman who was a member of the Institution took him to hear some of Davy's last lectures in Albemarle Street.

He took notes there too. Then he did something nobody had asked him to do: in his own words, he wrote them out "more fairly in a quarto volume." The lectures were over, and he turned his scribbles into a book.

Next he wrote to Davy. He explained that he wanted to leave trade for science and hoped that, if an opportunity came his way, Davy would favour his wishes, and he enclosed the notes. Davy replied on 24 December 1812. According to the summary in the published edition of Faraday's letters, Davy was pleased with the evidence of his zeal, memory and attention. He was leaving town until the end of January, but he would meet Faraday whenever Faraday liked, and he offered to be of service.

Faraday remembered how it ended. Davy sent for him, told him a laboratory assistant's place was open at the Royal Institution, and, Faraday wrote, "through his good efforts" he went there in early March 1813.

The notes mattered because of what they proved. Faraday had no credentials to show. What he could show was how closely he had followed Davy's own lectures, written down, ordered and bound. Davy could check for himself by opening the volume and seeing how closely this young man had listened, and the question changed from "who is this?" to "what could he do for us?" Anyone can write "I am serious about this field" in a letter. Far fewer people hand over a finished volume of proof along with it.
 
[[lessons]]
$$**Hand the person you want to learn from something they can read.** Saying "I would love to get into this field" costs nothing, and everybody says it, so it tells a manager or a master very little. A tidy set of notes from a course you sat through, a short report that solved a customer's problem, or a file in which you worked out the basics of a craft shows how you actually pay attention. The other person can judge you before the first conversation starts, and that conversation then begins with "what can you do?" instead of "who are you?"$$

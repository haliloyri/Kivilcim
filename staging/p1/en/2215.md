[[open]]
Under a fluorescence microscope, a thin slice of brain tissue glows in two colors. One dye lights up only cells that were copying their DNA, which means they were dividing. The other lights up only neurons. Whether any cell glows in both colors is the whole question.

[[story]]
For most of the twentieth century, the usual teaching was that an adult brain could lose neurons but never make new ones. Work in adult rodents and monkeys had already found new neurons in a few regions, yet the 1998 paper that addressed it for people opens with a plain admission: the genesis of new cells, including neurons, in the adult human brain had not yet been demonstrated.

Peter Eriksson, Fred Gage and their colleagues found an unusual way to check. A drug called bromodeoxyuridine, or BrdU, is a thymidine analog that gets built into a cell's DNA whenever it prepares to divide. Some patients had been treated with it, and after they died the team obtained their brain tissue. A cell that carries the BrdU label must have been born, by division, after the drug was given. The logic was that simple, and the catch was just as plain: nobody can take brain tissue from a living person to look, so the label could only be read after death, under a microscope.

The label alone could not say what kind of cell had been born, so the researchers added a second stain. They used immunofluorescent markers for neurons, NeuN, calbindin and neuron-specific enolase, and looked for cells carrying BrdU and a neuronal marker together. Per the paper's abstract, they found them in the dentate gyrus, a part of the hippocampus, the structure tied to learning and memory. New neurons, it reported, were being generated from dividing progenitor cells in adult humans. The authors concluded that the human hippocampus keeps its ability to generate neurons throughout life.

The study did not show how many neurons appear, or what they do for thinking, or what could make more of them. It established that the old rule had an exception.

Ratey's Spark draws on this line of research, including work from Gage's group, when it argues that movement may help the brain grow and protect itself. Later researchers have kept arguing about how much neurogenesis continues in adults and how to measure it, so the 1998 result is best read as the first firm proof of possibility, not a final count. A question that had been closed for decades reopened because someone found a way to mark a single cell's birthday.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask what measurement would settle the argument, then build it.** The old belief survived for decades partly because nobody had a label that showed a cell being born. Think of a family argument over whether the heating bill really went up after a new habit: one person insists it did, the other insists it did not, and they stay stuck until somebody reads the meter before and after. When you find the measuring step that both sides would accept, the disagreement becomes a task instead of a mood. You also learn to say which parts of a claim are shown and which are still guesses.$$

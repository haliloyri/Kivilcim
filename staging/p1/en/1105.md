[[open]]
Picture a room in Paris, early 1809. The Emperor of the French is shouting at his former foreign minister in front of a crowd of dignitaries, calling him a thief, a coward and a traitor. Anyone watching would assume they were seeing a career end in real time. The man being shouted at doesn't flinch. He doesn't argue or plead. He just stands there. And that silence turns out to matter more than anything Napoleon says.

[[story]]
The man was Charles-Maurice de Talleyrand, and his life is almost too long to believe. He was born in 1754 under the old French monarchy and died in 1838. In between, France went through the Revolution, the Directory, Napoleon's rule, the Bourbon Restoration and the July Monarchy. Governments rose and fell. Many of the people who served them were executed or driven into exile.

Talleyrand kept turning up near the top. Under one regime after another he held high office, and above all he ran foreign affairs. It was a strange kind of career. The flags changed and so did the people in charge, but he stayed where he was.

In January 1809, commonly dated the 28th, that career should have ended. Napoleon had come back from Spain and learned that Talleyrand had been talking with Fouché, the police minister, about who might succeed him. From an emperor's point of view, two of his most powerful servants were quietly planning for a France without him.

So Napoleon exploded, and he did it in front of other dignitaries. He called Talleyrand a thief, a coward and a traitor. A crude line is widely attributed to him in this scene, something to the effect that Talleyrand was dirt, or worse, in a silk stocking, though accounts differ on the exact wording. Then he stormed out.

##Talleyrand stayed composed, turned to the people still in the room, and reportedly remarked that it was a pity such a great man had been so badly brought up.##

That one sentence changed what everyone had just seen. A moment earlier the story was the Emperor exposing a traitor. Now it was about a ruler who had lost control of himself in public, while the man he attacked had kept his dignity.

He did pay a price. Talleyrand lost his court post as Grand Chamberlain. But he wasn't arrested and he wasn't destroyed, and he didn't fade away.

When Napoleon fell, Talleyrand played a leading role in the new regime and represented France at the Congress of Vienna, where the map of Europe was being redrawn. Years later, under King Louis-Philippe, he served as ambassador in London until 1834, when he was around eighty. He outlasted the man who had publicly condemned him, and then he served the governments that came after.

Robert Greene uses Talleyrand in several places in *The 48 Laws of Power*, and he tells the 1809 scene as an example of keeping your composure. But Greene is clear that Talleyrand is a master of power, not a moral model. In his own time he was widely condemned for bribery, betrayal and switching sides. Greene is describing how power works, not how it ought to.

[[lessons]]
$$**Calm moves the embarrassment to the attacker.** Napoleon had every advantage in that room: rank, anger, and a real grievance. Talleyrand had one quiet sentence about the Emperor's manners. Because he didn't defend himself or fight back, the witnesses were left looking at a shouting ruler and a composed target. When someone attacks you in public and you stay steady, the audience starts judging the one who lost control.$$

$$**Serve the function, not only the person.** People whose position depended on a particular ruler tended to fall when that ruler fell. Talleyrand's value came from something every French government needed, which was someone who could handle foreign affairs. That's why he could lose Napoleon's favor and still end up representing France at Vienna. Each new power needed that work done, and he could supply it.$$

$$**Effective is not the same as admirable.** The survival skill Greene describes came with real costs. Talleyrand was accused in his own time of bribery, betrayal and changing sides whenever it suited him, and in 1809 he really had been plotting around his master. The lesson isn't to copy him. It's to see clearly how durability works, and then to decide where your own ethical limits are.$$

[[reflect]]
- Think of a time you were criticized in front of others. Did the way you reacted help you, or did it help your critic?
- If your current boss left tomorrow, what part of your work would still make you necessary?
- Talleyrand adapted to every regime. At what point would adapting start to feel like opportunism to you?
- Which of your working relationships are you counting on to protect you?

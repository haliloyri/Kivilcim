A vice president at a company with the most admired balance sheet in energy writes a memo to her chairman. It is anonymous at first, then she signs it. Its most quoted sentence is a warning about her own employer. What happens next depends less on what she wrote than on the question somebody else asks about it.

[[story]]
On 14 August 2001, Jeffrey Skilling resigned as chief executive of Enron. Within days, Sherron Watkins, a vice president in corporate development, put her worries about the company's accounting into a memo to the chairman, Kenneth Lay. According to her lawyer, she had worked for chief financial officer Andrew Fastow. Reports at the time put the memo at seven pages, and it began as an anonymous note before she identified herself.

Two lines became famous. "I am incredibly nervous that we will implode in a wave of accounting scandals," she wrote. Elsewhere she asked, "Has Enron become a risky place to work?", and added a question for employees who had not grown rich in recent years: could they afford to stay? Bethany McLean and Peter Elkind chronicle Enron's rise and fall in *The Smartest Guys in the Room*, and the weeks before the bankruptcy are part of that arc.

Lay asked the law firm Vinson & Elkins to look into it and, according to news reports from January 2002, limited the assignment to a single question: did the letter contain new factual information? It was not a wider review of Enron's accounting. The firm reported that Enron had committed no wrongdoing.

On 2 December 2001, Enron filed for bankruptcy protection. The memo itself stayed out of public view until about five months after it was written, when the House Energy and Commerce Committee, chaired by Billy Tauzin, released excerpts in January 2002.

##The assignment had been drawn so that only new facts counted.## A memo that raised a warning about how existing numbers were put together could be read, by that test, as containing nothing new, and a lawyer who checks the letter for novelty has not been asked to check the accounts. The lawyers' report answered the question they were given. It could say little about the one the memo was really asking: how the company's numbers had been built, rather than whether anything in them was new.

The memo reads differently once you look at the order of events. First the warning went up the chain in writing. Then the person with the most power to act chose the frame in which it would be examined. Everything after that was decided by the frame, not by the quality of the writing.

[[lessons]]
$$**Settle who sets the question before you hand something over for review.** A landlord who tells a handyman "just confirm the roof is fine" will get a different report from one who says "tell me everything wrong with the roof," even though the same handyman climbs the same ladder. The handyman reports on what he was asked, so the check is only as wide as the brief, and whoever writes the brief has already shaped the answer. When you ask for a review, write the question yourself and say what would count as a problem. When you review, say what the brief leaves out.$$

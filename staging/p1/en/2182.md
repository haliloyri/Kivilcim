A manuscript sits in a censor's office in Rome with the wrong name on its first page. The author has called it a dialogue about the ebb and flow of the sea. The censor wants the sea removed from the title, and the argument that depended on it removed from the book.

[[story]]
Galileo Galilei began the book after a visit to Rome in 1624. At first he called it a dialogue on the tides, because he believed the tides were physical proof that the Earth moves. The finished work would be a conversation between three speakers about the old picture of the heavens, with the Earth at the centre, and the Copernican one, with the Earth in motion.

Writing took him a long time. According to a university museum account, he finished the text in April 1630, more than five years after he began. That is slow for a man who already had a European reputation and could have answered the world's expectations with something shorter.

Finishing was only the first half. The manuscript went to Rome for review, and the censor, Niccolò Riccardi, read it in 1630 without raising objections, yet the Roman edition never appeared. The authorities asked Galileo to take all mention of the tides out of the title and the opening, and the book became the *Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems*. The licence to print finally came in Florence, in the summer of 1631, about a year after the text was done.

It finally came off the press in 1632. Galileo dedicated it to Ferdinando II de' Medici, the Grand Duke of Tuscany, and the first printed copy reached the Duke on 22 February of that year.

The ending was not kind to the book. In 1633, Galileo was found vehemently suspect of heresy on the strength of it, and the *Dialogue* went onto the Index of Forbidden Books, where it stayed until 1835.

One can look at the logic of the case without taking sides in the verdict. Galileo spent more than five years writing, another year clearing the review, and the result was a work whose title and opening he was made to change before it could be printed. Writing was slow, and the gate that came after it was slower. A deadline-driven author would have stopped at the manuscript and called the job done in 1630. The real project, which ran from the first sketch to the printing licence, took about seven years.

[[lessons]]
$$**Count the review and approval stage as part of the project, and put it on the calendar from the first day.** Galileo's writing took over five years, and the permission to print took another year or more, so a plan that ended at the manuscript would have been wrong by a long way, and a deadline set at that point would have been missed. In your own work, a report is not finished when you stop typing; it is finished when the people who must sign it have signed. If you add a realistic stretch for that stage at the start, you promise less and deliver what you promised.$$

Sit in on enough exit interviews and the answers start to sound the same. Better offer. More money. A chance I couldn't pass up. It sounds tidy, and nobody argues with it. But catch the same person a few months later, over coffee, with nothing left to lose, and the answer often changes. It stops being a number. It becomes a name.

[[story]]
Here's how it usually goes. Someone hands in their notice. HR schedules the exit interview, asks the standard question, and gets the standard answer:

> "Honestly? It was the salary. They offered more."

It goes in the spreadsheet under compensation. If enough people say it, the organization concludes it has a pay problem.

A large body of research linked to Gallup pointed somewhere else. Marcus Buckingham and Curt Coffman popularized it in their 1999 book *First, Break All the Rules*, and it drew on roughly a million employee interviews. It found that when people leave voluntarily, their direct manager is a major factor, more than the job itself. That finding hardened into a slogan that business authors keep repeating: people leave managers, not companies. Simon Sinek repeats it too, in his 2014 book known in English as *Leaders Eat Last*.

So why is the salary answer so common? Think about what it costs to say the other thing. You might need a reference. You might run into that boss at a conference in five years. "Money" is safe because it blames no one. The real reason gets softened on the way out the door.

It makes sense that the manager matters this much once you list what they decide. They decide who gets seen by senior people. They decide who gets the interesting project and who gets the cleanup. They decide whether a mistake becomes a lesson or a mark against you, and who gets credit when something goes right. For most people the company is an abstraction. The manager is the part of it that shows up every morning.

That's usually where the story stops: bad manager, replace manager, problem solved. But the same data has a second reading. Some commentators argue it's more accurate to say people leave cultures, because the manager is simply the piece of the culture standing closest to the employee. That isn't a refutation. It's the same evidence seen from a different angle, and it points to a different fix. The first reading says change the person. The second says change the system.

Sinek's argument leans toward the second. Leaders shape the environment below them, and managers tend to treat their teams the way their own superiors treat them. A manager led by pressure learns to lead by pressure.

##So when you follow the name someone finally gives you, it often leads to another name, one level up.##

There's a practical side too. Many of the things employees blame on their manager were decided higher up: the budget, the pay band, the promotion quota, the impossible workload. Often the manager is only the one who has to deliver the news. The person across the desk looks like the cause because they're the one saying it out loud.

[[lessons]]
$$**The manager is the company, up close.** When the research says the direct manager outweighs the job itself, it's describing where the organization actually touches a person. Visibility, assignments, how mistakes are handled and who gets credit are all decided by one person. If that person gets those calls wrong, a strong brand and a good mission won't make up for it.$$

$$**"Money" is the safe answer.** The exit interview records compensation because naming your boss has a price. You might need references, and you might meet that person again. So the stated reason and the real one drift apart. If you only read the spreadsheet, you'll keep fixing pay while the actual cause stays where it is.$$

$$**Look one level up.** Sinek's point is that behavior flows downward. Managers reproduce how they're led, and many of the constraints they enforce were set above them. A low-engagement team often signals a problem at the level above it. Replacing the manager can lift the score for a while. Fixing how that manager is led keeps the pattern from coming back with the next hire.$$

[[reflect]]
- Think of someone you know who quit. Was it really the company, or the person they spoke to every day?
- If you manage people, how much of your style did you copy from your own boss, including the parts you didn't like?
- Next time you hear "they left for the money," ask what the person would have said if saying it cost them nothing.

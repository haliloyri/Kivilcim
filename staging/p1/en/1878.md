A Disney film is stuck on the page. It borrows from a Hans Christian Andersen fairy tale, its queen is written as the villain, and the team cannot make it move. The fix comes from changing how two characters are related to each other.

[[story]]
Disney's Frozen was inspired by Andersen's "The Snow Queen." In the early plan Elsa was a villain, but the team wanted to move away from that. According to co-director Jennifer Lee, they were heading toward comedy, music and a character-driven epic. Taking the villain out of Elsa was one thing. Knowing what to put in her place was another.

Director Chris Buck later described where things stood: "Until we came up with the idea of making them sisters, we were floundering a bit." The idea, he said, just clicked. Elsa and Anna were now sisters, and the story could be built on what two people owe each other.

Meanwhile Jennifer Lee, the writer of Wreck-It Ralph, had been giving notes on Frozen while she worked on that film. Producer Peter Del Vecho asked her to join Frozen as a writer, and she agreed. Then the schedule was moved up by a year, and she was asked to direct alongside Chris Buck.

Ed Catmull told Lee first that she should do whatever she had to do to the story. Then he added: "But you have to earn that ending." By Lee's account the ending had been pitched in 2009, and it is still the ending in the finished film. Frozen opened in the United States at Thanksgiving 2013.

What stands out is the scale of the fix that worked. Rather than patch the stalled story scene by scene, the team changed the relationship between the characters. Once they were sisters, the ending had something to pay off: what Catmull called earning it needs a bond that two people can actually earn it with. That is our reading and not a quote from the film's makers, but the order of events supports it.

It is also worth noticing what the team kept. The ending pitched in 2009 is still the ending, so whatever else changed was rebuilt around that fixed point.

There is also the clock. When the schedule moved up a year, a new director joined. So the change happened in how the work was organised as well as in the story, and the two were tied together.

[[lessons]]
$$**When a piece of work stalls, ask what it is really about before you patch any part of it.** If your Monday presentation will not come together, asking "which decision should this help someone make?" can put the remaining slides in order better than fixing them one by one. A bakery whose menu is not selling can ask why customers come in before adding new cakes. A question like that lets you gather scattered corrections around one relationship or one decision. Seeing that early saves you from polishing the parts you may end up dropping.$$

Brené Brown put a simple question to senior leaders: what, if anything, about the way people lead today needs to change? You might expect answers about strategy, technology or speed. The answer that came up most often was far more ordinary, and far more uncomfortable. It happens in almost every workplace, and probably in most families too.

[[story]]
The question appears in *Dare to Lead*, Brown's 2018 book on courageous leadership. She asked senior leaders what needs to change about leadership for organizations to succeed in a complex, fast-changing world.

The most frequently named problem had nothing to do with markets or tools. It was this: people avoid tough conversations. That includes one of the most basic tasks of leading anyone, giving honest, productive feedback.

The leaders also explained why. They pointed to a lack of courage. And they pointed to a norm of being "nice and polite" that, looked at closely, is mostly a way to avoid discomfort. The niceness sounds generous. What it protects is the person who doesn't want the awkward moment.

Brown then traces what happens to all those conversations that never take place. They don't vanish. She links the avoidance to passive-aggressive behavior, to people talking behind each other's backs, and to problems that quietly get worse because nobody named them while they were still small.

So the very thing leaders were trying to prevent, friction and hurt feelings, shows up anyway. It just shows up sideways, in gossip and confusion, instead of in one honest exchange.

Her answer to this is a short line that has become one of the book's best-known ideas:

##Clear is kind. Unclear is unkind.##

Being vague to spare someone's feelings feels considerate in the moment. But the person on the receiving end is left guessing. They don't know what's wrong, what good looks like, or how much trouble they're in. Brown's point is that this isn't kindness at all. The kind thing is to say what's true, clearly enough that the other person can act on it.

That reframes the choice. The question stops being "Should I be nice or honest?" and becomes "Am I protecting them, or protecting myself?"

[[lessons]]
$$**Politeness can be a hiding place.** Leaders in Brown's research described a "nice and polite" norm that was really about avoiding discomfort. Staying vague spares you the awkward moment. It doesn't spare the other person anything, because they still carry the problem, only now without the information. That's the heart of "clear is kind, unclear is unkind." The vague hint feels gentler to say, but it leaves the other person guessing about what's wrong and what good would look like.$$

$$**The unsaid finds another way out.** Brown connects avoided conversations to talking behind people's backs, passive-aggressive behavior and problems that keep growing. Skipping the hard conversation doesn't remove the conflict; it moves it somewhere harder to see and harder to fix. One clear exchange is often less painful than weeks of indirect friction. The avoided talk is also the one the gossip ends up replacing.$$

[[reflect]]
- Is there someone you're being "nice" to when being clear would help them more?
- When has someone's clear, honest feedback helped you, even though it stung at first?
- Where have you seen an avoided conversation turn into gossip or a bigger problem?

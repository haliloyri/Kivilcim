A bill signing in Washington. A bond salesman from New York stands in the room as the President picks up the pen. The subject is mortgages. Few people present realize that this signature opens a market, one that had been sitting behind a locked door for years.

[[story]]
In the late 1970s Lewis Ranieri joined Salomon Brothers' new mortgage trading desk. His job was to sell mortgage-backed securities, a product in which home loans are pooled and sold like bonds. The product existed, but finding buyers was hard. According to BusinessWeek, in 1977 only 15 states recognized these securities as legal investments. A pension fund or a bank could like the product and still be forbidden from buying it in many places. In other words, the quality of the product was the second problem and the law was the first.

That is why Ranieri's work was more than selling. BusinessWeek credits him with helping to create the market and with winning lobbying battles in Washington. The result was the Secondary Mortgage Market Enhancement Act of 1984. The law overturned the ban on selling mortgage securities without a government guarantee, overrode the states' own investor-protection rules, and allowed institutional investors to buy such securities when a rating agency rated them highly. Ranieri attended the signing by President Reagan.

Beside the rules there was the mathematics. Ranieri is quoted as saying "mortgages are math", and he is said to have hired PhDs. That kind of team helped develop the collateralized mortgage obligation, which splits a pool of mortgages into slices with different maturities. In 1983 Ranieri and Larry Fink of First Boston created the CMO together, and it drew in institutional investors who wanted to choose how much maturity risk they took.

The ending was not sweet. Salomon pushed Ranieri out in July 1987. Some called him a victim of his own success: his former traders went on to staff many of Wall Street's investment banks. The firm had trained people who knew the market better than anyone, and they were free to take that knowledge elsewhere. The man who built the market had left competitors with people and ideas that had caught up.

What stays with you is that this market was not born from a single sales move but from three jobs fitted together. First the legal barriers had to come down, then the product had to be shaped so a buyer could actually hold it, and only then could it be sold. Ranieri's story shows the worth of an idea, and also who opened the door in front of it. For a salesperson that is an unusual job description: explaining the product is not enough, the legal and technical ground under it has to be prepared too.

[[lessons]]
$$**If you are selling something new, count the removal of the rule or habit in its way as part of the job.** A product can be good and still go nowhere when buyers cannot take it for legal, technical or institutional reasons. At work this means that introducing a new tool to a team starts with fixing the approval process, the security rule and the training, not with the demo. Whoever clears the obstacle owns far more of the outcome than whoever had the idea. Plan the opening of the door as one of your own tasks, and write down early whose support you will need for it.$$

[[open]]
Two communities share distant ancestors. One lives in Arizona, the other high in the mountains of northwest Mexico. A researcher who walked from one to the other would have found the same family names and a similar genetic heritage, yet a very different number of people with diabetes.

[[story]]
The Pima people of Arizona have been followed by US government researchers for a long time. Since 1965 the National Institutes of Health has run studies in the community, and the rates of type 2 diabetes it recorded there were among the highest ever reported. For years the obvious explanation was inheritance: perhaps these people carried genes that made diabetes almost unavoidable.

A different group of Pima lives in the Sierra Madre of Mexico, far from the Arizona desert. In 2006 a team led by Leslie Schulz and Peter Bennett, with Eric Ravussin and others, published a comparison in the journal Diabetes Care. They examined three groups: Pima in Arizona, Pima in Mexico, and non-Pima Mexicans living in the same region as the Mexican Pima.

The result was blunt. Age- and sex-adjusted type 2 diabetes affected about 38% of the Arizona Pima, 6.9% of the Mexican Pima and 2.6% of the non-Pima Mexicans. The Mexican Pima's rate was less than a fifth of their Arizona cousins' rate, and close to that of their non-Pima neighbors. In other words, the gap between two branches of one people was far bigger than the gap between that people and the neighbors beside them.

Obesity told a similar story. Among Mexican Pima, 7% of men and 20% of women were obese, against 9% and 27% among the non-Pima Mexicans, and both figures were far lower than in Arizona. Physical activity was much higher in both Mexican groups than among the Pima of Arizona.

The authors also pointed out that the two Pima groups are genetically similar when compared with other Native American peoples. That detail matters, because it weakens the simple story in which ancestry alone explains the disease. Genes may still make some people more vulnerable. But a population with that heritage can have a rate close to its neighbors' in one setting and several times higher in another.

The study was a snapshot, not an experiment, and it cannot say which part of the Western environment mattered most. Diet, work, activity and many other things differ between the two places. The authors concluded that the lifestyle changes that come with Westernization play a major role in the global rise of type 2 diabetes, and they described the condition as largely preventable.

[[lessons]]
$$**When two similar groups end up very different, look at what surrounds them before you blame what is inside them.** Inheritance is the easy explanation, because it needs no further inquiry and nobody has to change anything. A fair comparison holds one thing steady, here ancestry, and lets the setting vary, which is how this study separated the two. Use the same move at work: if two teams with similar people get different results, compare their conditions, such as workload, tools and habits, before you compare their talent. The setting is also the part you can usually change, so you start where change is possible$$

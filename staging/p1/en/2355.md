[[open]]
Two women wait tables at the same restaurant in Key West, and when the last table is cleared they drive off in different directions. One heads for a small apartment thirty miles away. The other opens the door of her own car and settles in for the night.

[[story]]
Barbara Ehrenreich's *Nickel and Dimed* begins in Key West, where she answered help-wanted ads for three days before a restaurant called Hearthside took her on as a waitress. The pay was low, but the bigger puzzle was where to sleep. Published summaries of the chapter describe what she found, and it is a lesson in how housing is really priced.

She rented an efficiency apartment about thirty miles from Key West. A trailer, which sounds like the cheap option, was beyond her budget at the start. So she accepted the drive, with all its gas and hours, as the price of a door that locked.

Her coworker Gail had no such door. According to those summaries, Gail slept in her car, because she could not put together the deposit and the first month's rent that a landlord asks for before handing over a key. The rent itself was not the wall. The wall was the lump sum that has to arrive before the first night.

Ehrenreich later made the same point from a lectern. Coworkers who slept in vehicles, she told one university audience, did not call themselves homeless: "If you have a vehicle to sleep in, you don't call yourself homeless anymore." A car is a roof, a bed and an address of sorts, and it costs nothing at the start of the month.

Reviewers of the book draw the same conclusion about the wider pattern. Workers who lack a deposit and an advance month's rent are pushed toward motels that charge by the day or the week, and a motel night is dearer than an apartment night. A person with little money pays more per night of shelter than a person with a bit more, and she pays it every night.

Notice what Hearthside's manager was doing in the same weeks. According to the summaries, after meetings about a messy break room and about drug activity on the night shift, he introduced drug tests for new hires and random tests for staff. The tests answered the manager's worry about drugs. The arithmetic of a deposit, which decided where some of the waitresses slept, was a different worry, and no test measured it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Look for the lump sum that sits in front of the monthly cost.** A cheap monthly price often hides an expensive entrance fee, and the people who cannot pay it end up buying the same thing in small, dearer pieces. You can see the same shape in a training course that needs the whole fee up front, a tool you rent weekly because you cannot buy it, or a new hire who cannot afford the commute until the first paycheck arrives. When you design a service, a policy or a favour, ask what has to be paid before the first day begins, and whether you could split that step.$$

[[open]]
A man drops a compact disc into an ordinary envelope, addresses it to a friend's house in Santa Cruz, and waits. The whole experiment costs one stamp, and the question it asks is very small. If the disc arrives broken, a big idea dies cheaply. If it arrives whole, the idea may be worth a company.

[[story]]
Netflix began with a question about shipping. Marc Randolph and Reed Hastings founded the company on 29 August 1997 in Scotts Valley, California. Randolph admired Amazon and wanted a large category of portable items to sell online. They considered renting videotapes by mail and rejected the idea: VHS cassettes were too expensive to stock and too fragile to ship.

Then they heard about DVDs, and they tested the idea in the cheapest way available. They mailed a compact disc to Hastings's house in Santa Cruz. It arrived intact, so they decided to enter the home video rental and sales market, which the published account puts at sixteen billion dollars. Netflix began offering DVDs per rental on 14 April 1998 and introduced a monthly subscription in September 1999.

According to published accounts, in 2000 Blockbuster turned down a chance to buy the young company for fifty million dollars. At the time Blockbuster was run by John Antioco, who had left Taco Bell in June 1997 to become its chief executive. Blockbuster was the giant of video rental, with its business built on stores, and Netflix was a small mail operation. A rational manager could have looked at that gap and seen no reason to pay.

Ten years later the picture had reversed. Blockbuster filed for Chapter 11 on 23 September 2010, citing about nine hundred million dollars in debt, heavy losses and competition from Netflix, Redbox and video on demand. Antioco had been pushed out in July 2007.

The test asked a narrower question than any business plan could. It did not ask whether the world wanted DVD rental, only whether a disc could survive the trip. Both halves of the story are about testing, though one is about doing it and the other about skipping it. The founders did not argue about whether DVDs could survive the post. They put one in an envelope and found out. Their early idea was cheap to check and gave a clear answer in days. The incumbent, by contrast, judged a new idea against the business it already ran, where every number favoured the shops. Fifty million dollars was real money to defend and only a guess to risk. Waiting for proof feels prudent, but proof of a new model tends to arrive after the market has moved.

[[lessons]]
$$**Test the riskiest assumption of your idea with the cheapest experiment you can run this week.** The founders could have debated for months whether a disc survives the mail. Instead they posted one and had their answer in days, for the price of a stamp. Whatever your idea is, a new service, a changed process or a different way of working, name the one thing that must be true for it to work and find the smallest possible way to check it. A cheap result will move people more than a long argument and lets you change course before anything is costly.$$

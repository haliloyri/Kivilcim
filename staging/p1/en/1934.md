Two people lie in bed at night, one of them seriously ill, and the question between them is the kind most couples are spared. She asks what he is most afraid of or sad about. He answers with two words. What she asks next is about a child who does not exist yet.

[[story]]
In *When Breath Becomes Air*, Paul Kalanithi describes a night in bed with his wife, Lucy, a physician herself. He had metastatic lung cancer, diagnosed when he was 36, and his oncologist, Emma, had declined to estimate how much time he had, preferring to talk with him about his values. Lucy asked him, as they lay there, what he was most afraid or sad about. "Leaving you," he said.

The two of them had always wanted children, and the conversation turned to whether to have one now. Lucy put the hard questions on the table herself. Would a newborn distract from the time they had left together? And she asked, "Don't you think saying goodbye to your child will make your death more painful?"

Kalanithi's answer was a question of his own: "Wouldn't it be great if it did?"

The reply does not wave the pain away. It accepts that the pain is real and then asks what it would mean. One way to read it: if a child could bring so much meaning into his life that leaving became harder, that would be a sign of something worth having.

He also wrote that he wanted Lucy to make the final decision. She was the one who would probably raise the child alone, and she would be caring for him as his illness progressed. He did not treat the choice as his alone, because the consequences would fall on the two of them in different ways. That is a detail easy to skip: before he argued his side, he handed the decision to the person who would live with it longest.

They decided to have a child. He wrote that they would carry on living, instead of dying. Their daughter, Elizabeth Acadia, known as Cady, was born on 4 July 2014. Kalanithi died on 9 March 2015, when she was eight months old.

The exchange is worth studying because it lets both of them say the worst thing first. Lucy raised the objection that was hardest for him to hear, in plain words. The decision they reached was made with the pain already on the table, where neither of them had to guess at it.

[[lessons]]
$$**When both options carry pain, ask which pain comes with something you want.** A person deciding whether to take in a rescue dog, mentor a junior colleague or move closer to a sick parent often reasons that the loss later will hurt, so the safer choice is to hold back. That reasoning treats avoiding pain as the goal, when the pain is often the cost of something that matters to you. Naming both the cost and the thing it buys lets you decide with open eyes, and it lets the people involved say what worries them first, in plain words.$$

A visitor walks down into a basement where a small team is building a computer. Within minutes one of the engineers has steered him out of the room and toward the cafeteria. Nobody there is rude about it, but everyone present can feel that he was not meant to see what was going on.

[[story]]
Tracy Kidder's *The Soul of a New Machine* began with a suggestion from his editor at *The Atlantic*, Richard Todd, who thought Kidder should look into computers. Todd happened to know someone in that world. According to Wired's twenty-year retrospective on the book, Tom West, an engineer at Data General, had been his roommate in college.

Kidder and West went sailing together. On that trip Kidder heard about Eagle, a new computer being built at the company's site in Westborough, Massachusetts. It was a rebel project, running alongside the company's official one, and its leaders had no obvious reason to welcome a writer.

Then Kidder went to see it for himself. The Eagle team worked in a basement, in a part of the building known as 14A/B. Carl Alsing, who led the microcode side of the team, sent the visitor off to the cafeteria almost at once. Kidder remembered the atmosphere in one sentence: "There was this feeling of paranoia."

That was the start of the access, not the end of it. Reviewers of the book note that Kidder went on to spend countless hours with the members of the team. The cafeteria had not closed the door; it had only moved the first conversation to a place where nobody's work was on the table.

The scene told both sides something. The team knew the visitor was a magazine writer and a friend of West's, and Kidder saw for himself that a rebel project was running in a basement beside the company's official one, staffed by people who were wary of him. A first meeting that looked like a refusal had in fact given each side a measure of how close they could get, and how fast.

The book came out in July 1981 from Little, Brown. The following year it won the Pulitzer Prize for General Nonfiction and the National Book Award for nonfiction.

Look at the order of events. No one at Data General was approached with a proposal or a press office request. A magazine editor mentioned a field, a college friendship supplied a name, a boat supplied the time to talk, and only then did a basement supply the story. Each step was small and personal, and each one was taken before anybody had to decide whether to say yes to a book. By the time Kidder stood in that basement, he was a guest of someone the team already knew, even if the team itself did not trust him yet.

[[lessons]]
$$**Spend time with people in an ordinary setting before you ask to see their work.** A new manager who wants to understand how a closed-off team really operates will learn more from a shared coffee or a ride to a client than from a formal request to sit in on their meetings. Trust that exists before the request gives the other person a reason to say yes, and it makes an early "not now" easier to take without giving up. Treat the first refusal as a place to start the conversation from, and ask again once you have been around a while.$$

Dishes washed, carpets vacuumed, lawn mowed. By any count of chores, the house is in order. In a counselor's office, the woman who lives there says she does not feel loved, and nothing she says next is about the chores.

[[story]]
A couple married for 30 years once came to see Gary Chapman, as he tells it. The husband showed love through work. He washed the dishes, he vacuumed, he mowed the lawn. His wife, in spite of all of it, felt unloved.

She said, in the words Chapman reports: "He is a hardworking man, but we don't ever talk. We haven't talked in 20 years." There is no insult in that sentence and no accusation, only a long-unpaid bill. In a house where her husband did everything he could, she had carried the sense that something was missing for two decades.

Chapman summed it up later: here was a man who genuinely loved his wife, and here was a wife who did not get it. Christianity Today adds that the couple described feeling like roommates, and that Chapman was about to go back through his years of counseling notes.

The roommate comparison is worth a second look. A roommate also washes dishes, takes out the trash and mows the lawn. Doing those things does not, by itself, tell a person "I see you." What reached the wife was the silence between the chores.

In the vocabulary Chapman would later use, the husband spoke fluent acts of service. His wife, going by her own words, was waiting for conversation. Two people were each saying something real, and each was speaking past the other.

Both of them could have won an argument that evening. The husband could say "I do everything," and he would be right. The wife could say "he doesn't love me," and from where she stood, it felt true. Neither was lying. The gap between them came from one word meaning two different things in two different dictionaries.

Twenty years also tells you how the problem grew. It did not start with a fight. It built slowly, with nobody noticing, and each quiet day confirmed it again.

What Chapman took from the meeting had little to do with blame. He was interested in the route love travelled, and in how a message could be sent faithfully every day and still never arrive. The husband showed his love every single day, but not along the route his wife could receive it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask how your effort lands on the other side, then let their answer overrule your guess.** A manager can collect and tidy the reports every Friday and still hear that the team feels unnoticed, because what the team wanted was a conversation or a personal thank-you. A gap between effort and response usually means the effort took the wrong route, and it rarely means that care is missing. Next time, put "what did you receive from me?" next to "what did I do?", and you may find the cheapest fix is a single sentence.$$

A rating comes with a number attached, and the number is tiny: 0.12 percent. It says the thing carrying the rating will almost never fail, and investors behave accordingly. Then the number meets the housing market, and the market comes back with something very different: 28 percent.

[[story]]
Nate Silver's *The Signal and the Noise* includes the 2008 financial crisis and the way the rating agencies got it wrong. This is the story of one complicated product and the grade it was given.

The product was the collateralized debt obligation, or CDO: a bundle of many home loans, sliced into layers. Standard & Poor's (S&P) gave many of these layers the highest grade, AAA. The logic looked sensible on paper, since the top layer takes losses only after the lower layers have absorbed theirs. According to Silver, the grade told investors that a CDO had only about a 0.12 percent chance of defaulting.

What happened, by the same account and drawing on S&P's own data, about 28 percent of those CDOs defaulted. That is more than two hundred times the rate the rating implied. The agencies were also paid by the institutions that issued the products they rated, a conflict of interest that was heavily debated after the crisis.

The question Silver presses is where the number came from. On paper, it was easy to assume that home loans would go bad independently, because looked at one by one, most borrowers kept paying. Later reviews often said the chance of many loans failing together had been underestimated. In the account Silver gives, the 0.12 percent did not come from historical data. These products were new, and there was no past to examine. The figure came from a statistical model built on assumptions, and the model turned out to be wrong when it counted.

Here lies the detail that matters, and it is easy to miss. A thin number like 0.12 percent gives the impression of measured knowledge, as if thousands of observations stood behind it. What stood behind it was the internal consistency of a model that had never been tested.

The gap between 0.12 and 28 is too wide to be a rounding error. An error of more than two hundred times is not one a better rounding rule would fix. A gap like that tells you the method was not tried out for this job. When a forecast cannot measure its own uncertainty and still sounds precise, the precision is a warning sign.

[[lessons]]
$$**When you meet a very low risk figure, your first move should be to ask how many real cases it came from.** A tiny risk quoted for a product with no history, a new market or a method nobody has tried is often the echo of an assumption. The thinner the number, the more convincing it sounds, but sounding convincing is not the same as being measured. If the answer is "none", treat the figure as a guess rather than knowledge, and do not take an irreversible step because of it. A quick question in a meeting costs you a minute, while a wrong assumption can cost you far more.$$

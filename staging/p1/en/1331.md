Someone leaves a baby on your doorstep. You didn't ask for it. You didn't cause it. Nobody could reasonably call it your fault. And yet, the moment you open the door, whatever happens next is a choice you are making.

[[story]]
Mark Manson uses that doorstep in The Subtle Art of Not Giving a F*ck to separate two ideas people constantly blur together. Fault looks back and asks who caused something. Responsibility is about now: what you choose to do next. In his phrase, "fault is past tense, responsibility is present tense."

The baby isn't your fault. But you can't opt out of responding. Closing the door is a choice. Calling for help is a choice. Doing nothing is a choice too. The situation arrived without your permission, and your response still belongs to you.

Manson then tells the story of William James. As a young man, James had tried several paths and failed at them, including painting and medicine. He was frequently ill. He fell into deep despair.

There was no single bad decision to point to, no one clear fault behind it all. In a sense, his situation was a baby left on his own doorstep.

Then James read the philosopher Charles Renouvier on free will. ##Afterward, James decided to run an experiment: for a period, he would act as if he were fully responsible for everything in his life.## Manson's point is that this didn't require James to have caused his problems. It only required him to treat the next steps as his.

James went on to become one of the founders of American psychology. Manson presents that decision as the turning point. That's his reading of the story, and a later success doesn't prove that one decision caused it. But the shape of the choice is clear enough to borrow.

What makes it usable is the framing. James treated responsibility as an experiment for a period, not a vow for the rest of his life. He didn't have to accept blame for his past to try it. He only had to decide that what came next was his to choose.

[[lessons]]
$$**Fault and responsibility answer different questions.** The baby on the doorstep isn't your fault, yet the next move is yours. When people treat these as one question, they either take blame for things they didn't cause or refuse to act on things they didn't cause. Both keep them stuck.$$

$$**Responsibility can start as an experiment.** James didn't take a lifelong vow. After reading Renouvier, he decided to act as if he were fully responsible for a period. A trial with an end is much easier to begin than a permanent change of character.$$

$$**The situation isn't chosen, the response is.** "Fault is past tense, responsibility is present tense" puts your attention where you have some control. You can't rewrite how something landed on your doorstep. You can decide what you do this afternoon, and that decision is the part that is fully yours.$$

[[reflect]]
- Is there a problem you've avoided solving because it wasn't your fault?
- When have you felt more in control simply by deciding the next step was yours?
- What would a short, James-style experiment in responsibility look like for you?

&&Think of one situation you've been blaming on someone else: what is one next step inside your control, and would taking it feel heavier or lighter?&&

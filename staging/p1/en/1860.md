[[open]]
Four guns near a French manor house are shelling the men coming ashore at Utah Beach, and about sixty German soldiers stand around them. The team sent to silence them is smaller than half that number. At its head is a young lieutenant named Dick Winters, and something useful is waiting among the guns.

[[story]]
Stephen E. Ambrose's 1992 book *Band of Brothers* follows Easy Company of the 506th Parachute Infantry Regiment, and this scene falls on the first morning of the Normandy invasion, 6 June 1944. The paratroopers had jumped into France during the night. Near Brécourt Manor, a German battery was firing on the men landing at Utah Beach, and the shelling had to be stopped.

The battery had four guns and about sixty German soldiers to man and defend them. Against that, Lieutenant Dick Winters of Easy Company led a small team. Accounts count twenty-three paratroopers: twelve from Easy, nine from Dog Company, one from Able and one from Fox. Other tellings give a lower figure, but under any of them the attackers were fewer than half the defenders. The odds were plain.

By the end of the assault all four guns were destroyed. The Americans lost four men killed and two wounded. Of the roughly sixty Germans, twenty were killed and twelve taken prisoner, so more than half the garrison was out of the fight by the time the guns went quiet.

Among the captured positions the men found a German map marking other enemy positions nearby. It is a small detail with a large implication: finishing one job did more than close that job, it produced the information for the next one. Winters received the Distinguished Service Cross for the action, and he was recommended for the Medal of Honor. Guns first, then the map.

The numbers do not explain why it worked, but they show the scale of the result. A small team took guns away from a force at least twice its size, held its own losses to six men, and left with knowledge it had not carried in. That the team was drawn from four different companies also says something about how the job was staffed: by the people the task needed, not by whatever unit happened to own them.

Set the figures side by side and the picture sharpens. Against six American casualties, thirty-two Germans were out of the fight, which is more than five for every one. Then there is the map itself: a map in a defender's hands shows how the defense was laid out, so a paper left behind by one fight can point to where the next one could go. What makes the scene worth remembering is that the guns stopped, and that the position handed over the means to keep going.

[[lessons]]
$$**Finish one piece and let it show you the next.** Looking at a large job as a single heap keeps you standing still, while somewhere inside the heap sits a small piece you can complete this week. Someone reorganizing a café kitchen who first clears one shelf will see there which supplies are missing and where things really get used, and will read the next step off that shelf. Working this way lets you measure progress before the whole job is done. Instead of guessing the second move, you can look at what the first move left in your hands.$$

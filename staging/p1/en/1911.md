On a Mississippi farm, a girl who is not yet three stands in front of a grown-up and reads. A few years later she will recite Bible verses in church, loud enough that people there give her a nickname. She has no idea that one day she will make her living by asking questions.

[[story]]
The woman co-writing *What Happened to You?* with psychiatrist Bruce Perry has her own answer to the question in the title. Oprah Winfrey was born on 29 January 1954 in Kosciusko, Mississippi, to Vernita Lee, a housemaid, and Vernon Winfrey, a coal miner who later became a barber and a city councilman. Her parents never married. Her birth certificate spelled her name "Orpah," but people mispronounced it, and "Oprah" stuck.

For her first six years she lived with her maternal grandmother, Hattie Mae Lee, in rural poverty. Her grandmother taught her to read before she was three. She took the girl to church, where the child recited Bible verses so often that she became known there as "The Preacher." So the earliest scenes of a famous speaker's life were a farm, a book and a congregation.

At six, she moved to an inner-city neighborhood in Milwaukee to live with her mother. By 1962 her mother was struggling to raise both of her daughters, and Winfrey was sent for a time to live with her father in Nashville. Later she returned to her mother in Milwaukee. In a single decade, then, a child went from a farm to a city, from one household to another, and back again.

A child in those years does not choose where she lives. The decisions were made around her: a mother who was struggling, a father in another city, a grandmother on a farm. Reading such a map as a list of addresses misses the point, because each address meant different adults, different rules and a different daily routine. The child had to adjust to them every time.

Those are only the outlines. A fuller account of her childhood includes harder parts, and they belong to her to tell. What the outline already shows is that a life does not have a single storyline. Her grandmother's patient teaching and the later moves from household to household were both part of what happened to her, and neither cancels the other. Learning to read before three does not remove the difficulties of later years, and those difficulties do not erase what was learned.

That is the habit the title asks for. "What happened to you?" can be a question about a wound, but it can just as well be a question about who read to you, who taught you to stand up and speak, and who was there when the address changed. Any honest answer carries several threads at once.

[[lessons]]
$$**Count the people who helped you in the story of what happened to you, along with the people who hurt you.** A childhood, like Winfrey's, can hold a grandmother who taught reading and a series of moves in the same few years, and both shaped the child. When you tell your own history, or listen to someone else's, ask about the early teachers, the steady rooms and the voice that encouraged, as well as the hard events. This gives you a fuller picture, and a fuller picture is easier to build on than a single painful chapter, and it gives the person telling it permission to tell the whole of their life.$$

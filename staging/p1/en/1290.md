When David Goggins was a boy, his mother kept a cookie jar at home. Years later, in the middle of a 24-hour race, with his body breaking down and every part of him wanting to stop, he reached into a very different jar. This one held no cookies. It held proof.

[[story]]
In *Can't Hurt Me*, Goggins gives the idea a name borrowed from his childhood home: the Cookie Jar. His mother kept a real one when he was small. The jar he writes about as an adult is a mental one, and what goes into it is very specific.

It holds the hard things he had already survived, and the wins, big and small, that he had earned. Not just the triumphs. The hardships go in too. A childhood with a violent father is in there. So is teaching himself to learn after years of faking his way through school by copying other people's work. These are not happy memories. They are evidence.

He describes reaching into the jar during a brutal 24-hour race. His body was breaking down, and he wanted to stop. In a moment like that, the mind tends to do something unhelpful: it replays every failure and quietly forgets every survival. So he went looking, deliberately, for what he had already come through.

##The point, as Goggins frames it, is evidence, not cheerleading.## He was not telling himself he was great. He was reminding himself of specific things he had actually done. When you can point to a real hard thing you got through, the one in front of you looks a little less impossible. It becomes one more item on a list, rather than the first test you have ever faced.

That difference matters. A pep talk asks you to believe something about yourself. The Cookie Jar asks you to remember something about yourself. The first can collapse the moment reality pushes back. The second is harder to argue with, because it already happened.

There is also a quiet detail in what counts. You might expect a confidence tool to be built only from victories and good days. Goggins puts the damage in too. Getting through a violent home was not a win in any ordinary sense, but it was a hard thing survived, and that makes it usable.

The book does not leave the jar as a metaphor. It turns it into a challenge: write down your past victories and the obstacles you have overcome, so they are ready to draw on when you need them.

That last part is the practical key. The middle of a crisis is the worst time to try to remember what you have been through. The jar works because it is filled before the hard moment arrives, not during it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Confidence can come from a record, not a feeling.** Mid-race, with his body failing, Goggins did not try to hype himself up. He recalled specific struggles he had already survived. Evidence you already own is sturdier than a mood you try to create on the spot, because nobody can talk you out of something you actually did.$$

$$**Hard times count as deposits.** His jar includes a childhood with a violent father and the years of copying schoolwork before he taught himself to learn. Those were not victories, but they were things he got through. Leave your hardest stretches out of your record and you leave out some of your best proof.$$

$$**Fill it before you need it.** The book's challenge is to write the list down in advance. Under pressure, memory tends to serve up failures first. A written jar is there even when your mind won't cooperate.$$

- Which item in your own jar would surprise people who only know your wins?

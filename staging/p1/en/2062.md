The writ hangs in a toilet, and it hangs over a book. A writer has a chapter he cannot publish, because the man it describes has sued him for libel. For fifteen months he is not free to say a word about a subject as grave as the AIDS epidemic in South Africa.

[[story]]
The first edition of Ben Goldacre's *Bad Science* has a gap in it. The missing chapter, "The Doctor Will Sue You Now", was left out because for fifteen months, until September 2008, the vitamin-pill entrepreneur Matthias Rath was suing both Goldacre and the Guardian for libel. The case concerned three of his Bad Science columns, published on 20 January, 27 January and 17 February 2007, and the chapter appeared in a later paperback edition.

Goldacre writes that AIDS kills about 300,000 people a year in South Africa, one every two minutes. Rath arrived there, he says, with money made selling vitamins in Europe. In Britain his adverts had claimed that 90 per cent of chemotherapy patients die within months, and a Berlin court had ordered him to stop saying his vitamins could cure cancer or face a €250,000 fine. In South Africa he began buying full-page newspaper adverts. They called anti-retroviral drugs poison and a conspiracy by a drugs cartel, and offered vitamin pills as the answer.

According to Goldacre, Rath ran a trial in 2005 in Khayelitsha, near Cape Town, giving his own formulation, VitaCell, to people with advanced AIDS. The Cape High Court declared the trial illegal in 2008. Rath says none of the participants were taking anti-retroviral drugs; Goldacre reports that some relatives have said that they were, and that they were told to stop.

In the English court, a judgment was handed down on 5 March 2008. Rath had asked Mr Justice Tugendhat to strike out significant parts of the Guardian's defence. The court dismissed most of his applications and found for him on a single narrow point, and the defences of justification and fair comment stood.

In September 2008 Rath dropped the case. The defence had cost the Guardian more than £500,000, and Goldacre says Rath had already paid £220,000 of it. Goldacre then published the chapter and made it free to read.

What the story shows is the distance between saying something and showing it. Goldacre kept boxes of references, notes and witness statements, a pile he says was as tall as Rath himself. In a courtroom, how harshly a sentence is worded matters less than what sits behind it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you write a hard claim, put the paper behind each sentence beside it.** A restaurant reviewer who calls a kitchen dirty should already have the visit date, the photographs and any inspection report in a folder. A file like that leaves you holding more than your own sentence when someone objects, whether it is a lawyer, a boss or a friend, and it lets you answer calmly instead of defensively. The heavier the claim, the thicker the pile behind it should be, and when you have nothing to show, softening the line or leaving it out is usually the more honest choice.$$

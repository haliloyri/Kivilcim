At five past four on a Tuesday morning, a man lies awake in bed beside his wife. The game ended two days earlier, but the last play is still running in his head. Then he starts to cry, and the papers are already full of his name.

[[story]]
The man was Pete Carroll, and the play was the pass he called at the end of Super Bowl XLIX. He told the *Today* show that the tears came at 4:05 a.m. on that Tuesday, in bed with his wife, Glena. By then the verdict was already in. Reporters called it the worst call in Super Bowl history, and NPR's roundup of the reaction used exactly that phrase in its headline. An ESPN columnist went further and called it the dumbest and most damaging call the game had ever seen.

Carroll answered with one sentence that Annie Duke cites in *Thinking in Bets*. On *Today*, he told Matt Lauer it was "the worst result of a call ever." He did not say the call was good. He said the result was bad, and he said that if the pass had been caught, nobody would have thought twice about it.

Duke gives this habit a name from the poker table: resulting. It means judging a decision by how it turned out. A good outcome is read as proof of a good choice, and a bad outcome as proof of a bad one, with luck quietly removed from the picture.

Run the same play again with a different ending. Wilson's pass reaches Lockette, Seattle scores, and the win goes to the team that trailed. The call is identical in every detail: the same formation, the same plan, the same odds. The likely headline flips from "worst call ever" to praise for a bold coach, written with the same confidence.

That is what makes resulting hard to catch from the inside. It feels like analysis, because the evidence is vivid and the conclusion arrives instantly. Duke's own example is drunk driving: a person who drives home after drinking and arrives safely has not shown that it was a good idea. The safe arrival tells you about luck on that one night.

The practical move she draws from this is to separate the two ledgers. One records what you decided and why. The other records what happened. Only the first belongs to you, and only a comparison of the two over many rounds tells you whether your thinking is improving or you have just been fortunate.

[[lessons]]
$$**Keep a ledger for the quality of your choices that is separate from the ledger for what happened.** When a project fails or a risky bet pays off, the pull is to grade the person on the outcome alone, so the lucky get praised for bad habits and the unlucky get blamed for sound ones. A review that starts with "what did we know, and what did we weigh?" before it reaches "what happened?" treats both results fairly. Kept over a year, those two columns will show you which of your habits are working and which results were only luck.$$

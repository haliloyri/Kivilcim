[[open]]
In a suburb of Virginia there is a quarantine building that most passers-by never notice. Inside, monkeys shipped in from the Philippines are dying one after another. The people who work there walk through the door each morning, and none of them can say whether the sickness will ever reach a human being.

[[story]]
The monkey house in Reston, Virginia, in late 1989 belongs to the world of Richard Preston's *The Hot Zone*. A shipment of cynomolgus macaques, imported from the Philippines, had arrived at a quarantine facility there, and many of the animals fell ill and died. For a while nobody could say why.

Samples went to the US Army's infectious disease institute, USAMRIID, at Fort Detrick in Frederick, Maryland. Tests there found a virus related to Ebola in the monkeys. It was later named Reston virus. The trouble was timing. The African Ebola viruses killed a large share of the people they infected, and no one yet knew what the Reston virus would do to a human being.

The reasoning of those weeks was simple. If an unknown virus might be deadly to people, you begin by assuming the worst. The Army and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention worked side by side. People who handled the animals were tested. CDC examined about 2,200 monkey blood samples. Within months it introduced a special-permit requirement for importing monkeys, which took effect on 17 April 1990.

The outcome turned out different from the fear. According to the CDC, Reston virus does not cause illness in people. Of 178 people tested after contact with the animals, six carried antibodies to filoviruses, meaning they had met the virus. Four showed signs of recent infection, and one of those is believed to have caught it through a cut on a finger during an autopsy of an infected animal. None of them became sick.

It would be easy to look at that result and call the precautions wasted. That would be a mistake. On the day the decisions were made, nothing in hand showed that Reston virus was harmless to humans. The same virus had also reached facilities in Pennsylvania and Texas, so the problem was not confined to one building. The fair question about a precaution is what was known when it was taken. A fire alarm that rings on a day without a fire has not failed.

This is hard to hold onto in practice. When the feared outcome never arrives, the people who prepared for it can look as though they overreacted, because nobody sees the disaster that did not happen. In Reston the only thing they had to lean on was the size of what they did not know.

[[lessons]]
$$**Judge a decision by what you knew on the day you made it, not by how things turned out.** The teams in Reston acted on the worst case and later learned that the virus did not make people ill, which did not make their decision a bad one. A safeguard you put in place at work or at home may look pointless afterwards, simply because the disaster never came. After any decision, ask what information you had and what else you could reasonably have done with it. That question lets you tell a good decision that ended badly by luck from a bad one that happened to end well.$$

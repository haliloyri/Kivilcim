[[open]]
Picture the person on your team everyone likes. He's charming, easy to be around, and the kind of colleague who makes an ordinary week feel lighter. Now picture that his work isn't good enough, and you're the one whose job it is to say so. Most of us know exactly what we'd be tempted to do. Kim Scott did it, and she has spent years explaining why it was one of her worst mistakes as a manager.

[[story]]
Early in her management career, Kim Scott had an employee she calls Bob in her book *Radical Candor*. Bob was likable in the way that makes a team feel good about itself. He was charming, well-liked, and pleasant company. By every social measure he fit right in.

The problem was his work. It was consistently below the standard the team needed. It wasn't one bad week or one project that slipped. It was a steady pattern.

Scott knew this, and she also knew how she felt about Bob. She didn't want to hurt him. So she did what a lot of kind, well-meaning managers do. She softened the criticism, or she skipped it entirely. When they talked, she praised what she could and encouraged him to keep going. In her mind she was being supportive, and to Bob it probably felt that way too.

Meanwhile the rest of the team was living with the real situation. They started redoing Bob's output, or quietly working around it. Nobody announced it. It just became part of the job. And as the extra work piled up, so did the resentment. Scott's silence cost her something else as well: her team's trust in her judgment. From where they sat, either their manager couldn't see the problem, or she could see it and wasn't doing anything about it. Neither answer made them feel good about her.

This went on for roughly ten months. By then the situation was past fixing. The gap between Bob's work and what the team needed had been left alone so long that no conversation could close it. Scott had to fire him.

In that conversation, Bob asked her something she has never forgotten. It was some version of this:

> "Why didn't you tell me? Why didn't anybody tell me?"

##Scott had no good answer, because there wasn't one. The kindness she'd shown him for ten months was the reason he was hearing the truth for the first time on the day he lost his job.##

Scott later gave this failure a name: **ruinous empathy**. It describes caring about someone personally but failing to challenge them directly. You care, but you won't say the hard thing, and so your care does real damage. Her alternative is what she calls **radical candor**: caring personally *and* challenging directly, at the same time. For Bob, radical candor would have meant hearing early, clearly and specifically that his work wasn't there yet, from someone who plainly wanted him to succeed.

[[lessons]]
$$**Silence isn't the kind option.** Bob's question at the firing, "Why didn't anyone tell me?", shows what ruinous empathy really does. Scott believed she was protecting him. In fact she was withholding the one thing he needed to improve, which was accurate information. Bob never got the chance to fix a problem he didn't know he had. What felt like care from Scott's side meant, from his side, that nobody was on his side at all.$$

$$**Delay makes the conversation bigger.** Every softened review made the next honest one harder. One missed chance becomes a pattern, and the pattern becomes a gap too wide to mention casually, so it gets put off again. After roughly ten months, the talk Scott could have had as a small correction had grown into the biggest conversation a manager can have: a firing.$$

$$**The whole team pays for your silence.** Bob wasn't the only one affected. His colleagues redid his work, absorbed the extra effort, and grew resentful. The standard dropped quietly, and the team began to doubt Scott's judgment. Avoiding one uncomfortable conversation with one person ended up weakening trust across the whole group.$$

[[reflect]]
- Think of a time you softened feedback. Were you protecting the other person's growth, or your own comfort in the moment?
- Is there someone whose work others are quietly redoing or working around, the way Bob's colleagues did? Do they know?
- Look back at a conversation that ended up big and painful. What small, specific thing could you have said months earlier?
- Where are you giving someone encouragement when what they need is information?

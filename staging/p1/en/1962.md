A man visits his country house and gets angry at the crumbling walls, at plane trees with no leaves, at the old slave standing in the doorway. Each time, the caretaker gives him the same answer. The man has just begun to understand something, but he is not ready to say it aloud.

[[story]]
The twelfth letter to Lucilius begins with Seneca's complaint about his country place. He protests at the money being spent on the tumble-down building. His bailiff insists the flaws are not due to carelessness: he is doing everything possible, but the house is old. And this is the house that grew under Seneca's own hands. What does the future hold for him, he wonders, if stones of his own age are already crumbling?

Angry, he takes the next chance to vent in front of the bailiff. The plane trees, he says, are neglected: no leaves, branches gnarled and shrivelled, trunks rough and unkempt. Someone should loosen the earth at their feet and water them. The bailiff swears by Seneca's protecting deity that he has done everything and never relaxed his efforts, but the trees are old. Between you and me, Seneca admits, he planted those trees himself and saw their first leaves.

Then he turns to the door and asks who the broken-down dotard is. You have done well to put him at the entrance, he jokes, for he is outward bound. Where did he come from? The old man answers: do you not know me, sir? I am Felicio, you used to bring me little images, my father was Philositus the steward, and I am your pet slave. Seneca thinks the man is crazy. Has his pet slave become a little boy again? It could be, he says, since his teeth are just dropping out.

Seneca owes it to his country place, he writes, that his old age became apparent wherever he turned. But he draws a sweet conclusion from it. Let us cherish and love old age, for it is full of pleasure if one knows how to use it. Fruits are most welcome when almost over, and youth is most charming at its close. Each pleasure keeps its greatest delights for the end, and life is at its most delightful on the downward slope before the abrupt decline.

Time, he adds, is made of circles inside circles. One reaches from birth to the last day, then come youth, childhood, the year, the month, and the smallest of all, the day, which has its own sunrise and sunset. So every day should be run as though it closed the series and completed our existence.

He then recalls Pacuvius, who held Syria by long occupancy and held a funeral dinner in his own honour every day, with wine and the usual feasting. Afterwards he was carried from the dining room to his bedroom while the people around him applauded and sang in Greek that he had lived his life. Thus Pacuvius had himself carried out to burial daily. Seneca asks us to do from a good motive what Pacuvius did from a bad one: to go to sleep glad, saying I have lived. For the man who can say that, every morning that follows is a bonus.

[[lessons]]
$$**If you can say "I have lived" when you close the day, tomorrow arrives as a bonus.** Someone who makes the phone call to a relative they have been putting off, or finishes the half-done task on the desk before closing up, goes to bed without carrying a debt. Seneca's advice is not gloomy, since closing each day like a circle also takes the weight off things pushed to tomorrow. The habit lets you see age as the stretch where each remaining day can be lived with less hurry, and it makes the evening a moment to settle accounts with yourself instead of a collapse into sleep.$$

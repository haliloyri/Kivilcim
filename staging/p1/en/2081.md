On a Monday three rating agencies each change a few letters on a page. Nothing has changed in the company's offices, staff or policies. But in contracts signed years earlier, a clause has been waiting for exactly this moment, and the bills it triggers will arrive within a day.

[[story]]
Andrew Ross Sorkin's *Too Big to Fail* covers the insurance giant AIG, and this story concerns two days, 15 and 16 September 2008, right after Lehman Brothers filed for bankruptcy. AIG's financial products unit had sold credit default swaps, contracts that pay out when bonds go bad, on more than $500 billion of assets. According to an analysis from the Kellogg School of Management, AIG held no offsetting positions against them, so these were one-way bets.

Many of those contracts carried a condition. If AIG's credit rating fell, it had to post more collateral, which the Federal Reserve's later report on AIG describes as a feature of its financial products agreements. On 15 September all three major agencies cut AIG below AA-. The Kellogg analysis puts the collateral demands on its swaps at $32 billion that day, against $8.6 billion three days earlier.

The next day, 16 September, the Fed's Board, with full Treasury support, authorized the New York Fed to lend AIG up to $85 billion. The loan carried interest at three-month LIBOR plus 850 basis points. As security, the Fed took all of AIG's assets and those of its main unregulated subsidiaries, including the stock of nearly all its regulated ones. In return, the US government would receive 79.9 percent of AIG's equity.

The Fed gave its reason in a single sentence: "a disorderly failure of AIG could add to already significant levels of financial market fragility." The loan was meant to let AIG meet its obligations as they fell due while it sold businesses in an orderly way, and those sales were planned as the main source of repayment. The credit line was set at two years.

What changed on that Monday was not the company's buildings or staff. It was one letter grade, and the contracts had been written so that payment followed the grade. A debt that had been manageable on Friday became a demand that nobody could meet on Tuesday without help.

The terms say how the lender saw the position. A company that had been paying its own way a week earlier now pledged nearly everything it owned and most of its equity, in exchange for time.

[[lessons]]
$$**Read your contracts for the clauses that switch on when your standing changes.** Most agreements describe what happens on an ordinary day, but some contain conditions that only activate when a rating, a revenue level or a credit check moves. A café owner whose supplier agreement lets the supplier demand cash up front after a poor credit report might never have read that line while business was fine. Finding such clauses while things are calm gives you time to negotiate them, to keep a cash reserve against them, or at least to know what a bad week will cost before it arrives.$$

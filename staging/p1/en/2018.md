At the opening of France's new National Institute in Paris, a young naturalist stands up to read a paper about elephants. At the time it was still widely believed that no animal species had ever vanished from the Earth. By the end of his paper, that belief has a problem.

[[story]]
Elizabeth Kolbert gives the second chapter of *The Sixth Extinction* to the mastodon and to the man who read its teeth, Georges Cuvier. He had come to Paris in the spring of 1795, aged 26, and the paper he read the following year was his first in paleontology. Published accounts agree on its subject, a comparison of living elephants with fossil ones.

The method was plain. Cuvier set the jaws of Indian and African elephant skeletons beside mammoth fossils and beside a fossil skeleton then known as the "Ohio animal," the creature he would later name the mastodon. Each pair differed. Indian and African elephants turned out to be separate species, and the fossil animals differed from both.

The argument that followed has a clean shape. If the mammoth and the Ohio animal still lived somewhere, animals that large would be hard to miss, and nobody had found them alive. That left one explanation, which was that these species were gone. As published accounts report it, Cuvier wrote near the end of the paper that the facts seemed to prove a world before ours, destroyed by some kind of catastrophe.

He did not stop at elephants. That same year he turned to a very large skeleton, found in what is now Argentina and mounted in Madrid. Cuvier never saw it. Preliminary engravings had reached Paris in 1795, and from those, and above all from the skull, the teeth and the shoulder, he judged the animal to be related to the sloths. In early 1796 he named it *Megatherium americanum*, the great American beast.

What stands out is how little equipment the work needed. There was no new instrument and no expedition. It was comparison, done carefully, between bones that were already sitting in collections, and engravings that someone had thought to send. The question that made the difference was a plain one: does this bone match anything alive?

The paper was printed in 1800 under the title *Mémoires sur les espèces d'éléphants vivants et fossiles*. Later accounts credit it, together with his work on the sloth, with ending a long debate over whether extinction was real. Cuvier himself went on to defend catastrophism, the idea that sudden upheavals wiped out species and that new ones then took their place. That part of his thinking was later challenged, while his proof that species can be lost was not.

[[lessons]]
$$**Test the belief everyone shares by looking for the plainest evidence against it.** A belief held by a whole room usually survives because nobody has laid the object next to its alternative and compared the two side by side, and the longer it goes unchecked the more settled it feels. A restaurant that insists its slowest dish is its most popular could count the orders for a week, and a team that insists a feature is unused could check the logs before arguing. Pick one assumption your group repeats without checking, write down the single measurement that would settle it, and bring that measurement to the next discussion instead of another opinion.$$

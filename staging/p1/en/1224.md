A little boy in school overhears an adult talking about him. The words aren't meant cruelly, maybe, but they land: *that's the boy with the broken brain.* He carries that sentence with him for years. Later, he'll build a career teaching millions of people how to learn.

[[story]]
Jim Kwik tells his own story at the start of *Limitless*.

When he was about five years old, he had a head injury at school. Afterwards, things changed. School became a struggle. He had trouble concentrating. Remembering things was hard. Learning to read took him far longer than it took his classmates. Teachers would repeat something, and he still couldn't hold on to it.

At some point, he heard an adult refer to him as "the boy with the broken brain."

##A child who hears a sentence like that rarely forgets it. Kwik didn't.##

For years, he says, that label shaped how he saw himself. If his brain was broken, then of course he struggled. Of course he was slow. Of course other kids were smarter. The label didn't just describe his difficulties. It explained them in a way that seemed permanent.

Things came to a head in college. Kwik was overwhelmed. He was working harder than everyone around him and still falling behind. He was exhausted, and he felt like a failure.

Then a new thought struck him. All through school, he'd been taught *what* to learn: history, math, science. But nobody had ever taught him *how* to learn. How to focus. How to remember. How to read efficiently. He'd been trying to solve every problem with the same ineffective methods, and blaming his brain when they didn't work.

So he set out to study learning itself. He read everything he could about memory, speed reading, focus and the brain. He experimented with techniques on himself. And slowly, his results began to change. He could read faster, remember more and understand more deeply.

What changed wasn't his brain's basic capacity. It was his methods, and his beliefs about what that brain could do.

That discovery eventually became his work. Kwik began teaching others the same techniques, and later built a career helping people learn faster and remember more. His central message, in *Limitless*, grows directly from his childhood: many of the limits we believe about ourselves are really labels, and labels can be wrong.

The boy with the broken brain, it turned out, had never been broken. He'd just never been shown how it worked.

[[lessons]]
$$**Labels can last for decades.** One sentence overheard as a child shaped how Kwik saw himself for years. Labels like "bad at math" or "not a reader" often feel like facts when they're really someone's passing judgment. Noticing where your own labels came from is the first step to questioning them.$$

$$**Learning how to learn is its own skill.** Kwik realized he'd been taught what to learn, never how. Once he studied focus, memory and reading as skills, his results changed. Many struggles that look like a lack of ability are really a lack of good methods, which can be learned.$$

$$**A setback can become a mission.** Kwik's childhood difficulty became the source of his life's work. The struggle gave him both the motivation and the insight to help others. Painful experiences don't always become strengths, but sometimes they point directly to what we're meant to offer.$$

[[reflect]]
- What label about your abilities have you carried with you since childhood?
- Were you ever actually taught how to learn, or only ever taught what to learn?
- Where might you be blaming your brain when the real problem is your method?

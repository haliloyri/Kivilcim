In Washington, the gun maker Samuel Colt receives a visitor from Texas who is visiting during a recruiting trip. The visitor is not an engineer or a salesman. He has carried the weapon into a fight, and Colt will later credit him with improvements to it.

[[story]]
The visitor was Samuel Hamilton Walker, and the fight he knew about took place on June 8, 1844, near Walker's Creek in what is now Kendall County. S. C. Gwynne's *Empire of the Summer Moon* gives the Colt revolver a central place in the Rangers' contest with the Comanche. That morning about twenty Texas Rangers under Captain John Coffee Hays met a war party of about seventy-five Comanche.

The Rangers circled the Comanche camp, found an approach out of sight and charged, but they were too few and fell back. The Comanche chased them for about two miles. The Rangers took cover in a grove of trees, and when the warriors charged again, each Ranger could fire five shots from a surplus Colt Paterson revolver, a weapon the Texas Navy had held, without reloading. A single-shot gun needs time after every shot. These did not. Volley after volley broke the charge, although a few warriors got through and caused Ranger casualties.

The Comanche halted nearby. Hays led a mounted sortie, and a long rifle shot by Robert Gillespie killed a Comanche chief, after which the war party withdrew. The Rangers lost one man and several were wounded; Comanche losses are estimated at twenty to thirty.

Walker had been in that fight with Hays's company. Two years later, in 1846, he travelled to Washington on a recruiting trip and called on Colt himself. Colt later credited him with improvements, among them a stationary trigger and a trigger guard. The six-shot weapon that resulted carries the name Walker Colt. Walker died on October 9, 1847, at Huamantla in Mexico, and his name stayed on the gun.

Look at where the improvement came from. Colt designed the revolver, but the person who had tested it hardest was Walker. Walker was not an engineer, and still he knew where the design came up short in a way no workshop bench could show. Colt, for his part, listened, and the gun changed after that conversation. Knowledge from the field reached the drawing board, and the drawing board did not turn it away. Putting Walker's name on the result also tells you that the contribution was seen and recorded.

[[lessons]]
$$**Listen to the person who uses your product under the worst conditions, not only the person who designed it.** For a software team that is the customer on the support line. For a manager it is the colleague who works at the front of the process. A design built at a desk has been tested only in part, because the problems of real conditions tend to show up only there. If you hear a field complaint without defending yourself, the next version fits the people who use it. Ask concretely: where did it fail you last time, and what slowed you down?$$

Seventeen men sit around a table in a government building in Berlin. The stenographer takes down every word. In front of the chairman lies a memorandum, and it is not about Germany. It is about the laws of American states, and the men have clearly read it.

[[story]]
Isabel Wilkerson's *Caste* sets American racial hierarchy beside others in history, and this episode comes from that comparison. It rests on the research of the legal historian James Whitman, who read an old stenographic record in German archives. The record, which shows who said what in the room and was not published until 1989, covers a meeting on 5 June 1934 of the Commission on Criminal Law Reform, which was drafting new law for the Nazi state.

Justice Minister Franz Gürtner chaired it. He opened with a short statement and a Justice Ministry memorandum on "race legislation in the American states." One of the officials, Fritz Grau, presented a staff report on what he called the "Jewish menace." He proposed separating "racially foreign elements" from Germans and urged criminal bans on sexual relations between Jews and Germans, and along the way he cited American segregation laws. The judge and law professor Karl Klee treated the American statutes as a model worth studying. Roland Freisler, later a notorious judge, noted that "thirty of the states" had race legislation.

The interest did not stop at the meeting. Heinrich Krieger, a German lawyer, had studied at the University of Arkansas law school in 1933 and 1934 and then wrote a book titled *Race Law in the United States*, published in 1936. A review of Whitman's work calls his research indispensable for what followed. On 15 September 1935 the Nuremberg Laws were proclaimed: the Reich Flag Law, the Reich Citizenship Law and the Blood Law.

Whitman does not claim that America caused Nazism, and nobody retelling this episode should either. His point is narrower, and it is a point about method. When these lawyers needed to show that sorting people by descent was a normal thing for a modern state to do, they had a ready source of examples, and it was across the Atlantic.

That is why the comparison Wilkerson draws matters, and why the 1934 record is so unsettling to read. The men at that table did not need to invent the idea of a fixed hierarchy of human groups, written into statute. They could point to a country that already had one and call it a precedent, and a precedent carries a certain calm authority: it suggests that the question has already been asked and answered by someone respectable.

[[lessons]]
$$**A harmful proposal gets its power from the respectable precedent attached to it.** When someone says "other people already do this", the sentence moves attention from whether the rule is right to whether it has been done before. You can see it in a workplace policy copied from a competitor, or in a family rule defended with "that's how it always was". The remedy is a habit: when a precedent is offered, ask who else has done this, what happened to the people it was applied to, and whether the precedent itself deserves to stand. Those three questions pull the rule into the discussion instead of letting it pass unexamined, and they give the people deciding a moment to think.$$

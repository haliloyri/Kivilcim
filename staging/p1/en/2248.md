A plywood box with a slot cut in the lid sits on a break-room table, next to a pile of bagels. Nobody is watching it. A man will come back before lunch to collect the cash and the leftovers, and the only record of who ate what will be the money inside.

[[story]]
Paul Feldman had a doctorate from MIT and a career analyzing weapons spending for the Navy and other Washington agencies, and he was tired of it. Steven Levitt and Stephen Dubner told his story in a magazine article in 2004, and it fed into *Freakonomics*. At the research institute where he worked, he had started bringing bagels on Fridays as a treat for staff. People from other floors heard about it, and he began leaving out a basket for cash. At the peak he was carrying in 15 dozen bagels a week, and about 95 percent of the money came back.

In 1984 he quit and became a bagel man. His economist friends thought he had lost his mind. He drove around the office parks outside Washington, left bagels and a cash basket in a company's snack room early in the morning, and returned before lunch for the money and the leftovers. Within a few years he was delivering 700 dozen bagels a week to 140 companies and earning as much as he had as an analyst.

He had also built an experiment without meaning to. He knew what he delivered and what came back in cash, so he could tell, down to the penny, how honest each office was. The 95 percent from his own office, he realized, had been flattering, because the people there knew him and liked him. Among strangers he learned to settle for less. An office paying 90 percent or more counted as honest. Between 80 and 90 was annoying but tolerable, and below 80 he had to grit his teeth.

The container mattered as much as the customers. The open basket lost money, and a coffee can with a slot in its lid was too tempting as well. A plywood box with a slot, which he built himself, worked. By 2004 he was leaving about 7,000 of them a year and losing, on average, one to theft. The same offices that routinely took more than a tenth of his bagels without paying almost never took the box.

His records held other patterns. Offices with a few dozen employees paid 3 to 5 percent better than offices with a few hundred. Payment slid from 1992, reached about 87 percent by the summer of 2001, and rose by two points right after September 11. The week of Christmas cost him two points.

[[lessons]]
$$**Decide in advance how much slippage you can live with, then build for the honest majority.** A shared snack fund in an office or a lending shelf in a club will never return a full hundred percent, and the group that insists on perfect returns usually ends up with cameras, rules and bad feeling. The alternative is to pick a level below which you act and to treat the gap above it as the price of trusting people. Once you have that line, you can stop reading every missing coin as a betrayal and spend your attention on the few places where payment really collapses.$$

Twenty-six seconds on the clock, one yard from the end zone. A bruising running back waits in the backfield, and the defense across from him is lined up to stop exactly one thing: his run. The quarterback takes the snap and steps back to throw.

[[story]]
Annie Duke opens *Thinking in Bets* with this moment and with Pete Carroll, the coach of the Seattle Seahawks. It came near the end of Super Bowl XLIX. Seattle trailed New England 28-24 and stood at the Patriots' 1-yard line, second down, with one timeout left. A touchdown would win the game.

Carroll had Marshawn Lynch, his star running back, on the field. He called a pass anyway. Russell Wilson threw toward Ricardo Lockette, and Malcolm Butler, an undrafted rookie, stepped in front of the receiver and caught the ball at the goal line. The game ended there, 28-24 to New England.

Carroll's reasoning, as he and the press later reported it, had a clock behind it. Seattle sent out one tight end, three receivers and one running back against New England's goal-line defense, and Carroll felt a run was the wrong matchup. An incomplete pass stops the clock without costing a timeout. So the plan was to throw once on second down, then run on third and, if needed, on fourth, with a timeout still in hand. He also wanted to leave Tom Brady very little time if Seattle came up empty.

Now the other side of the ledger. Duke cites about fifteen years of NFL data showing that a short pass in that situation is intercepted less than two percent of the time. NFL.com reported that no quarterback had been intercepted from the 1-yard line all season. Seattle's call carried a risk, but a small one, and the outcomes it could produce were mostly good: a touchdown, or an incomplete pass that kept every option alive.

Look at what the decision was before the ball was thrown. It was a bet with several possible endings, and one of them, the rarest, was the worst. Fewer than one pass in fifty ends that way, and that was the one the world saw.

This is the detail that matters for anyone who decides things for a living. The quality of the call lived in the odds, the clock and the plan for the next two downs, all of which existed before Butler moved. Nothing that happened after the snap could change what Carroll knew when he made it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Judge a decision by the odds and options you held when you made it, and write them down before the result arrives.** Once the outcome is known, memory rewrites the choice to match it, and a one-in-fifty failure starts to look like negligence. A short note before a hire, a launch or a big purchase, listing the likely outcomes and your plan for each, gives you something fair to check later. Then a bad result tells you to look at the note, and only the note can say whether the thinking was wrong.$$

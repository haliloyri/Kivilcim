The first flight lasts thirteen minutes. The airplane is a collection of flat, angular panels, and it takes off from a secret base in the Nevada desert. Everything the program has promised, to the people who paid for it and the people who doubted it, rests on those thirteen minutes.

[[story]]
Ben Rich and Leo Janos's *Skunk Works* tells the story of Lockheed's stealth work, and its turning point was a small, odd demonstrator called Have Blue. In the summer of 1975 the Pentagon's research agency DARPA held what Lockheed calls a "pole-off", a competition to see whose design gave the weakest radar return. Skunk Works won, and with it the chance to build the aircraft.

The idea behind the winning shape comes from physics. Published accounts, including Rich's, say the key was a theory by a Soviet scientist, Pyotr Ufimtsev, about how radar waves scatter off the edges of an object. It suggested that what mattered for a radar return was how an object's edges were arranged more than how big it was. If so, a plane made of flat panels, angled so that radar waves bounced away from the source, could be nearly invisible.

Skunk Works built two Have Blue prototypes. The first one flew on December 1, 1977, at Area 51, and the flight lasted thirteen minutes, according to the National Air and Space Museum. Both prototypes were eventually lost in crashes. The Smithsonian says they had done their job: they proved that a flyable stealth aircraft was possible.

The next step came quickly. DARPA gave Skunk Works the contract for the operational aircraft less than a year after Have Blue's first flight. That aircraft, the F-117, first flew in 1981, 31 months after the award, and deliveries began in 1982. Its faceted panels and radar-absorbent coating made it nearly invisible to radar.

The F-117 stayed secret even after it entered service. It reached initial operational capability in 1983, and the program was acknowledged publicly only in 1988. The first formal public appearance came in 1990.

The structure of the story is worth noticing. The risky idea was tested on a smaller airplane before anyone committed to the real one. Losing the prototypes did not end the program, because they had been built to answer a single question. Once that was answered, the larger project had something solid underneath it.

There is also a matter of order. A far larger program was approved only after a competition had been won and a prototype had flown. The money followed the evidence. Each stage asked for a little more commitment than the one before, and each stage had a result to show for the last.

[[lessons]]
$$**Test the one assumption your whole plan rests on with a small version before you build the real thing.** A restaurant owner who wants to open a second site can serve the new menu for a month at the first one and see whether regulars order it. A team planning a large app can build the one screen where its central idea either works or does not. A small test that is allowed to fail costs little, and a result you can point to makes the bigger decision easier to defend when you take it to the people who must approve it.$$

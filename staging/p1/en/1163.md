In 1920, Americans elected a president who had one outstanding qualification: he looked exactly like a president. He was tall, handsome and dignified, with a deep, resonant voice. He turned out to be one of the worst presidents the country ever had.

[[story]]
In *Blink*, Malcolm Gladwell spends much of the book praising fast judgments: the firefighter who senses danger, the art expert who spots a fake. But he also devotes a chapter to the other side of the coin. He gives it a name: the **Warren Harding Error**.

The story begins in Ohio around the turn of the twentieth century. Harry Daugherty, an ambitious political operator, met a local newspaper publisher named Warren Harding. Daugherty was immediately struck by him. Harding had a strong jaw, broad shoulders, a warm voice and a presence that filled a room. Daugherty had a single thought.

##This man looks like a president.##

Harding was not a remarkable thinker. He was not known for hard work or deep knowledge of policy. He liked poker, golf and drinking with friends. But Daugherty took him under his wing, and Harding's appearance kept opening doors. He became a state senator, then lieutenant governor, then a United States senator. In 1920, the Republican Party, deadlocked over other candidates, turned to him. He won the presidency by a wide margin.

His time in office went badly. His administration became associated with scandal and poor judgment, and historians have consistently ranked him among the worst presidents in American history. He died in office in 1923.

What happened, Gladwell argues, is that voters and party leaders made a snap judgment. Everything about Harding's appearance triggered the idea of "presidential," and that impression crowded out the question that actually mattered: *could this man do the job?*

This, Gladwell says, is the dark side of rapid cognition. Our quick judgments are not always built on expertise. Sometimes they're built on associations and stereotypes we don't even know we hold: that a tall man looks like a leader, that a confident voice means competence, that an attractive person must be capable. Gladwell points out, for example, that tall men are heavily overrepresented among the CEOs of large companies. Nobody decided to promote tall men. The bias works quietly, one "gut feeling" at a time.

The difference between a brilliant snap judgment and a Warren Harding Error isn't speed. It's whether the cues your mind is reacting to are actually relevant to the question.

[[lessons]]
$$**Appearance can hijack judgment.** Daugherty's first thought, "he looks like a president," carried Harding all the way to the White House. His looks answered a question nobody should have been asking. When someone strikes you instantly as a leader or an expert, it's worth asking which signals created that impression, and whether they have anything to do with the job.$$

$$**Relevant cues versus irrelevant ones.** The firefighter's gut responded to heat and sound, which really do predict danger. Harding's supporters responded to height and voice, which don't predict good leadership. Fast judgment is only as good as the signals it's built on.$$

$$**Bias hides in ordinary choices.** Tall men being overrepresented among CEOs shows how quietly this works. No single decision looked unfair. Each simply "felt right." Noticing that pattern in your own choices is uncomfortable, but it's the only way to correct it.$$

[[reflect]]
- When did someone's appearance, voice or confidence mislead you about their ability?
- Which "looks the part" signals do you tend to trust too quickly?
- Whose ability might you be underrating because they don't look or sound like your idea of success?

&&In your next quick judgment of a person, what will you check before you trust it?&&

A dinner is served in Atlanta, at the Waverly Hotel, and the guests are the directors of the company that makes Oreos, Ritz crackers and Winston cigarettes. The host is the man who runs that company. He has taken care over the evening, and nobody at the table yet knows what the offer waiting behind it is.

[[story]]
Bryan Burrough and John Helyar's *Barbarians at the Gate* is about the 1988 fight over RJR Nabisco, and this story is about the paper that started it. Time magazine reported that the company's chief executive, F. Ross Johnson, hosted the directors at an elaborate dinner at Atlanta's Waverly Hotel on 19 October 1988. Around those days his group, backed by Shearson Lehman Hutton, proposed taking the company private at $75 a share, roughly $17 billion. The stock had been trading at about $56 in mid-October.

What made it an odd purchase was who sat on the buying side. According to Time, Johnson and seven other top executives would put in $20 million for a stake of about 8.5 percent. When the deal closed, that stake would be worth $200 million. It was scheduled to grow to 18.5 percent after that. Critics estimated it could reach $2.6 billion within five years, with Johnson's own share alone near $1 billion.

Johnson answered those numbers in Time. He said he had always meant to share the wealth with the roughly 15,000 employees who would remain, and he is quoted: "I wasn't going to take 18% of this company for seven people."

The board now had to sit on the other side of the table from the man who ran the company. A committee of five outside directors turned the proposal into an open contest, so that anyone who wanted RJR Nabisco could bid for it. One director described Johnson to Time as a "raider from the inside." Henry Kravis's firm, KKR, was among the bidders who came in.

The price climbed from there. By Time's issue of 5 December 1988, Johnson's own group had raised its offer to $100 a share, about $23 billion. That was $25 a share above where the group had started, and the gap appeared in a room that now held other buyers as well.

The logic of the episode is plain. A manager inside a company is often the person who knows its worth best and the person who gains most if it is sold cheaply. The committee kept those two roles from sitting alone at one table, and let outside buyers test the number.

[[lessons]]
$$**When you both know the facts and stand to gain, ask someone outside to test the price before anyone asks you to.** Picture a department head who writes the specification for a supplier contract and also takes the largest share of its benefit. The people around that head will sooner or later wonder whether the numbers were shaped, and they will stop trusting the figures even if they were honest. Inviting a second quote or a second reader into the decision early protects both the quality of the choice and the trust placed in you.$$

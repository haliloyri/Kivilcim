A list of 136 children from six institutions in Bucharest. The youngest are six months old, the oldest thirty-one months. A random draw will decide which of them leave for a family and which stay where they are. Nobody involved yet knows what the draw will mean twelve years later.

[[story]]
The researchers behind the Bucharest Early Intervention Project were asking a question that is hard to ask honestly: does a family change what happens to a young child's development? Bruce Perry and Oprah Winfrey's *What Happened to You?* rests on the idea that early relationships shape a person, and this study put that idea to a test few others could run.

Until then, comparisons between children raised in institutions and children raised in families were muddied by a problem. Families who adopt or foster are not a random sample, and neither are the children placed with them. Here the researchers could use chance. Of the 136 children, some were randomly assigned to foster care and the rest to care as usual, meaning they stayed in the institutions. A third group of children who had never lived in an institution was followed for comparison.

The team then returned to the children again and again, at 30, 42 and 54 months, at 8 and 12 years, and later in their teens. At age 12, 107 of the children took an intelligence test. The averages sat far apart. Children from the foster care group scored about 76, children who stayed in institutional care about 69, and the never-institutionalized comparison group about 99. The gap between foster care and usual care was statistically significant.

Look at what that result does and does not say. Foster care helped, and the difference came from a random draw, which is why researchers trust it. Yet the foster care children still scored well below the comparison group. A family did not erase the early years, and no one claims it did.

Timing also left a trace. In the published analysis, children placed at an older age were less likely to follow a typical pattern of scores. The fact is modest and specific, and it points in one direction: the sooner a child is in a steady relationship with a caregiver, the more room there is for development to move.

The study asked about intelligence scores because they can be measured, but its larger subject was a child's world: who answers when a baby cries, who is there in the morning, who stays.

[[lessons]]
$$**Give a young child one steady caring adult, and expect the effect to build over years rather than days.** Development in the first years responds to who shows up consistently, which is why a regular bedtime voice, the same teacher each term or one mentor who stays can matter more than a clever programme. Gains may be partial, as the Bucharest scores show, so treat any improvement as real without waiting for a full reversal. The same logic reaches you as an adult in any care role: the relationship itself is part of the intervention, so protect its continuity before adding techniques.$$

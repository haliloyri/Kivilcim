Here is Linda. She's thirty-one, single, outspoken and very bright. She majored in philosophy. As a student, she cared deeply about discrimination and social justice, and she joined anti-nuclear demonstrations. Now one simple question, which most people get wrong, even when they know better.

[[story]]
In *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, Daniel Kahneman tells the story of what became one of the most famous experiments in psychology. He and Amos Tversky wrote the description of Linda, then asked people which of two statements was more probable.

Linda is a bank teller.

Linda is a bank teller and is active in the feminist movement.

Take a moment. Most people feel a strong pull toward the second option. Everything in the description seems to point there. Linda sounds like someone who would be active in the feminist movement. "Bank teller" alone seems to miss who she is.

##Around 85 to 90 percent of undergraduates at several major universities chose the second option.##

But it can't be more probable. Every feminist bank teller is also a bank teller. So the chance that Linda is a feminist bank teller can never be greater than the chance that she is a bank teller. Adding a condition can only keep the probability the same or make it smaller. Kahneman calls this mistake the **conjunction fallacy**.

Why do we fall for it? Because System 1, the fast, intuitive part of the mind, doesn't naturally think in probabilities. It thinks in stories. It asks how well a description fits, how representative Linda is of each option. "Feminist bank teller" fits the story better. So it feels more likely, even though it's logically less likely.

Kahneman and Tversky didn't only test ordinary students. They gave the problem to doctoral students in the decision-science program at Stanford's business school, people with extensive training in probability and statistics. They made the same error at a similar rate.

Even knowing the answer doesn't completely turn off the feeling. Kahneman quotes the biologist Stephen Jay Gould, who described his own reaction. He knew the right answer, Gould wrote, yet a little homunculus in his head kept jumping up and down, shouting at him that she couldn't just be a bank teller.

That's what makes the Linda problem so unsettling. It's not a trick of wording. It's a demonstration that a coherent, detailed story can overpower simple logic, even in minds trained to resist it.

And it matters far beyond psychology labs. Forecasts, business plans and courtroom arguments often become more persuasive as they get more detailed. Each extra detail makes the story feel more real. And each one, logically, makes it less likely to be true.

[[lessons]]
$$**A good story can beat logic.** "Feminist bank teller" fits Linda so well that most people chose it, even though it can't be more likely than "bank teller." Our minds are built to judge coherence, not probability. When a scenario feels right because it hangs together nicely, that's exactly when to slow down.$$

$$**More detail, lower odds.** Every added detail makes a story more vivid and convincing, and every added detail makes it less probable. Plans that depend on a long chain of things going right, or fears built on very specific scenarios, deserve extra suspicion for this reason.$$

$$**Training doesn't switch off intuition.** Stanford decision-science students made the same mistake. Stephen Jay Gould knew the answer and still felt the pull. Knowing about a bias doesn't make it disappear. What helps is having a habit of checking the logic when the stakes are real.$$

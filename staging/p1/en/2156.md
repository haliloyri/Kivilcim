Fourteen typists are retrained on an unfamiliar keyboard layout, while eighteen others get extra practice on the one they already know. Both groups are timed. The man running the test also owns the patent on the new layout, and the report of the results does not mention it.

[[story]]
A theme that runs through Nassim Taleb's *Fooled by Randomness* is how readily we turn what happened into a story about why it happened. This story is about one such story, told for decades about the typewriter keyboard on which you are probably reading this. The version most people have heard goes like this: the QWERTY layout won by accident, a better design existed, and we are stuck with the worse one.

QWERTY goes back to Christopher Sholes, who patented it in 1868 and sold it to Remington in 1873. Its arrangement kept letters that often follow each other apart, so that the type bars of a mechanical typewriter would not jam. In 1936 August Dvorak patented a rival layout. In 1944 the US Navy ran the comparison described above: fourteen typists retrained on Dvorak's layout, eighteen given more QWERTY practice. The Dvorak group did better.

Dvorak himself was a Navy lieutenant commander, and the study was run by him. It is unclear from the report whether the experiment was properly controlled, and his patent was not mentioned. In 1956 the General Services Administration ran its own test and found that QWERTY typists were about as fast as Dvorak typists, or faster. Interest in Dvorak faded after that, though his supporters dispute the later study too.

In 1985 the economic historian Paul David used QWERTY as his central example in a paper titled "Clio and the Economics of QWERTY." His point was that a small early accident can lock in an outcome even when something better exists. The example travelled far, because it was easy to remember.

In 1990 Stan Liebowitz and Stephen Margolis published "The Fable of the Keys" in the Journal of Law and Economics. They argued that the claim of Dvorak's superiority rested on the Navy study, and that the study was weak. If the better layout was never shown to be better, QWERTY is no proof that markets get stuck with inferior products.

You do not have to take a side to see the shape of it. A neat story about why the world is the way it is spread because it was neat, and the evidence underneath turned out to be one test, run by an interested party, that a later test did not repeat.

[[lessons]]
$$**When a story explains why things are the way they are, look for the test underneath it.** A tidy origin story, such as why a street layout is awkward, why a firm's system is so old or why a tradition began, usually travels because it is memorable and not because someone checked it. Ask who measured the claim, who stood to gain from the result and whether anyone has tried again. A single study by an interested party is a reason to keep asking, and it lets you use the story as a hypothesis instead of a fact.$$

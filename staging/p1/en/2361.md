[[story]]
Andre Agassi's memoir *Open*, published in 2009, starts his tennis life in a Las Vegas backyard, on a court his father, Mike, built himself. At the centre of it stands a ball machine that Mike had modified: midnight black, on big rubber wheels, with an extra-long aluminum neck. Andre calls it "the dragon." He was seven then, and small for his age.

Mike set the machine on a base several feet high and pushed it flush against the net, so that it loomed over the boy. When it fired, the narrow aluminum head recoiled like a whip. The balls left at 110 miles per hour, and Mike aimed them at Andre's feet, which meant every one had to be hit on the rise. There was no time to wait for a friendly bounce.

The numbers came from Mike too. By his math, 2,500 balls a day made 17,500 a week and nearly a million a year. Balls lay across the court by the thousand, so Andre had little room to move. Stepping on one was forbidden, and Mike reacted, in Andre's description, as if his son had stepped on his eyeball.

The net itself stood six inches above regulation height. Mike's reasoning, as Agassi tells it, was that a boy who cleared this net every time would clear the net at Wimbledon one day. From the edge of the court came the same few orders again and again: hit earlier, crowd the ball, never in the net. Mike called the net "your biggest enemy."

The design behind all this is easy to see. The machine fixed the speed, the aim at his feet forced a short, early swing, and the sheer number of balls was meant to turn that swing into habit. Everything was built to make a real match feel slow. In theory, a child trained against 110 miles per hour in a cramped yard would find an ordinary opponent comfortable.

The Sports Illustrated excerpt from the book carries a title that says how Andre lived it: "I Hate Tennis." The same arithmetic that satisfied Mike, a million balls a year, was what a seven-year-old stood in front of every day. The method was precise and the ambition was large, and the boy on the receiving end, as he writes, hated the game.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask the learner what the practice feels like, as well as what it adds up to.** A daily count is clean and persuasive, and it says nothing about the person standing in front of it. A manager who sets a tidy quota for a new hire, or a parent who plans an hour of piano every evening, can track the totals for months without noticing that the work has turned into something to endure. Add one regular question about where the work drags, and you will see early whether the volume is building skill or only building resentment, while there is still time to adjust.$$

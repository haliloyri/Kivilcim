In a museum in Brussels stands a mounted great auk, a big black-and-white seabird that could not fly and that no living person has seen alive. For a long time nobody could say whose skin it was. In a collection in Copenhagen, meanwhile, sit the preserved insides of the last two birds ever reliably seen.

[[story]]
Elizabeth Kolbert tells the great auk's end in *The Sixth Extinction*, and the details of its last season are better documented than most. The bird was once abundant across the North Atlantic, and people hunted it for meat, oil and feathers. By the start of the nineteenth century its numbers in the north-west Atlantic had been decimated. The last few were breeding on the skerries off Iceland's south-west coast.

Scarcity made the birds valuable. Private collectors and museums wanted specimens, and between 1830 and 1841 several trips went out to a rock called Eldey, where auks were caught, killed and sold. Then three years passed with no recorded capture. A dealer named Carl Siemsen paid for another expedition to see whether any were left.

In 1844 the expedition reached Eldey and found two great auks among the smaller birds. Both were killed, and their egg was broken and thrown away. The birds never reached Siemsen. The expedition's leader sold them to Christian Hansen, who passed them on to an apothecary named Möller in Reykjavik, and Möller skinned them and sent the skins and preserved body parts to Denmark.

At the time, nobody recognised these two as the last of their kind. In the late 1850s the English naturalist John Wolley and Cambridge professor Alfred Newton travelled to Iceland and questioned people who had been on the Eldey voyage, which is how the story survived. According to historians of the episode, the trip led Newton to a new idea: that human action could wipe out a species, and quickly.

The skins, though, were lost. The internal organs of the two birds stayed in the Natural History Museum of Denmark, while the records of where the skins went had been poorly kept, and the great auk scholar Errol Fuller listed five mounted birds that might be them. In 2017 a research team sequenced mitochondrial DNA from all five and from the preserved organs. The male's oesophagus matched the Brussels bird exactly. The female's heart matched none of the five, each of which differed from it by at least 16 DNA positions.

The authors added a caution. Mitochondrial DNA cannot always identify one individual, but together with the historical record the match looked like more than coincidence. The wider picture is clearer still: a 2019 study of 41 great auk genomes found no sign that the species was at risk before intensive hunting began in the early sixteenth century, and its models suggest hunting alone could have been enough to finish it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Record where a thing goes at the moment it changes hands.** Importance is usually clear only afterwards, and by then the label, the receipt and even the memory of who carried the thing out of the room have often gone. A volunteer who boxes up a club's old banners and records on one page who took which box can still find them years later, while a volunteer who relies on remembering the handover cannot. When something important passes through several hands, take thirty seconds to write down where it went and who now holds it, so the question "where did it go?" always has an answer.$$

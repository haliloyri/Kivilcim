Alexander Graham Bell sits with a telephone receiver at his ear while a doctor draws a coil slowly along a man's back. The man is the President of the United States. Nobody in the room says a word. Bell is listening for one small click, and he is surprised by where the doctor chooses to search.

[[story]]
Candice Millard's *Destiny of the Republic* tells of a scientific rescue attempt: the search for the bullet inside President James Garfield, who was shot on 2 July 1881. For three weeks his doctors had probed the wound with their fingers without finding it. Alexander Graham Bell, who had patented the telephone five years earlier, volunteered to try something different.

His tool grew out of an induction balance, a device in which metal brought near a coil changes the sound in a telephone receiver. The one Bell built had a battery and several coils on a wooden platform, with one coil wired to a buzzer and another to an earpiece. A coil passed over metal produced a click. Before he ever went to the White House, he tested it on Civil War veterans who still carried bullets in places that were known.

On 26 July, at six in the evening, the real test began. In a letter to his wife Mabel, Bell wrote that the apparatus was spluttering, and that his assistant had gone down to the basement to check the interrupter when the doctors called Bell in anyway. The test ran with the instrument as it stood. Dr. Willard Bliss handled the exploring coils while Bell listened at the telephone, and Garfield lay on his side, held up by a man at the bedside.

Nobody spoke. Bliss moved the coil from the wounds down along the spine, and Bell was surprised, because he had understood that the doctors expected the bullet near the front of the abdomen. Bell later reported that the tone he heard was too feeble to be satisfactory.

He came back on 1 August. This time the doctors agreed that the bullet lay, with reasonable certainty, in the abdominal wall below and to the right of the navel. Bell, however, knew privately that the machine had registered no significant difference over the different parts of Garfield's body that day.

Later accounts suggest that the metal bedsprings under Garfield may have interfered, and that the device might have worked if the doctors had not kept steering Bell toward one area. Garfield died on 19 September, and at the autopsy the bullet was not where the doctors had said. Bell went home a defeated man, according to one account, but he kept refining the induction balance, which came to be known as the metal detector.

Consider what kind of experiment this was. The instrument had been checked on veterans whose bullets had known positions. At the bedside the position was unknown, the doctors held a firm belief about it, and the machine sat in a bed full of metal. A statement of reasonable certainty and a private null reading are two different things, one a belief and the other a measurement.

[[lessons]]
$$**Decide before the test which result would change your mind, and record a weak signal as weak.** Whether you are piloting a new process, checking a sales number or trying a habit, the instrument can only confirm what it is pointed at. Agree in advance where you will look, what a miss would mean, and who is allowed to point the measuring device somewhere unexpected. Think of a hiring review where the sample was drawn only from candidates the manager already liked: it says nothing about everyone else. A faint honest reading is worth more than a confident conclusion that nothing was able to contradict.$$

[[open]]
In a Hadza camp in northern Tanzania, people head out for the day wearing devices that record their heartbeat. The researchers are waiting to collect a single number by evening: how many minutes of the day did these people spend with their hearts working hard?

[[story]]
Two numbers about the same people tell the story, and the first one is large. The Hadza live in northern Tanzania and still get their food by hunting and gathering. The anthropologist David Raichlen and colleagues wanted to measure how much they move instead of guessing, so they fitted 46 people from two Hadza camps with monitors that tracked heart rate continuously. Heart rate is a way to tell how hard a person is moving.

The Hadza spent an average of 134.92 minutes a day in moderate-to-vigorous physical activity, about two hours and fifteen minutes. They kept up those levels across their lives instead of slowing with age. The researchers compared the figure with people in large epidemiological studies in the United States and found that the Hadza did more than 14 times as much.

There is also a yardstick. In industrialized societies the recommendation is 150 minutes a week, and few people reach it. Multiply the Hadza's daily average by seven and you get more than six times that weekly recommendation.

The team looked at blood pressure and some blood markers too. Blood pressure was measured in 198 people, and markers such as cholesterol, triglycerides and C-reactive protein in 23. High blood pressure was rare at every age, and the markers sat at favourable levels. The paper reported no evidence of cardiovascular risk factors in this community.

The second number comes from a later study, and it is the surprise. Raichlen's team strapped accelerometers to the thighs of Hadza adults and found that they spent an average of 9.90 hours a day not walking, similar to people in industrialized populations. The difference lay in how that time was spent. Hadza rest often took the form of squatting, and the researchers' measurements showed that these postures need more activity in the leg muscles than sitting in a chair does.

A limit matters here. The heart-rate figure rests on 46 people from a single group, so it describes those camps rather than all hunter-gatherers.

Put the two numbers together and the picture changes. More than two hours of hard movement sits next to nearly ten hours of not walking. Daniel Lieberman's *Exercised* is interested in details like this one: the same person can be very active and also rest for long stretches, and the two facts do not cancel each other out.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you measure yourself against a benchmark, check what that benchmark actually counts.** For the Hadza, 135 minutes of hard movement a day and almost ten hours of not walking describe the same days, yet each number captures something different. If you look at only one, you land on a result that is too rosy or too grim. The same slip appears at work, when we count meetings and miss the quality of the work, or count hours and miss how the breaks were spent. Asking what a number counts takes a minute, and asking which second number is missing is where the real insight tends to start.$$

[[open]]
In a crowded room of the Louvre, hundreds of phones are raised toward a small painted panel of a woman with folded hands. The painter never let it go. Whoever paid for it did not get it, and the man who made it carried it with him to another country.

[[story]]
Walter Isaacson's *Leonardo da Vinci* is the setting for this story about the Mona Lisa, and what stands out is how long Leonardo stayed with the painting. He began it in Florence around 1503, as a portrait of Lisa Gherardini, the wife of a cloth merchant named Francesco del Giocondo.

A portrait like this was a commission, and a commission normally ends with a handover. Published accounts say this one never did. Leonardo kept the painting. He did not hand it to the family, and years later, when he moved to France in 1516, he took it with him. The Louvre itself says he brought it there.

Leonardo died in France in 1519. After his death the painting passed into the French royal collection, and it was later hung at Fontainebleau and Versailles before it came to the Louvre in the years after the Revolution. Accounts differ on the exact moment King Francis I acquired it, whether before or after Leonardo's death, but they agree that it ended up in royal hands.

Think about what that means in practice. For roughly a decade and a half, through a move between countries and a changing set of patrons, one small panel stayed in the studio. A painter with no client waiting is a painter with no deadline, and no deadline means nobody to say the work is done.

We cannot know his reasons, and there is no point in inventing them. What the record does show is a pattern: Leonardo left several works unfinished or undelivered, and this one he kept for most of his life. The result is the most visited painting in the world's best-known museum.

Compare this with an ordinary commission. A client who is waiting shapes the schedule, the size of the work and the moment it counts as done. Without a client, those three decisions belong to the painter alone, and he can keep adjusting for as long as he is still learning something. That is the quiet value of a work with no due date: its only measure is the maker's own judgment. It is also a risk, because the same freedom lets a piece drift on forever. This panel shows the benefit more than the risk, since it is the painting people still queue to see.

[[lessons]]
$$**Keep one project that answers only to your own standard, and give it years.** Most of our work has clients, deadlines and handovers, and that is as it should be. But a single piece with no due date lets you try things, change your mind and learn from the same material over a long time. Choose something small enough to carry, such as a craft, a piece of writing or a model, and return to it on ordinary days. Do not expect a masterpiece to appear; expect to find out what your standards actually are, which is worth knowing in itself.$$

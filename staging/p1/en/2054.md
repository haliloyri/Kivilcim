A short report about a crime scrolls across a computer screen, one line at a time. Some lines are printed in a different colour, and the reader has been told what that colour means: these lines are false. Meanwhile the reader has a second job, which is to spot the digit 5 in strings of numbers.

[[story]]
The experiment was run by the psychologist Daniel Gilbert and his colleagues Romin Tafarodi and Patrick Malone, and they published it in 1993 under a title that sums up the result: "You Can't Not Believe Everything You Read." Their question was old and philosophical. Does a person first understand a statement and only then decide whether to believe it, as Descartes held, or does understanding already contain believing, as Spinoza held?

The volunteers read crime reports. The false statements were marked by colour, so a careful reader always knew which lines to ignore. Some false lines made the crime sound worse, for instance by adding aggravating detail, and others made it sound milder. After reading, each volunteer recommended a prison sentence between zero and twenty years.

Some volunteers read in peace. Others were kept busy with the digit-spotting task while the reports scrolled by. If understanding and believing were separate steps, the busy group should have recommended roughly the same sentences, because the colour told them what to discard.

They did not. According to published summaries of the results, the busy volunteers who read false lines that made the crime worse recommended an average of 11.15 years, while those who read false lines that excused it recommended 5.83 years. Among the volunteers who were not busy, the same pair of averages was 7.03 and 6.03 years. The calm readers had thrown the marked lines away. The distracted ones had let them stand as facts.

Notice what the design does. By labelling the false lines in advance, it removed every reason for a reader to be fooled except one: not having enough spare attention to apply the label. That is why the comparison is so clean, and why a gap of more than five years between two busy groups points at attention and not at gullibility or poor reading.

The gap is the story. Nothing about the reports changed between the groups; only the mental space available for the second step, doubting. Believing seems to arrive first and for free, and rejecting costs effort that a busy mind may not have.

Why does this matter for anyone who makes bets under uncertainty? Our beliefs are the raw material of those bets, and many of them were picked up while we were doing something else: skimming a message, half-listening in a meeting, scrolling past a headline.

[[lessons]]
$$**Hold your verdict on anything you absorbed while your attention was elsewhere until you can look at it again with your full attention.** Information you took in while busy was probably accepted before you had a chance to doubt it, so it can sit in your head as a fact with its warning label lost. Before you decide something from a message you skimmed, re-read it once, slowly, and ask what you would want to check. That small pause gives doubting the space it was denied the first time, and it costs far less than building on something you never checked.$$

[[story]]
Barbara Tuchman opens *The Guns of August*, her 1962 history of the opening weeks of the First World War, at a funeral. King Edward VII had died on 6 May 1910, aged 68, and London buried him on 20 May. According to published accounts, about 35,000 soldiers lined the route, and estimates of the crowd that came to watch run from three to five million.

The cortege left Buckingham Palace, stopped at Westminster Hall, then moved on through Whitehall, along the Mall and past Marble Arch to Paddington, where a train waited to carry the coffin to Windsor. In the procession walked Caesar, the dead king's favourite dog. Behind the coffin rode nine reigning monarchs, three abreast, in Tuchman's telling. They included the new king, George V, and the dead man's nephew, Kaiser Wilhelm II of Germany, along with the kings of Belgium, Spain, Norway, Portugal, Greece and Denmark and the tsar of Bulgaria.

The day before, the Kaiser had asked for Westminster Hall to be closed so that he could lay a wreath. Police warned that this would cause disorder. He went in through another entrance instead, while the public kept filing past the coffin. Around half a million people had passed through the hall during the lying-in-state.

Tuchman calls the occasion the greatest assemblage of royalty and rank ever gathered in one place, and, of its kind, the last. The second half of that sentence is the one that matters. Within five months the young king of Portugal, Manuel, had lost his throne to a revolution. In 1918 both the Kaiser and the Bulgarian tsar left theirs. Four years after the funeral, the German, the Briton and the Belgian were on opposite sides of a war.

What strikes you is how little the day itself revealed. Every sign on the street said solidity: uniforms, horses, cousins riding side by side, millions in black. A ceremony shows how much effort can be spent on a display, and it says nothing about how an arrangement behaves when it is pushed. The strain was already there, in places no camera pointed at, and it showed up later than the pageant did.

[[lessons]]
$$**A packed ceremony shows how an arrangement looks and says very little about how it holds under strain.** A company can fill a hall for its anniversary party in the very year its best customers are quietly drifting toward a competitor. The applause measures the effort put into the display, while the strain shows up in quieter places, such as who still answers the phone and who has stopped bringing problems. When something you rely on looks magnificent, ask what it is paying for the show and how it would cope on an ordinary bad Tuesday, when nobody is watching and nobody has dressed up.$$

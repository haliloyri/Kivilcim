Before sunrise in Ramadi, a SEAL sniper sees a silhouette slip into the compound and fires. Within minutes, the men inside the house and the men outside it are each sure the other side is the enemy. In the debrief that follows, two men say the mistake was theirs, and the commander turns both down.

[[story]]
Jocko Willink opens *Extreme Ownership* with a morning in the Ma'laab District of Ramadi. Iraqi Army, U.S. Army and SEAL forces are clearing buildings in the dark, and the resistance is heavy. One of the two sniper elements in Willink's Task Unit Bruiser has moved into a new building, away from its planned position.

Dozens of Iraqi soldiers, with their U.S. Army and Marine advisors, enter the sector a couple of hours ahead of schedule. In the early-morning darkness a SEAL sniper sees a man with an AK-47 creep into the compound. He believes they are under attack, and he shoots.

Gunfire comes back from the house. The Iraqi soldiers outside return fire and drop behind concrete walls while reinforcements are called. Marines and soldiers, assuming enemy fighters are inside, fire into the building. The SEALs in there are pinned down, and none of them can tell that the people shooting at them are friendly.

Willink stops his Humvee just behind an Abrams tank, its main gun trained on one building. Across the street stands a house riddled with bullet holes, and next to it a Marine gunnery sergeant coordinating an airstrike. "Hold what you got, Gunny," Willink tells him. "I'm going to check it out." He kicks open the compound door, which sits slightly ajar. Inside, a SEAL platoon chief reports that some insurgents entered the compound and they shot one of them. Willink's answer is short: "It was a blue-on-blue."

One of his men is wounded. An Iraqi soldier is dead, and others are hurt. The rest of the mission, by Willink's account, went well, and he still feels sick.

The debrief in the platoon space produces a long list of failures: plans changed without notice, an unclear communication plan, confusion over radio timing, an Iraqi Army change nobody passed along, shifting timelines, friendly positions never reported. Willink walks in and asks the room, "Whose fault was this?"

The SEAL who fired says it was his. The radioman from the sniper element says it was his. Willink rejects both.

> There is only one person to blame for this: me.

He had not been with the sniper team and was not directing the Iraqi element, but the task unit was his. He apologizes to the wounded SEAL and tells him he is lucky he was not killed. Then the whole team walks through the operation piece by piece. Had he pushed the blame onto someone else, Willink writes, he would have been fired; instead, the trust his commanding officer and master chief placed in him went up.

[[lessons]]
$$**Name your share first, and the conversation moves from "who?" to "what?"** When a repair shop fits the wrong part and the customer comes back, a manager who opens with "the apprentice misread it" gets defensiveness and a hunt for the guilty one. A manager who opens with "I didn't write the order slip clearly" lets everyone look at the slip, which means looking at the workflow. Owning your share does not shrink you, it sets the direction of the talk. It lets you search for the next mistake in the process instead of in a person, and it invites the team to search with you.$$

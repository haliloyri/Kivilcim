[[open]]
One man has just won a fortune in a state lottery. Another has been left paralysed by an accident. A researcher knocks on both doors with the same plain question about how happy they are, and the answers do not come out the way most people would bet.

[[story]]
In the late 1970s three psychologists, Philip Brickman, Dan Coates and Ronnie Janoff-Bulman, put a question to a group of people: is happiness relative? They compared Illinois lottery winners, whose prizes ranged from 50,000 to one million dollars, with people who had been paralysed in accidents, and with a control group that had neither won nor been hurt. The paper appeared in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology in 1978.

What they found surprised many readers. Winners rated their present happiness about the same as the controls did. The accident victims rated themselves lower than the controls, but the gap was smaller than anyone would have guessed. According to figures reported in later commentary, on a five-point scale the winners averaged about 4.0, the controls about 3.8 and the victims about 3.0. The authors read this as adaptation: a big change lifts or lowers mood for a while, and then people drift back toward their own usual level.

The Art of Happiness covers the same ground. Howard Cutler and the Dalai Lama discuss how people return to a baseline after events good and bad, and the Dalai Lama's view is that happiness depends more on the state of one's mind than on external events. The 1978 study is one of the best-known tests of that idea.

It deserves a fair caveat. The study compared different people at one moment instead of following the same people before and after, and the victims were interviewed within a year of their accident. Later reviews have called the lottery difference small enough to be a fluke, and newer work has found that big winners are somewhat happier than others and that disability does lower well-being. The finding that survives is narrower than the slogan. People adapt to a surprising degree, and they tend to predict their own adaptation badly.

That last point is the useful one for anyone who wants a bigger prize, a better job or a new house. The first weeks after a change are a poor guide to the years that follow it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Plan for getting used to a change before you chase it.** If you picture a promotion, a move or a new car, you tend to picture the first week of delight and to assume it will last. A teacher who dreams of a quieter school should expect that after a few months the quiet will be ordinary, and that something else will take the place of the old complaint. Knowing this lets you choose goals for what they will do on an ordinary Tuesday, and lets you put effort into the small sources of satisfaction that keep recurring, since they are the ones that last.$$

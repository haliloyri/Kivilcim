[[open]]
Cardboard cut-outs of the DNA bases lie on a desk in Cambridge, and a young American keeps trying to pair each shape with its twin. Nothing fits, however he turns them. Across the office, a visiting chemist glances at the drawing the cut-outs were copied from and says it is only a guess.

[[story]]
James James Watson's 1968 memoir *The Double Helix* tells the end of the chase. By early 1953 he and Francis Crick had a problem with the bases, the four chemical building blocks that make up the rungs of DNA. Watson had been trying to pair like with like, each base with a copy of itself, and the shapes would not line up.

The drawing he used for guanine came from a monograph by J. N. Davidson, and it showed the molecule in what chemists call the enol form. Jerry Donohue, an American chemist who had come to Cambridge on a Guggenheim grant for six months in 1952, shared an office with Crick and Watson. He told Watson that the enol drawing in the textbook was only a guess. The keto form, he said, was more likely. He based this on a paper by June Broomhead and on quantum mechanical calculations.

Published accounts say that within a few days Watson and Crick built their model of DNA. Watson, who had been playing with cut-out shapes of the bases, could now shape them as Donohue described. The form matters because it decides which atoms can hold on to each other across the gap between two bases. Paired that way, the bases fitted in a different arrangement: adenine with thymine, guanine with cytosine. One biography of Crick dates the discovery to 28 February 1953 and says the model was finished about 7 March.

Donohue's advice stayed in the record. The Watson and Crick paper that appeared in *Nature* on 25 April 1953 thanks him for "constant advice and criticism, especially on interatomic distances."

Look at where the error came from. It was not carelessness with the cardboard, and it was not a poor idea about pairing. A printed drawing in a standard book had been taken as a fact, and every attempt built on it failed in the same way, which is what a bad starting point looks like from the inside: many different tries, one repeated result. The person who found the problem was not the cleverest in the room at model building. He was the one who knew which parts of the drawing had been measured and which were an educated guess.

[[lessons]]
$$**Find out which of your inputs were measured and which were merely printed.** A figure or diagram in a trusted source can be a guess that has been repeated so often it looks like a fact. Say you are a café owner planning staffing from an industry rule of thumb you once read; check whether that rule came from counting real customers or from someone's estimate. Asking the person whose daily work covers the detail costs a short conversation, and when every attempt fails in the same way, even with a source you like, look at the starting assumptions before you try another variation.$$

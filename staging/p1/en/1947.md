[[open]]
On a golf course in Newton, Massachusetts, an eleven-year-old boy carries a heavy golf bag while the men walking the fairway ahead of him talk about stocks. He listens. Afterwards he finds those companies in the newspaper and starts following their prices, one after another.

[[story]]
Peter Lynch, whose *One Up on Wall Street* argues that an ordinary person can learn to pick stocks, started exactly this way. He told PBS's *Frontline* that he began caddying at eleven, and that the golfers he carried for were corporate executives who talked about stocks as they walked. Fortune later named the club as Brae Burn Country Club in Newton. Lynch took what he overheard and watched those stocks in the paper.

The job paid, too. "It was better than a paper route, and much more lucrative," he told Fortune. He added a question of his own: where else, at fifteen or sixteen, can you serve as a trusted adviser to high-powered people? Caddie scholarships carried him through Boston College, and he said the Francis Ouimet Fund was the main reason he could go on to Wharton with no student debt.

In those years he also put real money into a stock for the first time. He told *Frontline* it was a company called Flying Tiger, an air-cargo airline, and that he "actually put a thousand dollars in it." After the Vietnam War started, the stock rose, and it gave him, in his words, his first ten-bagger, which is Lynch's term for a stock that gains tenfold.

The golf bag opened one more door. One of the men Lynch had carried for was George Sullivan, the president of Fidelity's funds. In 1966, when Lynch went after a summer job at Fidelity, about 75 people were competing for three openings. He later said that he was the only one who had caddied for the president for years.

Fidelity took him on that summer. In 1977 he became head of its Magellan fund.

Look at what the caddie job gave him apart from the pay. It put him within earshot of people whose work was investing in companies, years before anyone paid him to think about stocks. It gave him a habit of checking talk against prices in the newspaper. It gave him a first ten-bagger, bought with his own thousand dollars, so he had seen a tenfold stock from the inside long before he managed a fund. None of that came from a classroom. All of it came from standing close to the game and paying attention.

[[lessons]]
$$**Stand close to the work you want to learn, and pay attention to how people talk about it.** A teenager who stocks shelves at a hardware shop can listen to which questions the contractors ask and which products they pick, and over time that teaches more than any catalogue. Proximity only helps if you notice what you hear and test it against something real, as Lynch did with newspaper prices. Looking for the place where the people you admire talk shop is a smaller step than looking for a mentor, and you can take it this month, starting with a single visit.$$

Two physicists sit down to work out what a few pounds of a rare metal could do, and the arithmetic comes out wrong for their comfort. It comes out small, and it comes out terrifying. They write it down in plain language, then add a sentence about what they are not sure of.

[[story]]
Richard Rhodes's *The Making of the Atomic Bomb* follows the bomb through several countries, and one stop was Britain. In 1940 two physicists, Otto Frisch and Rudolf Peierls, wrote a short memorandum there. According to the Atomic Heritage Foundation's presentation of it, the memo laid out what a "super-bomb" based on nuclear energy would be.

Its core claim was about material. Natural uranium contains only about 0.7 percent of the light isotope uranium-235. In its argument, a bomb would need that isotope separated out. Separation, the authors wrote, was difficult but could be done, and the cost was within reach.

Then came the size. The authors gave the critical size as about one pound. Below that amount, separated material would be safe, so the bomb would be built in two or more sub-critical parts that join when detonation is intended. The energy released, they wrote, would be about that of 1,000 tons of dynamite, in a small volume.

The memo did not hide what that meant. The blast would destroy life over a wide area, probably the centre of a large city. The weapon would also produce radioactive substances that stay deadly for days, and wind could carry the contamination for several miles. The authors judged the bomb nearly irresistible as a weapon, yet thought that the fallout would likely kill many civilians, which might make it unsuitable for Britain to use.

This was a plan of action as much as a warning. Their strategic argument was blunt. Germany might be developing such a weapon, so the best defence would be a counter-threat with a similar bomb. They urged starting production quickly, because separation would take months.

Their handling of their own doubt matters most. The critical size, they noted, rested on theory and had not been confirmed. They left that in plain view, and they pressed on anyway. They said what would test it: separating a small amount of uranium.

The result is a document that does three things at once. It states a conclusion plainly, it marks where the evidence is thin, and it names the experiment that would thicken it. A reader could act on it without being misled about how firm the ground was.

[[lessons]]
$$**When you hand someone a conclusion, mark which part is theory and name the test that would confirm it.** A claim that hides its weak point sounds overconfident, and a weak point offered with no next step sounds like an excuse. In a project update you might say that the forecast depends on one assumption and propose the cheapest check that would settle it this month. That keeps your credibility intact if you turn out to be wrong and gives the reader something to do rather than only something to believe. It also lets the next person in the chain decide how much weight to put on your number.$$

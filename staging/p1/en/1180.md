Before she starts any new book, Elizabeth Gilbert has a conversation with an old travel companion. It isn't a friend or an editor. It's her fear. And she talks to it as if it were about to climb into the back seat of her car.

[[story]]
In *Big Magic*, Elizabeth Gilbert is frank about something many creative people hide: every time she begins a new project, she's afraid. Afraid it won't be good. Afraid people will criticize it. Afraid it has all been done before. Afraid of looking foolish. Years of success haven't made this go away.

She doesn't think it ever will. And she doesn't think it should, entirely. Fear, as she describes it, is an ancient survival instinct. It kept our ancestors alive. It's very good at its job. But its job is narrow. Fear has essentially one message, and it repeats it over and over: *stop*. Don't go there. Don't risk it.

Creativity, meanwhile, always involves uncertainty. You never know how a project will turn out. So fear is triggered every single time.

For years, many people try to defeat their fear before they begin, waiting to feel brave. Gilbert takes a different approach. She accepts that fear is coming with her, and she sets the terms.

Before a new project, she speaks to it directly, almost like a letter. She tells fear that she and creativity are about to go on a road trip together. She knows fear will want to come along, because it always does. So it's welcome. It can have a seat in the car. It can have a voice; it's allowed to say what it thinks.

##But fear is not allowed to drive.##

It doesn't get to touch the road maps. It doesn't get to suggest detours. It doesn't even get to fiddle with the temperature. It rides along, and creativity and Gilbert make the decisions.

She has equally clear views on fear's favorite disguise: perfectionism. Many people see perfectionism as high standards, even a virtue. Gilbert disagrees. She calls it fear in fancy shoes and a mink coat, dressing itself up to look elegant. Underneath, it's the same fear, keeping you from ever finishing or sharing your work.

Her antidote comes from her mother, who liked to say: *done is better than good.* A finished, imperfect piece can be shared, learned from and improved on. A perfect one that never leaves your desk can't do anything at all.

[[lessons]]
$$**Fear comes along; you decide who drives.** Gilbert doesn't wait for fear to leave before she starts. She accepts it as a passenger and gives it a voice but not the wheel. That's a realistic goal. You can't stop feeling fear, but you can refuse to let it choose where you go.$$

$$**Perfectionism is fear in disguise.** "Fear in fancy shoes and a mink coat" is a memorable way of saying that high standards can hide avoidance. When you keep polishing and never finishing, it's worth asking whether it's really quality you're protecting, or yourself from judgment.$$

$$**Done is better than good.** Gilbert's mother's motto moves the goal from perfect to finished. Finished work can be shared, criticized and improved. Unfinished work does nothing. Getting to done, even imperfectly, is often the braver and far more useful choice for everyone involved.$$

[[reflect]]
- What exactly does your fear say when you're about to start something new?
- Where is perfectionism keeping one of your projects unfinished?
- What's one thing you could call "done" this week, even if it isn't perfect?

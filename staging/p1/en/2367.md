[[open]]
In a hospital hallway, a father tells his young daughter to be strong. She has a meltdown, and the family leaves without the scan they came for. The man who designed the machine has come to watch it in use, and he sees all of it happen.

[[story]]
Doug Dietz was an industrial designer at GE Healthcare, and by the time a Milwaukee magazine profiled him in late 2013 he had been designing the company's products for 24 years. One of them was a new, high-tech MRI scanner. He went to a hospital to watch it in use, and in the hallway he saw the family described above. Dietz later said: "I see this family unable to get their scan, and I felt like I had failed at my job."

The machine itself had done nothing wrong. What had failed was everything around it, and Dietz drew his conclusion in one sentence: "My job is to design the experience, not just the thing." That sentence moved the target of his work. A scanner can be perfect in engineering terms and still be a place a small child cannot enter.

He did not guess at a fix. According to the same profile, he gathered input from the Betty Brinn Children's Museum, from child life specialists, and from children themselves. The result was the GE Adventure Series. The scanner's equipment inside stays the same, but the room around it becomes a themed adventure. Themes include a space voyage, a cozy camp and a pirate ship.

The reason for the effort was practical. Dietz said that about 80 percent of children aged 3 to 8 needed sedation for scans. A child who has to be put to sleep before the procedure is a child for whom the room has already lost. Hospitals using the new designs reported a 7 percent increase in scans, the profile says. The profile also mentions a 6-year-old who completed a 40-minute PET/CT scan on her own in the camp-themed setting.

What stands out is where Dietz put his effort. He did not make the machine smaller or faster, and he did not write a better leaflet for parents. He changed the story the child walks into. A scan stops being a procedure to endure and becomes part of a game the child is already inside.

His starting point also deserves attention. He found the problem by going to the hallway and watching, not by reading a report from a desk. A designer who feels he has failed in front of a real family has a much clearer brief than one holding a satisfaction score.

[[lessons]]
$$**Judge your work by the experience of the person at the other end, and go and watch them use it.** A product that works perfectly can still fail the people it was built for, and the gap only shows when you stand where they stand. Whether you build software, run a clinic or plan a family trip, spend an hour watching someone go through the part you consider finished. Then change the story around the thing, not only the thing itself, and you may find that the real problem was never in the part you were polishing.$$

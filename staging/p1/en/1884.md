Forty adults gather around a radio in a community that has lived through genocide. The programme they hear is a soap opera, with characters, quarrels and a love story. Nobody tells them what to believe. A year later, researchers find that something has shifted in what these listeners think is normal.

[[story]]
In the years after the genocide in Rwanda, the psychologist Elizabeth Levy Paluck ran an unusual field experiment. It lasted a full year and took place in 14 sites. At each site, about 40 adults formed listening groups that heard a radio programme together.

Some groups heard a soap opera about reconciliation, a drama about reconciliation between divided communities. Other groups heard a different soap opera, one about health, and it was the control against which the first was judged. It was a clean design for a messy setting. The researchers did not have to guess what listening did; they could compare. Because the format was the same, in principle any difference between the groups could be put down to the message and not to the pleasure of listening to a drama.

What Paluck measured was not only what people said they believed. She also looked at what listeners thought others around them considered normal, and at how they acted. The reconciliation drama shifted those perceived norms. People in the groups that heard it came to see open dissent as more acceptable, along with trust between neighbours and marriage across the old divisions. Their behaviour moved with those perceptions.

Then comes the surprising part. Personal beliefs barely moved. Listeners did not turn into different people with different convictions about the past. What changed was their sense of what everybody around them accepted, and that sense changed what they did in public.

This is the logic worth noticing. A person who privately thinks something can still stay silent for years if they believe nobody else thinks it. When a story shows that others also want it, the silence becomes optional. The soap opera did not argue anyone out of an opinion. It changed the picture of the room each listener was sitting in.

One limit should be stated plainly: how long the changes lasted is unknown. The experiment shows that norms can be moved within a year. It does not show that they stay moved, and anyone borrowing the idea should keep that gap in view. A norm that shifted once could drift back.

[[lessons]]
$$**To change what people do, change what they believe other people think.** Many habits survive because everyone assumes everyone else expects them, even when each person privately disagrees and would be relieved to drop them. In a team that never raises problems in meetings, one visible example of a colleague raising a problem calmly and being thanked can change the unspoken rule faster than a memo can. Look for the belief about others that holds the behaviour up, then make a real counterexample easy to see. That route can work where a direct argument about private opinions gets nowhere.$$

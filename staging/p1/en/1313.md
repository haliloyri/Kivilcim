Ray Kroc looked at a hamburger stand and did not see a menu. He saw a method, a way of running the place that could be copied from one location to the next. Michael Gerber thinks that shift in what Kroc was looking at holds a lesson for every small business, including ones that will never sell a single burger.

[[story]]
In *The E-Myth Revisited*, Michael E. Gerber makes Ray Kroc and McDonald's his central example of what he calls the Turn-Key Revolution.

Most people would assume a successful restaurant rests on exceptional food. Gerber points somewhere else. What Kroc understood was that the product was not only the hamburger. The product was the business itself: a way of operating that could be repeated, location after location, and still work.

Gerber gives this model a name, the Business Format Franchise. Here the franchisor provides a complete, proven system for running the business, so the person who takes it on is not left to invent everything from scratch.

##The real product was not the hamburger but the way the business ran.##

Then Gerber turns the example toward ordinary owners. Most will never franchise anything. He tells them to work on their business as if it were the prototype for thousands more anyway. Build it as though someone, somewhere, will need to run an exact copy of it.

That instruction forces a certain kind of clarity. If a business is going to be replicated, nothing can live only in the owner's head. Every step has to be explicit enough for someone else to follow.

One of his rules for the franchise prototype sounds almost backwards at first. The model should be operable by people with the lowest possible level of skill. That is not a comment on people. It is a design goal. If results depend on rare talent, they are only as reliable as that talent's availability. If the system itself carries the know-how, ordinary skill can produce reliable results.

Underneath all of this sits a simple test. If the business only works when the owner is present, the owner has not built a business. They have built a job for themselves.

Kroc's insight, as Gerber tells it, was to stop thinking about the thing being sold and start designing the way it was delivered, so that it would work the same way no matter who stood behind the counter.

[[lessons]]
$$**The system can matter more than the product.** Kroc's Business Format Franchise treated the whole way of operating as the thing being offered. The hamburger was part of it, but the repeatable method is what Gerber wants owners to notice. Looking at your own work, the "product" may include how it gets made, not just what comes out.$$

$$**Design as if you will be copied.** Gerber's advice to treat your business as a franchise prototype works even if you never franchise. Imagining someone else running an exact copy forces you to write down what you currently do by instinct. The act of making it explicit is where the value starts.$$

$$**Aim for ordinary skill, reliable results.** The rule that the model should run with the lowest possible level of skill is a test of the design, not a judgment of people. If only your best person can make it work, the system is holding nothing. If a newcomer can follow it, the know-how has moved into the process.$$

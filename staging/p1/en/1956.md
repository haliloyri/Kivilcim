[[story]]
Neville Chamberlain met Adolf Hitler for the first time at Berchtesgaden on 15 September 1938. Afterwards he wrote to his sister Ida, and historians quote one line from the letter: here was a man who could be relied upon when he had given his word. It was an impression, formed in one meeting, about a man he had never dealt with before. Malcolm Gladwell's *Talking to Strangers* discusses Chamberlain's reading of Hitler.

The crisis was over the Sudetenland, the border region of Czechoslovakia that Hitler demanded. Chamberlain travelled to Germany again, arriving at Godesberg on 22 September and meeting Hitler the next day. Then, on 29 September, Hitler hosted Chamberlain, Mussolini and the French prime minister Daladier at Munich. No Czech representative was in the room, and Chamberlain had already accepted Hitler's demand for the Sudetenland, so the talks were about how and when Germany would take it.

Early on 30 September, Chamberlain and Daladier signed the Munich Agreement. Chamberlain also persuaded Hitler to sign a separate one-page statement. It called the agreement symbolic of the desire of the two peoples never to go to war with one another again. The National WWII Museum quotes Hitler as saying afterwards that the piece of paper was of no significance whatsoever.

Back in London on 30 September, Chamberlain was greeted by a cheering crowd on the airport tarmac and read from the joint statement. Later that day, outside 10 Downing Street, he said: "I believe it is peace for our time." Not everyone shared his view, and many doubted that war had been avoided by a policy that had left Czechoslovakia out of the talks altogether.

Less than six months later, on 15 March 1939, German forces seized all of Czechoslovakia. The paper had lasted a few months.

What deserves attention is the kind of evidence Chamberlain relied on. He had a handshake, a conversation, a signature and his own sense of the man, and all of it came from inside a few meetings. The things he could have set beside it, such as the Czech view or what Hitler had already done, were not part of the room. A meeting produces vivid information, but the vividness is about the meeting itself, and it says little about what the other person will do once they are home again.

[[lessons]]
$$**Weigh what a person has done at least as heavily as how the meeting felt.** A face-to-face impression is vivid, so it tends to crowd out slower evidence such as a record of kept and broken promises. Suppose a new landlord is warm and reassuring when you view the flat: the warmth tells you about that afternoon, while the previous tenants' experience tells you about the next two years. Before you act on a good feeling, write down two or three things the person has actually done, and let those sit next to the impression. If the two disagree, you have found the question worth asking before you commit.$$

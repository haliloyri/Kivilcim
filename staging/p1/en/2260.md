A board of directors meets by telephone to approve a stock split, more shares for the pay plan, a new corporate jet, a power plant in the Middle East, and one more item. The item was unlike anything the company had done before. The whole call is over in an hour.

[[story]]
That call took place on 28 June 1999, and the extra item was a company called LJM1. Bethany McLean and Peter Elkind chronicle Enron's collapse in *The Smartest Guys in the Room*, and the LJM partnerships of chief financial officer Andrew Fastow belong to that story. What follows comes from the report of a Senate subcommittee in 2002.

The directors were told that LJM1 would be a special purpose entity, kept off Enron's balance sheet, owned in part and managed by Fastow himself. Approving it meant waiving the company's code of conduct for its own chief financial officer. Its first deal involved Rhythms NetConnections, a high-tech stock Enron had bought at the company's initial public offering for $10 million and whose value had climbed to about $300 million. Enron had already booked that rise as earnings, and it wanted to protect them in case the price fell.

LJM1 could provide a "hedge" on the stock, the board heard, "at no cost to Enron." Better still, LJM1 would pay Enron $50 million in cash, which the board was reminded "counts as funds flow." The timing mattered too. Dr. Jaedicke, who had headed the Audit Committee, later told the Senate subcommittee the company "did not want to be in the position of having a fair value investment, a stock on their books, a mark-to-market [asset], without a hedge." The quarter closed on 30 June, two days after the call.

The process was thin. The proposal never went to the Finance Committee, contrary to the board's usual practice. Directors received written materials by fax three days before the meeting. The minutes show the board handled it alongside the stock split, the stock plan, the jet and the power plant, and the meeting lasted one hour. An outside accounting firm's fairness opinion, which some directors later pointed to, was delivered two months after the deal had taken effect.

The hedge itself did not behave like one. When Enron unwound the Rhythms transaction, the subcommittee found, Enron paid LJM1 a $30 million termination fee, even though the hedge should have resulted in LJM1 paying Enron millions of dollars.

##The unusual item sat in the middle of an ordinary agenda.## The minutes list it alongside the jet and the stock split, with nothing to set it apart.

[[lessons]]
$$**Give the unusual item its own slot on the agenda.** A volunteer committee that approves an odd request from its treasurer between choosing a picnic date and ordering new chairs will usually vote the way the last three items went. Decisions come in batches, and a batch lends its pace to everything in it, including the one thing that has never come up before. Before a meeting, mark the single proposal nobody has seen in this form, give it a separate session and send its papers days ahead, so that the people deciding have time to ask the questions the routine items never invite.$$

A visitor sits down in a hospital across from one of the most gifted mathematicians of his generation. He asks a plain question: how can a man so devoted to reason believe that beings from space are recruiting him? The answer he gets is short, and it follows its own strict logic.

[[story]]
The mathematician is John Nash, and the story comes from Sylvia Nasar's biography *A Beautiful Mind*, which Morgan Housel draws on in *Same as Ever* when he writes about how human beings come to believe things. In the spring of 1959 Nash's wife, Alicia, had him committed to a hospital. He was convinced that extraterrestrial or supernatural beings were recruiting him.

A Harvard mathematician, George Mackey, came to visit. In the accounts of that meeting he asks the obvious thing: how could someone who put such weight on logic and proof take the idea of aliens seriously?

Nash's reply, in Nasar's telling, was that his ideas about supernatural beings had come to him in the same way as his mathematical ideas. So he took them seriously. Sources differ slightly on the exact words, but the content is consistent.

Read that answer slowly, because it is not stupid. For a mathematician, a certain kind of thought arrives suddenly and feels right, and later it proves to be right. That is the experience that makes a career, and it is a reasonable thing to rely on most of the time. Nash had learned, over years, to trust the feeling that comes with a good idea.

The trouble is that the feeling does not check itself. In mathematics there is an external judge, the proof, which accepts or rejects the idea whatever its owner feels. A belief about beings who are recruiting you has no such judge unless someone from outside supplies one. Mackey's question was an attempt to do exactly that, to ask where the idea came from and what gave it authority.

Housel's use of the episode is broader than one man's illness. Every one of us has convictions that arrived with a strong sense of rightness, whether about a colleague's motives, a stock, a diet or a political side. How sure we feel is information about our minds. It is not information about the world.

The story does not claim that Nash was foolish, and it would be a misreading to take it that way. The point lies in how ordinary the mechanism is, and how little the conviction depends on how intelligent the person holding it happens to be.

[[lessons]]
$$**Treat a strong feeling of certainty as a claim that still needs a witness from outside.** When an idea arrives with force, whether it is a hiring decision, a suspicion about a partner or a plan to quit, the force tells you something about your state of mind, but nothing yet about the facts. Before you act, ask what test the idea could fail and who could run it, preferably someone who will not simply agree with you. A good check is cheap compared with acting on a wrong certainty, and the people who love you will often be willing to do it if you ask them directly.$$

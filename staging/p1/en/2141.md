A laboratory room smells of freshly baked chocolate cookies. On the table sit a plate of cookies and, beside it, a bowl of radishes. One student is told that the radishes are hers and the cookies are not, and she is left alone with both.

[[story]]
Roy Baumeister, one of the two authors of *Willpower*, ran this experiment with three colleagues and published it in 1998. The students had come to the lab hungry, according to published accounts, and were left alone for about five minutes with the warm cookies and a bowl of radishes. Some were allowed to eat the cookies and chocolates. Some were told to eat only radishes. A third group skipped the food part altogether.

Then everybody got a puzzle that could not be solved. The researchers timed how long each person kept trying. The radish eaters quit much sooner than the others, around eight minutes against roughly nineteen for the cookie eaters, if published accounts are right.

Baumeister's reading was bold. Resisting a cookie had cost these students something, and that something was also needed for the puzzle. He called it ego depletion and proposed that choosing, holding back emotion and resisting temptation all draw on one limited supply. Further experiments in the same paper, with personal choices, suppressed emotions and a task that left people passive, pointed the same way.

The idea spread through psychology and later through popular books, including the one Baumeister wrote with John Tierney. Then came the test. In 2016 a team ran one standardised depletion procedure in 23 laboratories with 2,141 participants in total. The effect they measured was d = 0.04, with a confidence interval running from -0.07 to 0.15, so zero sat comfortably inside it.

Baumeister and his colleague Kathleen Vohs answered that the replication used a different procedure from the original, so it could not speak for the original claim. A larger project with 36 laboratories and 3,531 participants, reported in 2021, again found almost nothing, according to published summaries.

The radish experiment is therefore a real result from one room and a doubtful law of the mind. The students probably did quit sooner, and a puzzle with no solution at all is a harsh test of anyone's patience after sitting beside forbidden chocolate. Whether a shared reservoir of willpower explains that, and whether it applies to office workers, dieters and drivers, is the part that has not held up, and it is the part that most popular retellings kept.

[[lessons]]
$$**Treat a vivid experiment as a question to be tested again.** A study can be well run, published in a top journal and still describe something that does not repeat under the same conditions elsewhere. Say a manager reads an exciting finding and wants to reorganise the whole team around it; trying it with one small group first costs little, and it shows whether the finding survives a different office. The habit is to ask, before you act on a result, who has repeated it and what they found. Over time you will notice how many confident claims rest on one room full of students.$$

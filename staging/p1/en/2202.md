A reporter at a White House press conference asks about a worry that has been growing among scientists. The President answers in a few seconds, and the answer is short enough to fit on a card. Within months, the question inside it has a committee, a report and a Senate hearing room.

[[story]]
On 29 August 1962, a reporter asked President John F. Kennedy about growing concern among scientists over possible long-range side effects from widespread use of DDT and other pesticides. Had he considered asking the Department of Agriculture or the Public Health Service to take a closer look? Kennedy answered: "Yes, and I know that they already are." He added that this was particularly so "since Miss Carson's book," and that "they are examining the matter."

That was one answer in one press conference, but it moved the subject of the book from magazine pages onto the government's agenda. The serialisation in The New Yorker had run that summer, the book itself was a month away, and the chemical industry's critics had been loud. Now the President had said in public that the government was looking into the subject.

In the spring of 1963 two things landed within six weeks of each other. On 3 April CBS Reports aired an hour-long programme, "The Silent Spring of Rachel Carson." Cancer treatment was sapping Carson's strength, yet she came across as calm and reasonable. Opposite her sat Robert White-Stevens, spokesman for the agricultural chemical industry, in a white lab coat. He called the book's major claims "gross distortions of the actual facts," and viewers could compare the two manners for themselves.

The next day, 4 April, Senator Abraham Ribicoff of Connecticut announced hearings on pollution, including federal pesticide regulation. On 15 May 1963 the report of the Life Sciences Panel of the President's Science Advisory Committee, the group Kennedy had asked to look into Carson's claims, was released with his clear approval. According to the Environment and Society Portal, the panel largely confirmed the book's main claims. The hearings opened the very next day.

On 4 June Carson herself testified. Ribicoff greeted her with the words: "You are the lady who started all this."

Think about the sequence. Carson did not prove her case by arguing louder than her critics. The people who tested it stood outside the quarrel: a presidential science panel and a Senate hearing led by a senator from Connecticut. Their findings carried weight because they had not been part of the exchange of attacks.

[[lessons]]
$$**When your claim is challenged, ask for a check by people who have nothing riding on the result.** Defending your own work harder only adds one more voice to the argument, while a neutral review gives the audience a verdict they did not hear from either side. In practice this means inviting a colleague from another team to review your analysis, or asking an outside auditor to test your numbers, before the dispute hardens. If the review confirms you, the point is settled. If it does not, you have learned that before it cost more.$$

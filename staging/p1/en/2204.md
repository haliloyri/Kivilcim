[[open]]
A young American geneticist sits through a talk in Naples. He studies viruses, and he has privately feared that DNA is too irregular a molecule for anyone to decode. Then the speaker shows X-ray pictures of DNA, and the pictures are covered in sharp, well-defined spots.

[[story]]
James Watson's *The Double Helix* is his 1968 account of how he and Francis Crick worked out the structure of DNA. One of the turning points came at a scientific meeting in Naples in 1951. Watson was then a young American who had been working on bacteriophages, the viruses that infect bacteria, and he was based in Copenhagen.

Maurice Wilkins of King's College London spoke at that meeting about his group's X-ray work on DNA. According to published accounts, he and his student Raymond Gosling had produced diffraction photographs with dozens of well-defined spots on a clear background. Those photographs showed, for the first time, that DNA could be truly crystalline.

That mattered to Watson because of a fear he had carried. He had worried that DNA might be an irregular structure, hard or even impossible to decipher. A crystal is the opposite of irregular: its pattern repeats, and a repeating pattern can in principle be worked out. The spots on Wilkins's pictures were the visible sign of that repetition. X-rays bounce off a regular array of atoms and reinforce one another at particular angles, which leaves sharp dots on the film, while a jumble of atoms would leave only a smear. Sharp dots therefore carried a plain message: this molecule has order, and the order can be read.

Watson changed direction. He turned from phage genetics toward the structural chemistry of nucleic acids and proteins. In the autumn of 1951 he arrived at the Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge, where he began working with Crick.

The photographs did not give him the structure. That would take nearly two more years, a failed first model and a great deal of other people's data. What the pictures changed was the odds. A problem he had filed as probably hopeless became one worth giving his career to, and he moved cities and laboratories to work on it.

Notice how small the trigger was, and how much of the work still lay ahead. Nobody at the meeting solved anything. One speaker showed one kind of evidence, and one listener, who happened to be carrying exactly the doubt that evidence answered, heard it as an invitation.

[[lessons]]
$$**Test the "impossible" label again whenever new evidence turns up.** A problem you wrote off months ago may look different after one new fact, and the label you gave it may have been a guess about the evidence rather than a finding. Suppose a small shop owner decided long ago that online orders would never suit a business like hers, and then she sees a neighbouring shop's order book at a trade meeting. That is the moment to reopen the question and price the work properly, instead of quietly keeping the old verdict. Reviewing your own verdicts costs an afternoon, while a wrong verdict can cost years.$$

[[open]]
A small room, a camera on a stand, two chairs. A newly married couple has been asked to talk for fifteen minutes about something they keep disagreeing on, and the tape starts rolling. Whatever is said in the first sentence has already begun to shape the rest of the quarter hour.

[[story]]
In the late 1990s, the psychologists John Gottman and Sybil Carrère published a study of 124 newlywed couples, each married for less than nine months. Every couple was videotaped discussing an ongoing area of conflict in their marriage, and then Gottman and Carrère followed them for six years. At the end of that period, 17 of the couples had divorced.

The question was how much of that ending could be seen in the tape, and the answer was surprising. The researchers coded only the first three minutes of the fifteen, scoring the emotion in each partner's words, faces and gestures. The marriages that later ended had shown more negative emotion in those three minutes and less positive emotion than the marriages that lasted.

According to the University of Washington's news release at the time, the shape of the opening mattered. In the couples heading for divorce, the wife's first words were often a global criticism of her husband rather than a complaint about one thing, and the husband tended to answer defensively. Gottman said the biggest lesson of the study was how a couple begins a discussion about a problem.

Seventeen divorces is a small number, and these were newlyweds in one study, so the result is no law of marriage. It is better read as a picture of how momentum works in a hard conversation. A complaint that opens as "you always" or "you never" asks the other person to defend their whole character, and a person defending their character cannot also be curious about the problem.

Douglas Stone, Bruce Patton and Sheila Heen make a related point in *Difficult Conversations*. They advise opening from a "third story," a description of the problem that both people could accept as fair, rather than from your own story of who did what to whom. The tapes suggest how early the tone of a conversation is set, long before the point at which anyone would say it had gone wrong.

The practical point is modest. You may not be able to control the rest of a hard conversation, but the first sentence is entirely yours, and the study suggests it carries more weight than its length would imply.

[[lessons]]
$$**Open a hard conversation by naming one specific event.** A sentence like "The report went out without the numbers I asked for" gives the listener a problem to look at, while "You never listen" gives them a character to defend. In a repair shop, a classroom or a kitchen the pattern is the same: the way you begin sets the temperature for everything the other person says next. Write your first sentence before you speak, check that it names an event instead of a flaw, and say it in a tone you would accept if you were on the receiving end.$$

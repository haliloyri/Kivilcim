On a Thursday evening in Reno, Nevada, a man holds a telephone to his ear. At the other end the president and the lawyer of a Wall Street firm in which he holds a stake are describing a problem. He does not grasp, that night, how serious it is. A few days later he will tell a cabinet member that he is living one of the most important days of his life.

[[story]]
Start with the rule. In 1990 the Treasury decided that no single firm could bid for more than 35 percent of the securities offered at an auction. According to Fortune, Salomon Brothers' trader Paul Mozer pushed against that limit. In December 1990 and again in February 1991 he bid up to the cap, placed unauthorized bids in customers' names, and moved the securities those bids won into Salomon's own account without telling the customers. Salomon ended up holding more than 35 percent of the securities in those auctions.

The real damage came afterward. In late April Mozer told John Meriwether about the February violations. Meriwether told Tom Strauss, and Strauss, John Gutfreund and Donald Feuerstein met to discuss it. Fortune says they concluded that the New York Fed had to be told. Yet nobody informed the regulators in April, May, June or July. About three and a half months passed before the disclosure on August 8.

On Thursday, August 8, Warren Buffett was in Reno. Salomon's president and its lawyer briefed him by phone, and Buffett did not grasp the seriousness that night. The next day, Friday, Gutfreund and Strauss resigned. Buffett agreed to serve as interim chairman, and the firm announced it that afternoon. Meriwether resigned the following day on his own initiative.

On Sunday, August 18, the Treasury barred Salomon from bidding in auctions, both for itself and for customers. Fortune reports that Buffett told Treasury Secretary Nicholas Brady: "Nick, this is the most important day of my life." Jerome Powell, an assistant secretary at the Treasury, read Buffett the revised statement and asked, "Will that do?" Buffett answered, "I think it will." Around 2:30 in the afternoon the ban on the firm's own account was lifted, while the customer ban stayed.

In the end Salomon settled for $290 million, and Mozer was sentenced in December 1993 to four months in a minimum-security prison. The timing is plain: the violation was in February, the disclosure in August. In between lay months in which nobody acted wrongly alone, yet everybody waited for someone else to speak.

What deserves a closer look is the silence. Four people knew what had to be done, because they had even reached a decision. Every day a little more reasoning was added to the gap between knowing and saying, and in the end that gap cost the firm's leaders their jobs.

[[lessons]]
$$**Report bad news on the day you learn it.** A problem does not shrink with time; each quiet week adds a new justification and a new person who knew, and a mistake slowly becomes a crisis of trust. At work that means telling your manager or your customer about a broken delivery, a missed date or a wrong number the same day. A bad piece of news told first is usually a fixable error, and every day of waiting turns it into a secret that someone later has to explain, and the person who speaks first keeps the chance to tell the story in their own words.$$

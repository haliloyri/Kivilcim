Something happens in front of you that you don't want to forget. Before you have really decided anything, your phone is already in your hand and the camera is open. Nobody sent you a notification. Nir Eyal argues that this reflex was not an accident, and that the pull of a photo app has less to do with filters than with a feeling you brought with you.

[[story]]
In *Hooked: How to Build Habit-Forming Products*, published in 2014, Nir Eyal lays out what he calls the Hook Model. It has four phases: a Trigger, an Action, a Variable Reward, and an Investment.

The trigger is what prompts the behavior. Some triggers are external: a notification, an ad, a message from a friend. Others are internal. They are feelings or situations the user already has, with nothing on the screen required.

Eyal uses Instagram to show what an internal trigger looks like. The trigger, as he describes it, is the fear of losing a special moment. You see something worth keeping, and that small worry is what sends you to take the photo and share it.

##The hook starts with a feeling you already have, not with anything the app sends you.##

Next comes the Action, the simplest behavior a person does in anticipation of a reward. Eyal stresses that designers should reduce effort here. The easier the action, the more likely it happens. Open, point, tap.

Then the Variable Reward. What keeps people coming back, Eyal argues, is not a reward they can predict. It is not knowing what responses or content will appear. That uncertainty draws people back more than a predictable payoff would.

The last phase is Investment. The user puts something into the product: content, followers, preferences. Each of these makes the product more valuable to that person. And each one loads the next trigger, giving them a reason to return.

Eyal presents the hook as a cycle, not a single event. Each pass through it, trigger to action to reward to investment, strengthens the link. Repeated enough times, the product becomes a habit.

Seen this way, the strength of an app like Instagram is not only in its features. It sits in the connection between a feeling the user already carries and an action that answers it quickly, with a reward that is never quite the same twice.

[[lessons]]
$$**The strongest triggers are already inside the user.** Eyal ties Instagram to the fear of losing a special moment, a feeling that exists before any app is opened. A notification has to be sent every time. A feeling arrives on its own. Products that answer an existing feeling do not need to keep knocking.$$

$$**Uncertainty keeps people checking.** Variable Rewards work because you do not know what will appear: which responses, which content. A fixed payoff can be predicted and set aside. An unpredictable one invites one more look. That is worth knowing both when you build something and when you notice yourself refreshing.$$

$$**What you put in pulls you back.** The Investment phase is easy to overlook. Content, followers, and preferences make the product more valuable to you personally, and they set up the next trigger. The more you have put into something, the more reason you have to return to it.$$

[[reflect]]
- Which app do you open before you have decided to open it?
- What feeling is usually there just before you reach for it?
- What have you put into that app, photos, followers, settings, that would be hard to leave behind?

&&The next time you reach for your favorite app, which part of the hook brought you there?&&

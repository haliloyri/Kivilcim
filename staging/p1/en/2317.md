A capsule hits the top of the atmosphere wrapped in glowing gas, and nothing it says can reach Earth. The silence has a schedule, a little over three minutes. At the console in Houston, one person has a single job while it lasts: be ready to say the capsule's name the moment the time is up.

[[story]]
For Apollo 13, the part of the flight that Jim Lovell and Jeffrey Kluger tell in *Lost Moon* ended on 17 April 1970 with the entry into the atmosphere. Every Apollo crew lost radio contact during entry, because the superheated air around the command module, Odyssey, blocked the signal. This time, after days in a damaged ship, the wait carried more weight, and nobody on the ground could do anything for the crew until the radio came back.

The Apollo Flight Journal, NASA's annotated transcript of the mission, shows how the wait was handled. Capcom Joe Kerwin had called "Odyssey, Houston" before the blackout, once at 142:36 into the mission and again at 142:38, and had passed up a welcome-home message. The blackout was expected to begin about 19 seconds after entry interface, the point where the capsule meets the atmosphere at 142:40 into the mission, and to end at 142:44:24.

That time came and went. At 142:46:03, well past the predicted end, Kerwin said: "Odyssey, Houston standing by. Over." Five seconds later Jack Swigert answered: "Okay, Joe." Kerwin replied: "Okay. We read you, Jack." The blackout had run roughly a minute and three quarters longer than planned.

Look at what Kerwin did with the extra silence. He did not add speculation or a new plan. He made the same plain call he had been making, and as soon as the line opened he gave an ordinary piece of information: the weather pictured on television "looks just as advertised; real good."

The rest went in order, each step reported as it happened. Swigert reported "We got two good drogues" at 142:49, Kerwin said the capsule was on the mains, and at 142:54:44 Kerwin told the crew, "We copied splashdown at 142 hours, 54 minutes, 44 seconds from liftoff." A helicopter crew reported that the three parachutes had fallen into the water.

The lesson sits in the gap. Where the plan had a silent period, the ground held to a fixed call and a fixed wait, and when contact returned it went straight back to routine business. A planned silence is not a blank, it is a stretch where the right action is already decided.

[[lessons]]
$$**When you cannot hear anything back, hold to a fixed call and a fixed time rather than filling the silence with guesses.** Kerwin's "standing by" took no new decision, which is why it was safe to repeat when the blackout ran past its predicted end. The call also keeps you steady when there is nothing to solve. You can use the same habit while waiting for a client's answer, a test result or a late relative. Decide beforehand when you will check, which plain sentence you will send, and what you will do once the reply arrives, so the waiting itself is part of the plan.$$

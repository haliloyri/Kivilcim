Two groups learn the same short finger sequence on a keyboard, typing it with the hand they don't normally use. They practise, they stop, and they walk away. Hours later they come back. One group is noticeably faster and more accurate, even though nobody touched a keyboard in between. The other group is exactly where it left off. What happened in those hours?

[[story]]
The experiment comes from Matthew Walker's lab, and he describes it in *Why We Sleep*. The volunteers were right-handed, and the task was to type a set sequence with the left hand. It's a motor skill, the same family of learning as a run of notes on a piano or a new move in a sport.

Everyone practised until the end of a session. Then the groups split. Some were retested after a night of sleep. Others were retested after the same number of hours spent awake during the day.

The awake group had not lost anything. They simply hadn't gained anything either. Their speed and accuracy looked much like they had at the end of practice.

The group that slept came back better. Walker reports they were roughly 20 to 30 percent faster, and more accurate too. They had done no extra practice. The improvement arrived while they were lying still.

Walker then looked at what kind of sleep went with the gain. It tracked stage 2 NREM sleep, and in particular short bursts of brain activity called sleep spindles. Those spindles are dense in the last hours of a full night.

That detail matters for anyone who sets an early alarm. When you cut a night short, you don't lose a random slice of sleep. You lose the early-morning stretch, and that is exactly where this spindle-rich sleep sits.

##Practice builds the skill, but in Walker's reading, sleep is when the brain consolidates and automates it; the improvement comes offline.##

He takes the idea beyond the lab. Walker reports that athletes who sleep under about six hours, or less than eight, show sharply lower time to physical exhaustion, in a range of 10 to 30 percent, and a higher risk of injury. He says he has advised professional sports teams on sleep as part of their performance work.

The picture that emerges is a slightly uncomfortable one for anyone who trains hard. Athletes put in hours to shave off fractions of a second. Yet a large part of what that training teaches gets finished later, while they are doing nothing at all. And it is finished in the part of the night that most often gets sacrificed to an early start.

The typing study makes the point in the plainest way possible. Same sequence, same practice, same number of hours before the retest. The only difference was whether those hours included a night of sleep.

[[lessons]]
$$**The skill finishes after you stop.** Practice is only the first half of learning a movement. In Walker's study, people who slept typed the sequence faster and more accurately the next day without a single extra repetition. The session plants the skill; the night completes it.$$

$$**The last hours of the night carry extra weight.** The overnight gain tracked stage 2 NREM sleep spindles, which cluster late in a full night. So six hours is not simply three-quarters of the benefit of eight. An early alarm trims the very stretch where motor learning seems to be consolidated.$$

$$**Short sleep can quietly waste work you already did.** The group retested after the same hours awake didn't get worse, but they didn't get the boost either. If you practise hard and then cut the night short, part of the effort you invested never gets its payoff.$$

[[reflect]]
- Think of a skill you practised hard and then found easier after a break. What happened between the two sessions?
- When you cut sleep, is it usually the start of the night or the early morning that goes?
- Is there something you're learning right now where you judge progress only by how the practice itself felt?

&&Before your next session of learning something physical or technical, could you protect the full night after it and notice whether the first attempt the next day feels different?&&

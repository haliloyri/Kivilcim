One of the greatest scientific minds in history once explained how he thought, and the answer surprised many people. Words, Albert Einstein said, seemed to play almost no role at all. Tony Buzan built a whole method of note-taking around that kind of insight.

[[story]]
Einstein was once asked by the mathematician Jacques Hadamard to describe how his mind worked when he was thinking creatively. His reply became famous. Words, whether written or spoken, didn't seem to play any real part in his thinking. Instead, he worked with signs and more or less clear images, which he could call up and combine at will. Only later, with effort, did he translate the results into words and mathematics.

He gave a vivid example from his youth. As a young man, he imagined what it would be like to ride alongside a beam of light, traveling at the same speed. What would he see? That simple picture, played out in his head, became one of the seeds of his later thinking about relativity.

##Some of the most important ideas of the twentieth century began as pictures, not sentences.##

Tony Buzan, author of *The Mind Map Book*, was fascinated by thinkers like Einstein and Leonardo da Vinci, whose notebooks are full of sketches, arrows and diagrams alongside the words. He argued that they point to something true about how everyone's brain works.

Buzan called it **radiant thinking**. When you think of an idea, he said, your brain doesn't produce a neat list. It produces associations that branch outward in every direction. Think of "summer," and you might get sun, holidays, a particular beach, ice cream, a song, a memory of a friend. Each of those branches into more. The mind is a web, not a queue.

Yet the way most of us were taught to take notes is strictly linear. Lines of text. Bullet points. Everything in one color, from top to bottom. Buzan argued that this uses only part of what the brain can do. It captures words, but it loses the connections, images and patterns that help us understand and remember.

His answer was the **Mind Map**. You start with a central image or word in the middle of the page, representing your main topic. From it, thick branches radiate out for the main themes. Smaller branches grow from those for details. You use single key words rather than sentences, and add colors and small pictures along the way.

The result looks less like a list and more like a tree, or a map. For Buzan, that's exactly the point: notes that look like the way we think.

[[lessons]]
$$**Great thinking often begins in pictures.** Einstein said words barely featured in his creative thinking; he worked with images he could combine and replay. Riding alongside a beam of light was a picture before it was physics. Giving yourself permission to think visually can open ideas that sentences alone keep closed.$$

$$**Linear notes capture only part of how you think.** Buzan's radiant thinking describes the mind as a web of associations. Standard notes, one line after another in a single color, flatten that web. When notes feel dead or hard to remember, it may be because they've lost the connections between the ideas.$$

$$**Branching notes match branching minds.** A Mind Map puts the main idea in the center and lets related ideas radiate outward, with key words, color and images. It's a simple change in format that can make relationships between ideas visible, and make the whole topic easier to recall.$$

[[reflect]]
- When you work on a hard problem, do you think more in words or in images?
- What happens to your ideas when you only write them down as lists?
- Which subject you're learning would benefit from seeing all its connections on one page?

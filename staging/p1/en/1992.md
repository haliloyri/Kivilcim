At noon on a Wall Street pavement, hula dancers and ukulele players gather while reporters wait with notebooks. The man they have all come to watch is the founder of a retail chain from Bentonville, Arkansas. He has promised to dance with them, and the only thing that makes him do it is the promise.

[[story]]
Sam Walton, whose 1992 memoir *Made in America* was written with John Huey, made a promise about profit and then had to keep it in public. If Wal-Mart's pre-tax profit reached eight percent, he would do the hula on Wall Street. According to the Encyclopedia of Arkansas it was a wager with David Glass, and it concerned the profits of 1983. Walmart's own history calls it a promise to the company's associates.

Eight percent was a demanding figure. Walmart's history says the retail industry average at the time was around three percent. So Walton had attached a very public, very silly penalty to a target far above what competitors typically earned. The bet said that his people could do much better than their industry, and that he would look foolish if they did.

They did. In 1984 Walton danced the hula on Wall Street, at high noon according to the Encyclopedia of Arkansas, keeping his word to the people who worked for him. Walmart's account says Glass made sure it would be a proper event: he hired a troupe of hula dancers and ukulele players and alerted the media. Walton, the same account says, was somewhat reluctant. He did it anyway.

A promise made inside the company had become a scene on the most serious street in American finance. Nothing about it was dignified, and that is what makes it work as a story. The person who set the target paid its price himself, in front of strangers, and the price was embarrassment rather than money.

Look at how the bet was built. It tied a hard number to something personal, so the target stopped being an order handed down and became a shared project with a visible stake on both sides. The people reaching the number did the work. The person who named the number took the risk of looking ridiculous. Because the promise named a place and a time, nobody had to wonder whether it would be kept.

The loser's part was also what everyone remembered afterwards. Few people could recite the profit figure for 1983, yet the image of a company founder dancing the hula in the financial district is the part still retold.

[[lessons]]
$$**Put a visible, slightly silly price on your own side of a goal, and pay it first.** A target that only demands effort from others reads as a demand, while one the leader stakes something on reads as a shared bet. The stake does not need to be large, only specific, public and a little undignified, such as a haircut, a costume or a song at the next meeting. When your team sees you hold up your end where others can watch, they trust that the goal is meant seriously and that its reward will be real.$$

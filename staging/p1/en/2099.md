A man kneels on the deck of a whaleship, nailing a square of canvas over a hole in a boat. Two other boats are still out after whales, the ship is waiting with her sail hauled back, and the morning is fine and clear. Nothing in the weather gives any sign of what is about to happen.

[[story]]
Nathaniel Philbrick's *In the Heart of the Sea* tells the story of the Nantucket whaleship Essex, and the best-known record of her last morning is the one written by her first mate, Owen Chase. On 20 November 1820 the Essex was cruising in the Pacific, just south of the equator and, as Chase put it, more than a thousand miles from the nearest land. Around eight o'clock the man at the masthead called out that he saw whales.

Three boats went down. Chase had the harpoon in the second boat, and the whale he struck threw itself against the boat and stove in her side with its tail. He grabbed the boat hatchet and cut the line, then stuffed three or four jackets into the hole and rowed back to the ship. He was nailing canvas over the damage when he saw a very large sperm whale, about eighty-five feet long by his estimate, lying quietly off the weather bow.

It came straight for the ship. Chase shouted for the helm to go hard up, but the whale hit the bow before the words had settled. It slid under the keel and lay stunned alongside for a minute. Then it came back and struck again, and this time the bows were smashed in.

##From the first blow to the moment the crew left the ship, Chase wrote, no more than ten minutes could have passed.##

He told every man to look after himself. The steward went down into the cabin twice and carried up two quadrants, two books of navigation tables and the captain's trunk. Chase snatched two compasses from the binnacle. They shoved the spare boat off the deck just before the ship went over on her side.

When Captain Pollard rowed up he asked what had happened. Chase records his own answer: "We have been stove by a whale." The two of them cut away the masts so the wreck would right itself, then chopped through the planking above two casks of bread. They came away with six hundred pounds of hard bread, about sixty-five gallons of water for each boat, a musket, a couple of files and about two pounds of boat nails. Twenty men now had three open boats between them.

Chase wrote that he looked at the quadrants and compasses and thought of them as the probable instruments of their salvation. Bread and water would keep them alive for a few weeks. Only those instruments would tell them where they were, and which way to steer across an ocean with no landmarks.

[[lessons]]
$$**When everything is collapsing, save first what tells you where you are.** Think of a freelance designer whose laptop is stolen the week before a deadline. The files are painful to lose, but the client list, the passwords and the project notes decide whether she can restart on Monday. In a minute of panic it is easy to rescue whatever is nearest, so decide ahead of time which two or three things give you your bearings. Then, on the bad day, your hands already know where to go, and the rest of the rescue can wait for an hour or two.$$

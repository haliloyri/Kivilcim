Some leaders always have the answer. They never admit a mistake, never look uncertain, and keep tight control of every detail. To many people, that looks like strength. Brené Brown looks at the same behavior and sees something else: armor.

[[story]]
In *Dare to Lead*, Brené Brown describes armor as the self-protection we put on to avoid feeling vulnerable. Leadership is full of uncertainty, risk and emotional exposure. Decisions might go wrong. People might be disappointed. You might be criticized in public. Armor is how many people cope with that.

Brown is not dismissive of armor. It usually develops for a reason. At some point, it protected us. But she argues that in leadership, it comes at a high cost. Armor makes it harder for people to trust you, harder for teams to be creative, and harder for anyone to bring you bad news or new ideas.

She contrasts **armored leadership** with **daring leadership**, and gives concrete examples of each.

One is being a **knower** versus being a **learner**. The armored leader needs to be right and to have the answer, and treats not knowing as a threat. The daring leader is more interested in getting it right than in being right, and is willing to say, "I don't know yet."

Another is using **power over** people versus **power with** them. Armored leaders control, command and hoard decisions. Daring leaders share power and build it in others.

A third is **perfectionism** versus **healthy striving**. Perfectionism, Brown argues, is armor: it's driven by fear of judgment and failure. Healthy striving is about growth and doing good work.

##Armor makes a leader look invulnerable. It also makes them unreachable.##

Another example is what Brown calls zigzagging: dodging and weaving around a hard issue, spending energy to avoid the conflict instead of facing it. Daring leaders rumble with the issue directly, staying in the discomfort long enough to work it out.

Brown's point is that teams follow the leader's lead. When a leader never shows uncertainty, others learn to hide theirs. When a leader never admits a mistake, others cover up their own. When a leader takes off some of the armor, saying "I got that wrong" or "I need your help with this," it becomes safer for everyone else to do the same. That safety is where trust, honesty and creativity come from.

Taking off armor isn't about oversharing or falling apart in public. It's about being willing to be seen as a real person who is still learning.

[[lessons]]
$$**Armor protects you and isolates you.** Brown doesn't mock armor; it usually developed to keep us safe. But in leadership it blocks trust, creativity and honest feedback. Recognizing your own armor, whether that's control, perfectionism or always having the answer, is the first step to deciding when you still need it.$$

$$**Be a learner, not a knower.** Armored leaders need to be right. Daring leaders care about getting it right. "I don't know yet, let's figure it out" can feel risky to say, but it invites the team's best thinking instead of their silent compliance. It also tends to lead to better decisions.$$

$$**Your armor sets the team's norms.** When a leader hides mistakes and uncertainty, the team learns to hide theirs. When a leader admits them, it becomes safe for everyone. Taking off some armor isn't just personal growth; it changes what's possible for the people around you.$$

[[reflect]]
- What kind of armor do you reach for at work: control, perfectionism, cynicism, always having the answer?
- Where are you trying to be the knower when you could be a learner?
- What do the people around you learn from how you handle your own mistakes?

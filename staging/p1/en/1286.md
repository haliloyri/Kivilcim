A rich man is suffering from a painful illness. He is willing to pay any doctor, any price, for a cure. There is only one thing he won't do: give up the gluttony that brought the illness on.

James Allen tells this little story on purpose, because he thinks most of us are closer to that man than we'd like.

[[story]]
The rich man appears in *As a Man Thinketh*, Allen's short book usually dated to 1903. He isn't a real patient. He's one of several illustrations Allen uses to make a single point.

That point is stated in one of the book's best-known lines: circumstance does not make a person; it reveals them to themselves.

Allen observes that people are anxious to improve their circumstances but unwilling to improve themselves. And because of that, he says, they remain bound. They want the situation to change while they stay exactly as they are.

His examples make the pattern easy to see. There is a poor man who wants better surroundings, yet shirks his work. There is the rich man, ready to pay anything for a cure, as long as he can keep the habit causing the illness. And there is an employer who cuts his workers' wages to raise his profits, and ends up failing.

##In each case, the person wants the outcome while holding on to the very behavior that produces the problem.##

Allen goes further than many readers today would. He argues that thoughts shape character, and that character tends, over time, to show up in a person's circumstances, including their health. He presents this as a moral law. It is his philosophical view, not the result of research, and it's worth reading that way. Plenty in life is outside anyone's control.

But the hopeful part of his argument doesn't depend on the strongest version of the claim. A person often cannot choose their circumstances directly. They can, however, choose their thoughts, and through them, their behavior. In Allen's account, that is how people shape their circumstances indirectly.

So the question he leaves behind is not "Why is this happening to me?" It's closer to: "What am I doing that keeps this in place, and am I willing to change it?" The rich man, in Allen's telling, answers no.

[[lessons]]
$$**Wanting the result isn't the same as wanting the change.** Allen's rich man would pay any price for a cure but won't give up his gluttony. That's what makes the illustration sting: he's not lazy or indifferent, he's eager. He just wants the outcome delivered while the cause stays untouched. Eagerness for a better situation can sit right next to a refusal to change the habit feeding it, and the eagerness can hide the refusal.$$

$$**Circumstances show you who you are.** Allen's line, "Circumstance does not make the man; it reveals him to himself," shifts the focus. A hard situation is not only something that happens to you. It also shows you your own habits and choices, sometimes ones you'd rather not see. That can be uncomfortable, but it's also where the useful information is. The poor man, the rich man and the employer each had a clue in front of them.$$

[[reflect]]
- Is there a situation you'd like to change where you're hoping for the result without changing your part in it?
- When has a hard circumstance revealed something about you that you didn't expect?
- Which of your habits would you most like to keep, even though it feeds a problem?

A government takes office in a country that has just stopped being a protectorate. A year later, diamonds are confirmed in its soil. Every politician in that room can see two paths, and the quick one leads straight into a private pocket.

[[story]]
Botswana is one of the cases Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson use in *Why Nations Fail* to show that a country can take the good path. The territory was the Bechuanaland Protectorate until 30 September 1966, when it became the Republic of Botswana, and Seretse Khama became its first president. His own history with Britain was not smooth. Khama, heir to the chieftainship of the Ngwato, married a British woman, Ruth Williams, and in 1950 Britain banished the couple from the protectorate under pressure from apartheid South Africa. After protests by his supporters he was allowed to return in 1956.

Then came the test. Diamonds were discovered in 1967, only a year after independence. Mineral wealth of this kind has made other countries famous for the wrong reasons: whoever holds power is tempted to grab the revenue, fund loyal supporters and stop answering to anyone. Published accounts describe Botswana's route differently. The government formed a partnership with the diamond company De Beers, which became Debswana, and the income went toward schools, health care and infrastructure.

The authors' argument is about order. In their view Botswana did not become well governed because it got rich. It could use its riches well because it already had rules that limited what any leader could do and gave ordinary people a share in decisions. The windfall arrived in a country where it was harder to steal.

There is a fair counter-argument, and it matters. Some commentators say the diamond monopoly, not the political rules, explains the success, and others note that mineral wealth guarantees nothing, since many resource-rich countries remain poor. The authors' case is therefore best read as a pattern, not a law: the same diamonds, in a different set of institutions, could have fed a different story.

What stays with you is the timing. Rules cost almost nothing in the years when there is nothing to fight over, and become priceless the moment something valuable turns up. By then it is far too late to write them.

Think about what that means in practice. A rule that says the revenue belongs to the public purse is easy to break when a single person can quietly decide otherwise, and easy to keep when many people would notice and object. The diamonds did not create that difference. They only made it visible, and very expensive to ignore.

[[lessons]]
$$**Write the rules for sharing a windfall before the windfall arrives, because afterwards everyone has a stake in writing them for themselves.** A team that decides how a bonus, a big client or a new budget will be divided only after it lands tends to argue about fairness while the strongest voices quietly take what they can. Agreeing in advance, when nobody knows who will benefit, makes the rule easier to accept and harder to bend. Ask which good surprise could arrive next year and whether the people involved already know who decides what happens to it.$$

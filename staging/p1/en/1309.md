A hand holds a Slinky from the top. With the other hand underneath, it lets the bottom go. The spring drops, stretches, and starts bouncing up and down. Donella Meadows asks a question that sounds almost silly: what made it bounce? The obvious answer is the hand. She thinks the obvious answer is wrong, and that it is the same mistake we make with problems that keep coming back.

[[story]]
Donella H. Meadows opens *Thinking in Systems: A Primer*, published in 2008, with that Slinky. The hand holds it from above, supports it from below, and then takes the lower hand away. The Slinky bounces.

The hand did something, of course. It released the spring. But the bouncing itself, the up and down rhythm, comes from the Slinky's own structure. The release was only the trigger. The pattern was built into the thing.

That is her central point. A system largely causes its own behavior. Outside events can set it off, but the shape of what happens comes from how the system is built.

To make this usable, Meadows describes any system as three kinds of things: elements, the interconnections between them, and a function or purpose.

Then she asks which of those matters most. You might expect the elements, since they are the most visible part. She argues they usually matter least. Take a football team. You can replace every single player over time, and it is still recognizably the same team.

Now change something else. Change the rules, or have the same people play a different game altogether. The players are identical, but the system is very different. Its interconnections and purpose shifted, and so did everything it does.

##Swap every player and it is still the same team; change the rules and it becomes a different game.##

This explains a frustrating pattern. When a problem keeps returning, the natural move is to blame the latest outside event or to replace a part, usually a person or a tool. But if the behavior comes from structure, those fixes leave the structure alone. The next trigger arrives, and the same pattern plays out again with new faces.

Meadows's Slinky is a small reminder to look past the hand. When something keeps repeating, the more useful question is not who touched it last. It is how the thing is built.

[[lessons]]
$$**Look at structure before triggers.** The Slinky bounces because of its own coils, not because of the hand that let go. When a behavior repeats, the most recent push is usually just the release. The pattern lives in how the system is put together, and that is where a lasting fix has to reach.$$

$$**Replacing parts is often the weakest move.** A football team with all-new players is still the same team, playing the same way under the same rules. Swapping a person, a vendor, or a tool feels decisive, but it touches the elements, the part Meadows says usually matters least.$$

$$**Change the rules and the system changes.** Keep the same players and change the rules, and you have a different game. Rules, connections, and goals are less visible than people, but they shape what the system actually does. That makes them the places worth examining when a problem refuses to go away.$$

[[reflect]]
- Which problem at work or at home keeps returning even after you changed the people or tools involved?
- What rule, incentive, or goal might be quietly producing that pattern?
- When it happened last time, did you look at the trigger or at the setup that made the trigger matter?

&&For the problem that keeps coming back, what is the one rule or connection you have never tried changing?&&

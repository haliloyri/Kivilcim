In a Johannesburg park, a woman walks beside a small boy as though she were his mother. A few steps behind them, a second woman trails along as if she were the maid. Nobody strolling past would guess that the woman at the back, the one who seems so unimportant, is the real mother.

[[story]]
After Trevor Noah was born, his mother rented a flat in Joubert Park, next to Hillbrow in Johannesburg. Noah describes the park as the city's Central Park, with gardens, a zoo and a huge chessboard with human-sized pieces. The trouble was the law: relationships between races were illegal, and a mixed-race child was living, walking evidence of one.

His mother found a way through. Having two colored parents was not illegal, so she presented her son as colored. But the boy was big for his age, which made him hard to pass off or hide. In Noah's own words, when he was one you would have thought he was two, and at two he looked about four.

So they set up an arrangement. A neighbour named Queen, a colored woman, walked beside Noah in the park as if she were his mother. His real mother followed a few steps behind, like a maid. Noah still has photographs of this setup. If police appeared, his mother had to drop him and deny he was hers.

The weak link was a person nobody could script. One day his father tried to join them in the park. Little Noah ran after him calling "Daddy," people turned to look, and his father panicked and fled.

At his grandmother's house in Soweto, the situation was tighter still. Only Black people were allowed to live in Soweto, so a light-skinned child stood out sharply, and the government watched closely. His grandmother kept him indoors, and he could play only in the yard, never in the street. Because the next-door neighbour was a "blackjack," a Black person working for the police, she had to smuggle him in and out when the neighbour was not looking.

When Noah was three, he dug under the driveway gate and ran off. A search party found him. Looking back, he notes that the family could have been deported, his grandmother arrested, his mother imprisoned and him sent to a home for colored children.

What the details share is that everything visible was arranged correctly: a role, a neighbour, a walking distance. What broke, each time, was the least controllable part, a three-year-old. An outsider would not have noticed that, but everyone inside the arrangement knew where its weak point sat.

[[lessons]]
$$**Look for the part of your plan that cannot follow instructions, and build the arrangement around it.** The family's setup had roles, distances and rules, but a child running to his father or digging under a gate fitted into none of them. In a project schedule, a presentation or a household routine, you can name in advance what is not in your hands and give it its own safeguard. Then the plan does not collapse when the unpredictable person or event shows up, because only that one piece drifts and you are ready for it. That is craft, not pessimism.$$

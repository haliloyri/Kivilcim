At 46, a fund manager walks away from the top-ranked stock fund in America. Letters arrive from investors who are upset that he is going. Behind the decision sits a record that sounds like a typo, and a remark about how many of his picks actually worked.

[[story]]
When Peter Lynch took over Fidelity's Magellan Fund in May 1977, it held about $18 million. When he left in 1990, at the age of 46, it held more than $14 billion, according to Fortune. Wharton Magazine, profiling its alumnus, reports that Magellan was the top-ranked general equity fund in America during his years, with an average return of 29.2 percent a year, and that it lagged the S&P 500 in only two of them. His retirement brought letters from upset investors.

Lynch's *One Up on Wall Street* argues that ordinary people can use the same eyes he used. In an interview with PBS's *Frontline* he put a count on his hits rather than a slogan. "I think I listed over a hundred stocks that went up over ten-fold when I ran Magellan and I owned thousands of stocks," he said.

Set the count next to the arithmetic. A stock that rises tenfold gains 900 percent, which Lynch calls a tenbagger. A stock that falls as far as it can loses 100 percent, and no more. A fund that holds thousands of stocks over thirteen years does not need most of them to work. It needs enough of them to run far, and the arithmetic of the losers is capped while the arithmetic of the winners is not.

He also had a way of looking for them. "The person that turns over the most rocks wins the game," he said. "That's always been my philosophy." Wharton Magazine also quotes him on the other half, a taste for the simple: "Go for a business that any idiot can run." Among the Magellan picks Wharton lists are Taco Bell, Pier 1 Imports and Dunkin' Donuts, ordinary names a shopper would know. Taco Bell, Lynch told Frontline, was one of the first stocks he bought, and people "wouldn't look at a small restaurant company." That was a rock nobody was turning over. The 29.2 percent average also includes the two years he trailed the index, so the record came from winning big overall, not from winning every year.

So the record rests on two plain habits. One is looking at a very large number of companies, the rocks. The other is accepting that many of the stocks will be ordinary and a few will be extraordinary, and holding on long enough for the few to matter.

[[lessons]]
$$**Make enough small tries that one big success can pay for the misses.** A freelance writer who pitches one magazine at a time is crushed by every refusal, while one who has many pitches circulating can lose most of them and still land a few good commissions. Because a loss has a floor and a win does not, a long list of cheap, sensible bets tolerates being wrong far more often than you expect. Count how many real attempts you have made this year, and let the number of tries do part of the work that confidence cannot. A rejection then becomes one line on a list, not a verdict on you.$$

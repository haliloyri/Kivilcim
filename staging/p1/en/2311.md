Engineers grumble that their assignment amounts to bolting a bag onto the side of an old machine. Nobody says it with a smile. It is a rude phrase, and it describes something specific: a product that is worse than it could be, finished sooner than a better one.

[[story]]
Tracy Kidder's *The Soul of a New Machine* records the phrase. Among the Data General engineers, the goal of the project that became Eagle was described as "put a bag on the side of the Eclipse". The Eclipse was the company's existing 16-bit minicomputer, and a bag on its side meant an add-on that would stretch it toward 32 bits without redesigning it. The complaint was about what such a product would be: inferior, but ready quickly.

The book also records Tom West's own motto, and it sounds like a provocation: "Not everything worth doing is worth doing well." A reader's first reaction might be that it excuses sloppy work. It reads differently when you set it next to the complaint, because the complaint is exactly about doing a thing well versus doing it fast.

The machine that came out of Eagle was a 32-bit computer, fully compatible with the Eclipses already on the market. It used new technology and, for most of its design, the work of recent college graduates. Both choices cost something. A fully compatible machine had to carry the old design's rules, and a team of newcomers had less experience to lean on.

So the motto is not an instruction to be careless. It is a statement about where to put effort. Compatibility with existing Eclipses was a requirement that customers could see, and the project met it. Beyond such requirements, a team racing a deadline cannot polish every corner, and West's phrase says out loud that some corners will stay rough.

That is a harder idea than it looks. Most managers say they want everything done well. West's motto admits that a team has a limited supply of time and attention, and that deciding where "good enough" stands is part of the job. The alternative is to let each engineer make that decision silently, in a hundred small places, and end up with polish where nobody looks and rough spots where everybody does.

There is a time dimension as well. Any team choosing between a product that is quick but incomplete and one that is late but whole is really deciding which shortcoming a customer can live with. West's motto turns that decision from an embarrassing admission into an open choice, and once the choice is open the team can argue about it, instead of discovering the rough corners one by one after the fact.

[[lessons]]
$$**Decide in advance which parts of a job must be done well and which only need to work.** A cook preparing a dinner for guests might put the care into the main dish and settle for good-enough side dishes, instead of spreading attention thinly across everything. Then tell the people who work with you what you chose. Saying so openly lets everyone on the team spend effort on the same few things. It also keeps the rough parts from being mistaken for neglect later, because you chose them on purpose and can say why, whether the question comes from a client, a manager or your own doubts later.$$

In the first months of the war, newspapers print a startling item: Germany is close to making butter from coal. The headline reads like a recipe, though the ingredient on the table is black coal, and the source is a scientist who has left Germany.

[[story]]
The roots of industrial food often run deeper than we assume. In the history of ultra-processed food, the subject of Chris van Tulleken's *Ultra-Processed People*, there is a curious side story: the idea of making fat from coal. The episode is told here as the record has it.

Late in 1939 the Associated Press reported that the Nazis were close to producing butter from coal. The report rested on Willy Lange, a scientist who had left Germany and who had shared the information with a group of American scientists. In other words, the news reached the paper second-hand.

Behind the idea lay the Fischer-Tropsch process, which turned coal into alcohols, waxes and synthetic oils. Some of those oils could be made into margarine once they had been separated from petrol and lubricating oils. On paper there was a sensible road: coal, then fat, then something edible.

Historians of science find the report somewhat overexcited. An article published by the Science History Institute calls extracting edible oils from coal both impractical and inefficient. It says Lange and the AP "got a bit overexcited". The headline showed only the destination, not the difficulties along the way.

Still, the episode was no invention. An abstract of a review published in *Food Technology* in 1947 states that in Germany synthetic butter was made through the Fischer-Tropsch synthesis for the production of fat. The idea had left the laboratory and become a real product.

It also helps to look at the news as a chain. One scientist talks to American colleagues, they pass it to a newspaper, and the paper passes it to readers. As the chain gets longer, it becomes harder to ask the source anything. The history article also speaks of oils being turned into margarine, while the headline said butter. Those two words are not the same thing.

The story holds both at once: a real industrial process and the exaggeration wrapped around it. The tie between food and industry is far older than today's packaged goods. Calling something butter does not prove it is butter.

[[lessons]]
$$**When a headline about a new food startles you, treat it as a claim waiting to be checked.** A headline can make something look both true and inflated, and you only see the gap by going back to the source. If a friend says that a newly launched product is entirely natural, asking who says so and on what evidence is a good first step. That way you neither swallow nor dismiss a report at once, but look at where it comes from before deciding, and that habit is reason enough to pause ten seconds before you share anything.$$

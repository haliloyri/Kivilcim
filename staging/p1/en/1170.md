Try this before reading on. A bat and a ball cost $1.10 in total. The bat costs $1.00 more than the ball. How much does the ball cost? Notice the number that just popped into your head. Daniel Kahneman built one of the most influential books of our time around that number.

[[story]]
If you're like most people, the answer that arrived was 10 cents. It came quickly. It felt obvious. And it's wrong.

If the ball cost 10 cents, the bat would cost $1.10, a dollar more, and together they'd cost $1.20. The correct answer is 5 cents: a ball at 5 cents and a bat at $1.05, which adds up to $1.10.

In *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, Daniel Kahneman reports how often people fall for it. More than half of the students tested at Harvard, MIT and Princeton gave the intuitive, wrong answer. At less selective universities, more than 80 percent did. These were intelligent young people who could easily have solved the problem. They just didn't check.

##The mistake isn't a lack of intelligence. It's that the first answer feels too right to question.##

Kahneman uses this puzzle to introduce the central idea of his book: two ways our minds work. He calls them **System 1** and **System 2**.

System 1 is fast and automatic. It works without effort and without our deciding to use it. It's what lets you read the emotion on a face, finish the phrase "bread and...," or drive on an empty road while chatting. It's what produced "10 cents" instantly.

System 2 is slow and deliberate. It's the effortful thinking you use to multiply 17 by 24, fill in a tax form or check whether an argument makes sense. It's what you need to find "5 cents."

The problem, Kahneman says, is that System 2 is lazy. Effortful thinking costs energy, and the mind avoids spending it when it can. So when System 1 offers an answer that feels right, System 2 often just approves it without checking. Many of the students who wrote "10 cents" had everything they needed to catch the error. Their slow system simply never got involved.

Kahneman is not saying System 1 is bad. Most of the time, it's remarkably good. It gets us through the day, handles thousands of small judgments and is often right. But it has blind spots. In certain situations, it makes systematic, predictable errors. Kahneman spent decades mapping those errors with his colleague Amos Tversky, and much of the rest of the book explores them.

The first step in avoiding them is simply knowing that the fast answer and the right answer aren't always the same.

[[lessons]]
$$**A quick answer can feel certain and still be wrong.** "10 cents" arrives with total confidence. That feeling of obviousness is exactly what stops us from checking. Kahneman's puzzle shows that confidence and correctness are different things, and that our fastest answers deserve a second look when the stakes matter.$$

$$**Being smart isn't the same as being careful.** More than half of the students at some of the world's top universities got it wrong. They didn't lack ability; they didn't use it. Intelligence protects you only if something prompts you to actually engage it.$$

$$**Know when to wake up System 2.** Because slow thinking is lazy, it tends to approve whatever System 1 suggests. You can't run everything through System 2, but you can learn which situations deserve it: numbers, big decisions, and moments when an answer seems suspiciously easy.$$

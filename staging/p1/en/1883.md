[[open]]
A man on a treadmill watches a news report about a rival's new phone. The next day he tells his business partner something that should have set off every alarm in the company. His partner answers with a sentence that sounds like calm and works like a sedative.

[[story]]
In 2007, Apple launched the first iPhone. Mike Lazaridis, co-founder of Research In Motion, the maker of the BlackBerry, saw a report on the launch while he was exercising on a treadmill. The details come from the book *Losing the Signal*, by Jacquie McNish and Sean Silcoff, which is based on their reporting for the Globe and Mail.

The next day he told his co-CEO, Jim Balsillie, what had struck him. "They put a full web browser on that thing," he said. For a company whose phones had been built around email and a keyboard, that was a remark with a lot of weight in it, and he said it plainly.

There was a complication, and it made the news feel survivable. The mobile carriers, the companies that sold the phones and controlled the networks, had stopped RIM from putting a full browser on its own devices. So RIM could not simply answer Apple's move with the same move. Balsillie's reply was short: "It's OK, we'll be fine."

The sentence deserves a close look because it was not stupid. RIM was successful, its customers were loyal, and its own constraints were real. Everything in it could be defended one fact at a time. What it did was close the conversation. Lazaridis had just named the one feature the rival had and his company lacked, and the reply moved the talk away from that feature and toward the comfort of present success.

A company in this position has two jobs. It has to treat the rival's leap as information about what customers will soon expect, and it has to ask which of its own assumptions that information breaks. A line like "we'll be fine" answers neither question. It reports a feeling and presents it as a conclusion.

RIM later lost its leading position in smartphones to the kind of device that had been shown on that treadmill morning. The point of the scene is not that two executives were fools. It is that the alarm rang clearly, in plain words,, one of them heard it, and the room found a way back to quiet.

[[lessons]]
$$**When a rival makes a leap you did not expect, test the sentence "we'll be fine" against what you just saw before you say it aloud.** A reassurance feels like leadership, but it often ends the very discussion the surprise should start. After a competitor's launch, a lost client or a sudden change in customer behavior, write down what the rival can do that you cannot, and list the assumptions that fact breaks. Only then decide whether you really are fine. This keeps the alarm from being soothed away before anyone has acted on it.$$

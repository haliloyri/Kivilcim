A film studio has its camera loaded and ready for a roll of raw film. The supplier, though, sells film only to a short list of names, and that list is made up of the companies that hold the film patents. If your name is not on it, you own a camera and have nothing to put inside.

[[story]]
In Tim Wu's *The Master Switch* the movies appear as an early example of an information empire, and the story begins with Thomas Edison's patents. In 1908 Edison's company joined the other major holders of film patents to form the Motion Picture Patents Company. The pool gathered under one roof patents that had been rivals before. Its members included Biograph, Vitagraph, Essanay, Selig, Lubin, Kalem and Pathé.

The trust's power did not rest on patents alone. Eastman Kodak, the largest supplier of raw film, was part of the arrangement, and according to published accounts it agreed to sell only to member companies. George Kleine, a leading film distributor, was inside as well. The trust set one uniform rental rate for all licensed films, which meant the members would not compete with each other on price, and in 1910 it created a subsidiary, the General Film Company, that came to dominate film distribution across the country.

The logic was to hold three choke points at once: the machine patents, the raw film and the distribution. A producer without any one of the three could not begin work or could not get a finished film in front of audiences. Being in the trust meant being on a list. Someone outside it might own a camera, yet find nothing to run through it.

Unlicensed independent producers kept working anyway. According to published accounts, many of those outside the trust moved to Hollywood, far from Edison's New Jersey base, where enforcing patents was harder. The same accounts say that in 1911 Kodak changed its exclusive contract and began selling film to unlicensed independents too. That meant one of the three choke points had opened.

The government sued on 15 August 1912. Once patent royalties ended in September 1913, the trust leaned on General Film, as those accounts tell it. On 1 October 1915 a federal court ruled that the trust violated the Sherman Antitrust Act. The company was sold on 31 July 1918.

The weak spot in the structure was that each choke point rested on a separate agreement or the life of a separate patent. Once one of the three doors opened, the other two no longer gave the same protection. In an arrangement like this, power depends on every insider keeping the rule, and an outsider can start with a single open door.

[[lessons]]
$$**If your advantage runs through a gate, list in advance who is able to open it.** A structure that holds three choke points looks solid, but if each one depends on someone else's consent or promise, the strength is borrowed. When your edge in business rests on one supplier, one customer or one contract clause, thinking ahead about what happens if that party changes the deal saves you time and lets you have the talk before panic starts. If your edge passes through a single gate, the cheapest moment to look for a second route is while the gate is still open.$$

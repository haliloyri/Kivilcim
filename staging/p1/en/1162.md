A museum spends fourteen months and considerable money proving that a statue is a genuine ancient Greek masterpiece. Then an art historian walks up, looks at it for a moment, and finds himself staring at its fingernails. Something about them is wrong. He can't quite say what.

[[story]]
Malcolm Gladwell opens *Blink* with this story.

In 1983, an art dealer approached the J. Paul Getty Museum in California with an extraordinary offer: a marble kouros, a statue of a standing nude youth, of a type made in Greece around the sixth century BC. Only about two hundred kouroi are known, and most are damaged or in pieces. This one was almost perfectly preserved. The asking price was just under $10 million.

The Getty didn't rush. For about fourteen months, it investigated. A geologist examined the marble with scientific tests and concluded the surface had aged over centuries, not months. The museum's lawyers examined documents describing the statue's history and ownership. Everything seemed to check out. The Getty bought it, and the statue went on display.

Then the experts started looking at it.

##The Italian art historian Federico Zeri found his eyes drawn again and again to the statue's fingernails. They seemed wrong.##

Evelyn Harrison, a leading authority on Greek sculpture, felt a sudden sense that something was off. Thomas Hoving, former director of the Metropolitan Museum of Art, said the first word that came into his head when he saw it was *fresh*, a strange word for something supposedly 2,500 years old. When the statue was shown to experts in Athens, one described feeling as though there were a glass wall between him and the work.

None of them could fully explain what bothered them. They only knew, within seconds, that something didn't feel right.

The Getty began to look again. And the case started to unravel. Some of the documents that had supported the statue's history turned out to be forged. The more closely people looked, the more doubts grew. To this day, the kouros has never been definitively settled either way. The Getty labels it as Greek, from around 530 BC, or a modern forgery.

For Gladwell, the point is what happened in those first few seconds. Months of careful analysis had pointed one way. The quick, wordless judgments of experts who had spent their lives looking at ancient sculpture pointed the other, and they had noticed something the tests hadn't.

He also hints at something uncomfortable. The Getty badly wanted a statue like this. That wish may have made it easier to accept evidence that said yes.

[[lessons]]
$$**Expertise sees what tests can miss.** The geologist's analysis focused on the marble. Zeri, Harrison and Hoving had spent decades looking at ancient sculpture, and they reacted to the whole object at once. Their snap judgments weren't guesses. They were years of looking, compressed into a moment of unease.$$

$$**Experts often feel before they can explain.** "Fresh," a glass wall, something wrong with the fingernails: none of these is an argument. Yet each pointed at a real problem. In your own field, a hard-to-explain feeling from someone experienced deserves investigation, not dismissal.$$

$$**Wanting it can blind you.** The museum very much wanted a rare, beautiful kouros. Fourteen months of analysis still ended on the answer it hoped for. When you badly want a result, it's worth asking someone without that stake to take a quick, honest look before you commit.$$

[[open]]
A crippled spacecraft is coasting home, and its flight path is slightly off. The computer that would normally fix it is switched off to save power. Two men hold hand controllers instead, and they steer by holding a bright patch of the Sun on one mark and the thin edge of daylight on Earth in another.

[[story]]
Apollo 13 was already heading back toward Earth, the situation Jim Lovell and Jeffrey Kluger describe in *Lost Moon*, but the entry path needed a small correction. About 105 hours into the flight, Houston passed up the numbers for a burn of the lunar module's descent engine. The change in speed was to be 7.8 feet per second, roughly a brisk walk. The engine would run at 10 percent throttle, after a ten-second push from four small thrusters to settle the fuel, and the crew would shut it down by hand at about 14 seconds.

The hard part was pointing the spacecraft. The lunar module's guidance computer was off, so the astronauts lined up by eye. Houston told them the Sun should sit on the spacecraft's forward axis, perpendicular to the line between night and day on Earth. Fred Haise described the lit part of Earth in the top half of the sighting device, with the edge of the daylight lying on the marked line. Capcom Vance Brand had earlier given the crew a reassurance, quoted in the transcript: "the burn is very insensitive to burn time and attitude."

Houston had planned the ignition for 105:30 into the mission, but the Capcom, Jack Lousma, told the crew the timing was theirs to choose, and they fired earlier, at 105:18. Haise called the steps: engine arm, ullage, ignition. Afterward he said he made "maybe about six or seven inputs," and that Lovell's needle was "hanging in there pretty good."

The burn gave 7.4 feet per second. Haise reported that he had started with about 0.2 showing, and Lousma replied, "Trim it to 7.6." A short burst from the small thrusters closed the gap, and Apollo Control announced: "Delta V achieved was 7.6 foot per second."

Look at what Houston did here. They did not demand a perfect burn from two tired men in a cold cabin. Instead they said which errors the maneuver could absorb, gave a stop limit, and kept one precise number for the end, where a quick trim could reach it. What the crew had to do became a few large, forgiving moves followed by one small, exact one.

[[lessons]]
$$**Tell the person doing a hard job which errors the job forgives and which one it does not.** Houston said the burn was insensitive to time and attitude, so Lovell and Haise could stop fighting for precision where none was needed. Then they spent their care on the single number that mattered and fixed it with a trim. You can do the same when you delegate a report, a repair or a launch: name the two or three things that must be exact, and say plainly that the rest has room. People work faster and calmer once they know where the margin is.$$

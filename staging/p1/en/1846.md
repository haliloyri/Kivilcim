In a shopping mall, a young man walks up to a young woman who is on her own and asks for her phone number. Same request. What changes is where the conversation starts: in front of a flower shop, a cake shop or a shoe shop.

[[story]]
One of the examples often cited when people explain Robert Cialdini's idea of pre-suasion is a study by the French researcher Nicolas Guéguen. He published it in 2012 in the *Journal of Social Psychology*, and the title carries a hint: "Say it... Near the Flower Shop". The question was whether flowers could change how people answer a request.

The first experiment ran in a lab. About a hundred female undergraduates were taken one at a time into a room where an attractive man was waiting. In some rooms there were flowers and in others there were not. Roughly 80 percent of the women in the room with flowers gave him their number, against about 50 percent in the room without.

The second experiment ran in the field. A young man approached 600 young women who were walking alone in a shopping mall and asked for their numbers. He did this in three places: in front of a flower shop, a cake shop and a women's shoe shop. In front of the flower shop about 24 percent said yes. In front of the cake shop and the shoe shop the share was clearly lower. Same request, and more yeses beside the flowers.

The figures deserve an honest look. The man was turned down most of the time, and even at the flower shop roughly three in four said no. The flowers did not work a miracle. They tilted the numbers upward, and they did it while only the place of the request changed.

The lab and the mall are very different settings, yet both point the same way, which gives the idea more weight than a single result would. Each of them also sets the request inside a scene that was chosen in advance rather than invented on the spot.

One plausible reading is that flowers are tied to romance, courtship and gifts. A woman walking past a flower shop has those associations a little closer to the surface, and a stranger's question may feel less out of place inside them. A cake shop and a shoe shop are pleasant places too, but neither sets a romantic scene.

That is the point Cialdini keeps returning to. The request matters, and so does the scene in which you make it. The same question finds a warmer reception in one spot because that spot's own associations are already prepared to receive it.

[[lessons]]
$$**When you ask for something, pick a place and a moment that already carries the right association.** A coordinator looking for volunteers for a neighborhood clean-up will probably do better asking people on the very street that needs it, with the littered corners in view, than in a corridor between unrelated errands. The words of the request stay the same, while the surroundings supply a ground that is ready to receive them. This guarantees nothing and only nudges the odds, and as long as your request is real and honest you are simply setting a scene that suits your question.$$

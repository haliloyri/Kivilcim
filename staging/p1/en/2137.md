[[open]]
A thin envelope arrives in the department mailbox, with a postage-paid reply envelope tucked inside. The questionnaire asks how happy you would be in each of the next ten years if you won tenure, and how happy if you lost it. You are still waiting for your own decision, so every answer is a guess.

[[story]]
Ninety-seven assistant professors in the College of Liberal Arts at the University of Texas at Austin received that questionnaire, and 33 sent it back. On a scale from 1 to 7, they expected tenure to leave them at about 5.9 in happiness over the following five years. They expected a denial to leave them at 3.4, a gap of two and a half points. After year five, they said the gap would mostly close.

The envelope came with a cover story. Its sender, a professor at the University of Virginia, wrote that he was studying "the lives of people who are or have been faculty members." In fact it was part of a research paper by Daniel Gilbert, author of *Stumbling on Happiness*, and four colleagues, who wanted to compare those forecasts with what actually happened to people.

For that they needed the other side, so they traced 123 former assistant professors whose tenure cases had been decided between 1984 and 1994. Ninety-two had been promoted and 31 turned down. Replies came back from 47 of the first group and 20 of the second.

Those who had been through it described a much narrower gap. Among people whose decision was about two and a half years old, the tenured scored 5.24 and the denied 4.71. Half a point is not a statistically meaningful difference. Among decisions around eight years old, the scores were 5.82 and 5.23, and again the two groups could not be told apart.

The details matter here. Only 7 of the denied professors had a recent decision and 13 an older one, so the researchers urged caution. The forecasts for the long run, years six to ten, turned out accurate. What people got wrong was the first few years, in which the denied did better than predicted and the tenured slightly worse.

The authors explain it by what they call the psychological immune system: after a bad event, the mind starts producing ways to make sense of it and live with it, mostly without our noticing. People who forecast do not count on that process. They picture the blow, and they forget that they will be the one responding to it. Note also that forecasters and experiencers were different people, so the comparison is across groups, not within one person's life.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you judge how long a setback will hurt, ask someone who has already lived through it.** If you are dreading a missed promotion or a lost client, the scene your mind replays is usually day one, not month six. A colleague who went through the same thing can tell you what the third month actually felt like, and your own past disappointments can answer the same question. With that information you can make the decision using facts about recovery instead of fear, and a frightening outcome shrinks to something closer to its real size. It may still be unpleasant, but you will know what you are facing and how long it tends to last.$$

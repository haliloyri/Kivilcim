Picture two artists working equally hard. One suffers over every sentence, and treats the suffering as proof that the work matters. The other works just as seriously, but with a sly grin, as if playing an elaborate game. Elizabeth Gilbert says we can choose which one to be.

[[story]]
In *Big Magic*, Elizabeth Gilbert describes two very different ways of living a creative life. She calls them the **Martyr** and the **Trickster**.

Martyr energy, she writes, is dark, solemn and heavy. The Martyr believes that creativity demands suffering. Great work must come from pain, sacrifice and struggle. The harder it is, the more it hurts, the more it must mean. Many artists, Gilbert says, were raised on this story, the tortured genius, the suffering poet, the novelist destroyed by their own brilliance. For the Martyr, pain isn't just part of the work. It's a sign of worth.

Trickster energy is different. It's light, clever and playful. The Trickster works hard too, but doesn't believe suffering is required. The Trickster trusts that things can work out, and enjoys the game of making something. When obstacles appear, the Trickster looks for a way around them, sometimes with humor, rather than treating them as tragic proof of the artist's destiny.

##Gilbert says she deliberately chooses the Trickster, not because she cares less, but because she wants to keep making things for a lifetime.##

She's direct about why. The Martyr's belief that suffering is necessary isn't only untrue, she argues. It's harmful. It turns creativity into a burden. It encourages people to wear exhaustion and misery as badges of honor. And it can make them suspicious of joy, as though work that came easily couldn't be good.

The Martyr also expects something in return. If I suffer enough, the Martyr believes, I'll be rewarded. When the reward doesn't come, bitterness follows. The Trickster makes no such bargain. It plays because playing is worth doing.

This connects to Gilbert's broader thoughts about fear. Creative work will always involve uncertainty and some fear; that can't be avoided. But how you relate to it can be chosen. You can treat each difficulty as a tragedy, or you can treat it as part of a strange, sometimes funny adventure.

Gilbert's message is not that creativity is always easy. It's that suffering doesn't have to be its price, and that lightness and seriousness can live together. You can care deeply about your work and still refuse to let it make you miserable.

[[lessons]]
$$**Suffering isn't proof of seriousness.** The Martyr believes that pain shows the work matters. Gilbert calls that belief harmful. It can make people suspicious of ease and proud of exhaustion. Good work can come from joy just as easily as from misery, and it's far more sustainable when it does.$$

$$**Lightness and hard work can coexist.** The Trickster doesn't work less; it works with less dread. Humor, playfulness and trust make obstacles feel like puzzles rather than tragedies. That attitude often keeps people creating for much longer than the Martyr's heavy commitment ever could.$$

$$**You can choose your attitude.** Gilbert treats Martyr and Trickster as choices, not fixed personalities. Noticing which energy you bring to your work is the first step. Deciding, deliberately, to approach it a little more like a Trickster can change how the whole creative process feels.$$

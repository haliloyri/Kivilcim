It's 1943, and a father on a family vacation has just taken a picture of his daughter. She's about three. She wants to see it. Why can't she see it now? Every adult already knows the answer: film has to be developed. The father happened to be Edwin Land, and he didn't stop at that answer.

[[story]]
Warren Berger tells this story in *A More Beautiful Question*, and it starts with the most ordinary moment imaginable.

In 1943, Edwin Land was on vacation with his family. He took a photograph of his young daughter, who was about three years old. And she asked him why she couldn't see the picture right away.

It's the kind of question small children ask all day long. It also had a perfectly good answer. Film has to be developed. You take the pictures, the roll goes off to be processed, and some time later you see the results. That was simply how photography worked.

A parent could give that explanation in a sentence, and the conversation would be over. Most would. The answer was correct, after all.

Land did something different. He treated her question as a real problem rather than a misunderstanding to be corrected. Instead of explaining why she couldn't see the picture, he began working out how a camera could produce the picture immediately.

##The sensible answer was true, and the invention depended on Land refusing to be satisfied with it.##

The work took years. In 1948, Polaroid brought its instant camera to market. The question a three-year-old asked on vacation had turned into a product that did exactly what she wanted: it showed you the picture right away.

Berger uses the story to make a point about questions themselves. A naive "why" can open a path that an expert hasn't considered. Land knew far more about photography than his daughter did. That knowledge was exactly what made "film has to be developed" feel like the end of the matter. Her not knowing let her ask the question that his knowing had closed off.

Berger then widens the lens. Children, he notes, ask a great many questions. But schools and workplaces tend to reward answers rather than questions. People learn that the fast, correct explanation is what earns approval. Over time, the habit of asking "why can't it be different?" gets quietly trained out.

In that light, the most remarkable thing in the story isn't the chemistry or the engineering that came later. It's the moment Land heard a child's question, recognized that a perfectly good answer was available, and chose to take the question seriously anyway.

[[lessons]]
$$**A naive question can expose a hidden assumption.** "Why can't I see it now?" only sounds naive because everyone accepted that film must be developed. The child didn't know the rule, so she questioned it. Experts often can't ask that kind of question anymore, because the assumption has stopped looking like an assumption.$$

$$**Explaining and exploring are two different responses.** Land had two options: give the correct explanation, or treat the question as a problem. Both were reasonable. Only one led to instant photography. The difference wasn't in the question he heard but in what he decided to do with it.$$

$$**Answer-driven places train questions out.** Berger argues that schools and workplaces reward answers over questions. That helps explain why a three-year-old asked what adults didn't. If the only thing that earns credit is the right explanation, fewer people will ever stop to ask whether the explanation needs to be true.$$

[[reflect]]
- When did a child or a newcomer ask you something you answered too quickly?
- Which "that's just how it works" explanation do you give most often?
- Where are you rewarded for fast answers more than for good questions?
&&The next time someone asks "why can't we just...?", could you pause before explaining why not, and ask yourself what it would take for the answer to be yes?&&

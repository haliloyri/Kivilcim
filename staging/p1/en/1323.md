Someone from outside your field asks a question about your work. It's basic. Maybe a little naive. And before they've finished, you already have the reply ready: "It just doesn't work that way." Warren Berger suggests that reflex deserves a second look, because it may be your expertise talking, not the facts.

[[story]]
Here is the uncomfortable idea at the center of this part of Warren Berger's *A More Beautiful Question*. The more you know about a field, the fewer basic questions you tend to ask about it.

On the surface that seems backwards. Expertise should make you better at improving things. You understand the details, the history, the reasons behind each practice. And much of the time, that knowledge is exactly what you need.

But Berger points to a cost that builds up alongside it. As people gain expertise, they come to "know" how things work. Once you know, there's less reason to ask. The questions that a beginner would raise have already been answered in your head, often long ago.

That knowledge comes packaged with assumptions. Some are true and useful. Others were true once, or were never tested, or simply became the way things are done. Because they arrived as part of your expertise, they don't feel like assumptions. They feel like facts. And that can make certain possibilities hard to see at all.

This is where newcomers and outsiders come in. They ask questions that insiders often consider naive. Why do you do it in that order? Why does that step take so long? Why can't it be simpler?

##An insider hears those questions as a lack of knowledge, but Berger sees them as a way to expose assumptions the insiders stopped noticing.##

The newcomer isn't smarter. They just haven't absorbed the assumptions yet, so the assumptions are still visible to them.

Berger's recommendation is to borrow that view on purpose. He draws on the Zen idea of "beginner's mind": deliberately stepping back and looking at your own field as a beginner would. What would you ask if you were new here? What would seem strange?

He adds a second move. Ask how other fields have solved a similar problem. People in a different domain don't share your assumptions, so their solutions can suggest options your own field has ruled out without ever really considering them.

It's worth being clear about what Berger is not saying. He doesn't claim expertise is worthless or that beginners know better. The point is balance: knowledge and questioning have to be kept together, so that what you know doesn't quietly stop you from asking.

The practical challenge is that the imbalance is hard to notice from the inside. The assumptions that limit you most are the ones that don't feel like assumptions at all.

[[lessons]]
$$**Expertise can turn assumptions into "obvious."** Berger's point is that experts ask fewer basic questions because they already know the answers. The trouble is that some of those answers were never tested. Over time they stop looking like choices and start looking like the way things simply are.$$

$$**A naive question is information.** When a newcomer asks why something is done a certain way, the easy response is to explain. Berger suggests treating the question as a signal instead. It may be pointing at exactly the assumption the rest of the team has stopped seeing.$$

$$**You can borrow a beginner's eyes.** Beginner's mind is something you can practice, not only something you lose. Stepping back to look at your field as a newcomer would, and asking how other fields handle a similar problem, are two deliberate ways to get some of that fresh view back.$$

- Where do you say "that's how it's done" without checking whether it still holds?

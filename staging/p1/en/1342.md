It's 2004, and Dan Harris is reading the news headlines on *Good Morning America*, live, in front of millions of viewers. Staying composed on camera is a basic part of his job. Partway through, he can't get the next words out. The ABC News correspondent is having a panic attack on national television.

[[story]]
Harris was not someone you'd expect to fall apart on air. He was an ABC News correspondent and anchor, a professional skeptic whose work depended on staying steady while the cameras rolled. That's what made the moment so jarring. It happened in the one place his composure was supposed to be most reliable.

Afterward, he looked at what had led there. He had reported from war zones, and after coming home he had been using recreational drugs. A doctor pointed to that as a likely factor in the attack. The breakdown on screen, in other words, had roots in how he'd been living off screen.

The next turn came from an unlikely direction. Harris was assigned to cover religion. Through that beat he ran into ideas about "the voice in the head," the constant inner commentary most people never question, and eventually he ran into meditation.

He wasn't impressed. To him, meditation belonged to gurus and hippies, not to a hard-nosed newsman. It was the kind of thing he might report on with a raised eyebrow, not something he'd do himself.

##What changed his mind wasn't a mystical experience. It was evidence.## A growing body of scientific research on meditation, and teachers who explained it in practical, non-mystical terms, gave a skeptic something he could work with. He started practicing. Eventually he went on a silent meditation retreat.

The result he reports is notably modest. Meditation didn't make his anxiety disappear. What it gave him was a better ability to notice anxiety as it arrived, and not be swept along by it. He later put a number on the difference, and it was not a dramatic one: about ten percent happier.

That modesty is part of why the story holds up. It isn't a tale of a man cured overnight. It's a panic attack on live television, an honest look at what fed it, a practice he initially scoffed at, and a small, real change in how he handled the feelings that had once taken over mid-broadcast.

[[lessons]]
$$**A breakdown can start an honest look.** The panic attack happened in front of millions, yet it led Harris, with a doctor's help, to trace it back to his habits after the war zones. The visible moment became the reason to examine what had been building out of sight. The most visible moment of his career became a reason to look at the least visible part of his life.$$

$$**Skepticism can lead toward a practice.** Harris dismissed meditation as something for gurus and hippies. He didn't drop his skepticism; he applied it. Scientific research and practical teachers gave him reasons that met his own standard, and that's what got him to try it.$$

$$**Aim for a new relationship with anxiety.** His result wasn't the end of anxiety. It was noticing it sooner and being carried away by it less, which he summed up as about ten percent happier. A goal like that is one you can actually reach.$$

[[reflect]]
- Is there a practice you've dismissed mainly because of the kind of people you associate with it?
- When stress hits, do you usually notice it building, or only once it has taken over?
- What would count, for you, as a modest but real improvement in how you handle pressure?

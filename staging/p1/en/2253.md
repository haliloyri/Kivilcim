A canning plant, the night shift, a worker with long hours to fill. He writes about baseball, page after page, and wonders whether anyone would pay to read it. Nobody has asked him for a single word, and he has no idea how to get the pages in front of anyone.

[[story]]
Bill James's name is tied to the ideas behind Michael Lewis's *Moneyball*, the habit of understanding baseball by questioning its numbers instead of just watching games. This story is how James got started. He was born in Holton, Kansas, joined the army in 1971 and then studied at the University of Kansas. He worked night shifts at the Stokely-Van Camp pork-and-beans cannery in Lawrence, and wrote much of his early baseball work there.

In 1977 he published that work himself, as a 68-page booklet he called the *Baseball Abstract*. It was full of statistics. He had no publisher, no shop and no famous name. He placed a small ad in The Sporting News, and 75 people bought a copy.

Seventy-five is a small number for a baseball book. Each of those readers, though, had sent money to a stranger for a booklet they had not yet seen. Work that came off one man's table after a night shift had been judged worth paying for by people who had only seen a few lines of advertising. The second edition the following year sold 250 copies, more than three times as many. James kept doing the same thing, new numbers and new questions and another booklet, and the first five editions, 1977 to 1981, were all self-published.

By 1982 the readership had grown enough that a national publisher agreed to distribute the later editions. A booklet that began on night shifts now had a national publisher, which meant readers no longer had to find a small ad to get it.

Twenty-six years after that first booklet, in 2003, John Henry, the new owner of the Boston Red Sox and a former James reader, hired him as a senior adviser on baseball operations. The Red Sox won the 2004 World Series, their first title since 1918, and Lewis's book had come out the year before.

What stands out is how small the beginning was. James did not wait for a publisher to approve him. He printed what he had written, placed an ad, and watched how his first readers answered. The second edition was fed by that answer, and so were the ones after it. A man who never asked anyone's permission ended up advising a club.

[[lessons]]
$$**Show your work to a small audience before you feel it is ready, and then make the next edition.** Someone keeping notes on restaurants for their neighbourhood who sends the first pages to a building group learns at once which sentence confused people and which tip actually helped. The replies show exactly what the next version has to fix, and fixing it is far less tiring than starting from nothing each time. A small first run is the cheapest way to test an idea without leaving it alone, and the people who answer give you both corrections and a reason to carry on.$$

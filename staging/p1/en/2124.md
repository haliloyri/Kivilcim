A strip of dark seaweed is wrapped around a ball of rice and eaten whole. Somewhere in the gut of the person eating it, one bacterium holds a tool that most of its relatives lack. The tool was not invented in the gut. It was picked up from the sea.

[[story]]
Ed Yong, the author of *I Contain Multitudes*, reported this story in National Geographic in 2010. Jan-Hendrik Hehemann, then at the University of Victoria, led the work with Mirjam Czjzek, Gurvan Michel and colleagues, and it appeared in the journal *Nature*. It begins with a marine bacterium called *Zobellia galactanivorans*, which has five enzymes for breaking down porphyran, a sulphur-rich carbohydrate found in seaweed.

Hehemann went looking for similar genes elsewhere and found six. Five belonged to other marine bacteria. The sixth belonged to *Bacteroides plebeius*, a bacterium that lives in the human gut, which made the match strange: a gene for digesting seaweed sat inside an organism that had never lived in the sea. The team then screened the gut microbes of 13 Japanese volunteers and found porphyranase genes in them. Among 18 North Americans they found none.

The explanation lies in a food. Nori, the seaweed around sushi, is the only food people eat that contains porphyran. Traditionally Japanese people did not cook it before eating, so the marine bacteria living on the seaweed rode into the gut alive, and gut bacteria could swap genes with them. Genes that move sideways between unrelated bacteria, instead of down from parent to offspring, are what biologists call horizontal gene transfer. Once a gut bacterium could digest porphyran, the seaweed became a meal that other bacteria could not touch.

One detail pushes the idea further. An unweaned baby girl who had never eaten sushi carried a porphyranase gene, and so did her mother. This suggests the genes can be passed from parent to child, so a gene borrowed from the ocean once can travel on through families.

The researchers were careful about what this proves. Michel said, "This horizontal gene transfer remains a rare event," and among the Japanese volunteers only *B. plebeius* had picked up these enzymes. Keeping such a gene probably takes strong selection, such as daily access to seaweed as a food source, and weekly sushi would probably not supply that. Modern sushi uses roasted nori, so contact with marine bacteria is now unlikely. How long the genes have been in Japanese guts, and whether heavy sushi eating is needed to acquire them, remains unclear. The samples were small.

[[lessons]]
$$**Look at what you meet every day, because daily contact decides what stays.** A skill or habit that your surroundings supply every day is kept and grows, while one met only now and then fades, even when the first taste was good. A cook who tastes and adjusts the sauce at every meal learns far more than one who attends a weekly class and then says nothing about cooking for six days. Look at your own week and notice which abilities your daily surroundings keep feeding and which ones you only visit, since that tells you where your effort will actually stick.$$

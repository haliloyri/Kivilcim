One afternoon a man sits in a university library with a book on how to value a stock. The book gives a tidy rule, and the man reads it through. Then he stops, because the rule leaves out the one thing every investor lives with.

[[story]]
Harry Markowitz remembered that afternoon later, in his Nobel biography, and Peter Bernstein's *Against the Gods* counts his idea among the turning points in the history of risk. In his own account, the core of portfolio theory came to him one afternoon in the library, while reading John Burr Williams's *Theory of Investment Value*. Williams argued that a stock is worth the present value of the dividends it will pay. Simple to state, and neat to apply.

Markowitz saw what the rule left out: future dividends are not known in advance. Williams's tidy figure needed one number for each future payment, and those payments are uncertain. A rule built on them treated an uncertain stream as if it were a certain one.

The trouble went deeper. If investors cared only about the highest expected return, they would pour everything into the single stock that promised the most. Real investors do the opposite. They spread their money across many stocks, and Markowitz argued that this is sensible and not a mistake. He worked it out in a paper, "Portfolio Selection", in the *Journal of Finance* in 1952.

The paper set out what the old rule missed. Securities' returns are too closely tied to one another for the law of large numbers to cancel out the risk, so diversification cannot remove all the variance. Ten stocks that rise and fall together are hardly safer than one. Ten that move apart are. The portfolio with the highest expected return is not necessarily the one with the lowest variance. There is a rate at which an investor can gain expected return by taking on variance, or reduce variance by giving up expected return. Risk, in other words, had to be counted next to return and priced against it. A buyer could now ask what each extra unit of expected return would cost in extra uncertainty, and could answer with numbers.

According to published accounts of his thesis defence at the University of Chicago, in 1955, Milton Friedman half-jokingly suggested that portfolio theory was not economics. The theory outlived the objection. In 1990 Markowitz shared the Nobel prize in economics with Merton Miller and William Sharpe.

[[lessons]]
$$**Compare options on risk as well as on average return.** A family weighing two ways to save for a child's studies might look only at which one is expected to grow more. One of them swings widely from year to year and the other grows slowly and steadily, and the family's own situation decides which swing it can live with. If you ask how much risk each choice carries and whether the extra return pays for it, you stop chasing the best average and start choosing a trade-off you understand. The question applies equally to a job, a business plan and a household budget.$$

In some classrooms, nearly every answer sheet ends with the same long run of B's, or with B and C taking turns. Children who guess do not guess in step. Someone had filled in the blanks for them, and two economists realized that a pattern like this could be counted across a whole school system.

[[story]]
Brian Jacob and Steven Levitt, co-author of *Freakonomics*, had unusual material to work with: the question-by-question answers of every student in grades three to seven who took the Iowa Test of Basic Skills in the Chicago Public Schools between 1993 and 2000. Their final data set held more than 40,000 classroom-years of results and over 700,000 student-year records.

The stakes of those tests had gone up in 1996, when a new chief executive, Paul Vallas, began holding schools accountable. A school where fewer than 15 percent of students scored at or above national norms in reading went on probation and could be closed, with its staff dismissed or reassigned. Students in certain grades also needed minimum scores to move up a grade. Teachers and administrators told the researchers that probation was an extremely unwelcome place to be.

A teacher who cheats leaves two kinds of trace. The class jumps in score one year and falls back the next. And since the quickest way to change answers on the forms is to alter the same block of consecutive questions for many students, the sheets show runs of identical answers that chance would almost never produce. Either trace can appear innocently, so the authors built an algorithm that looks for both together.

Their estimate was that serious cheating by a teacher or administrator happened in at least 4 to 5 percent of elementary classrooms each year. The students in flagged classrooms lost most of their gains the following year. Students in classrooms with equally large gains but no suspicious answers kept most of theirs. A classroom flagged on the math test tended to be flagged on reading too, and flagged classrooms clustered within the same schools and repeated from year to year.

The cheating also followed the incentives. After 1996 it rose sharply in classrooms with many low-achieving students, the ones most exposed to probation. Classrooms with average or better students showed no increase.

The authors were careful to call their figure a minimum. When they altered answer sheets on purpose to test their own detector, it missed much of the moderate cheating, such as a teacher who changed only a few answers and avoided long blocks. The method catches the clumsy cases. The careful ones slip through.

[[lessons]]
$$**Look at what a rule rewards before you judge the people working under it.** A support team measured only on how many tickets it closes each day will sooner or later close some that are not solved, and nobody on it needs a dishonest character for that to happen, because the number pays for it. When someone's results jump, the useful question is how the measure could be reached without the thing it stands for. If you set targets, you can pair them with a second check that a shortcut would not satisfy, and if you receive them, you can say out loud where the number and the work come apart.$$

The rocket plane is ready, and so is the bomber that will carry it into the sky. Only one thing is missing: the pilot cannot close his own door. The hatch lever needs a hard pull with the right arm, and two of his ribs are broken.

[[story]]
Tom Wolfe's *The Right Stuff* follows the test pilots of the 1940s and 1950s, and Chuck Yeager stands among them. This story is the flight that made him famous. On 14 October 1947, over the dry lake bed at Muroc in California, Yeager was due to fly the Bell X-1, a bullet-shaped, rocket-powered research aircraft. He had named it Glamorous Glennis after his wife. The plan was to carry it up under a B-29 bomber, release it, and push it faster than sound.

Two days earlier Yeager had gone riding and fallen from his horse. According to the usual accounts he broke two ribs. The X-1 could not be entered casually. Its door was sealed from inside with a heavy locking lever, and closing it took a strong pull with the right arm. With his ribs broken he could not give that pull.

The fix was small and ordinary. A fellow pilot, Jack Ridley in most tellings, cut a length off a broom handle. Yeager used it as an extension, so he could work the lever with his left arm and leave the sore side alone. The hatch closed, the X-1 was lifted aloft, and it was dropped from the bomber.

Yeager lit the rocket engines and climbed. At around 43,000 feet he reached Mach 1.06, roughly 700 miles an hour, according to the Smithsonian's account. The needle went past the number everyone had feared, and the aircraft did not break apart. The flight itself was kept quiet for some time afterwards.

What stands out is the proportion between problem and answer. Engineers had studied the sound barrier for years, yet the biggest obstacle of that morning was a door he could not shut. The answer came from a hangar: a piece of broom handle, found and cut in a minute.

Look also at how Yeager handled the injury. He neither cancelled the flight nor pretended the pain away. He found the one thing the ribs prevented, and then the cheapest tool that gave it back. Everything else in the plan stayed as it was, because he matched the tool to the exact spot of the problem, not to its size. Big tasks usually jam at one small point, and that point looks trivial next to the whole.

[[lessons]]
$$**When a small obstacle blocks a big task, repair the obstacle with the plainest tool you have.** A broken rib was not going to be mended before the flight, but the single movement it prevented could be replaced. In daily work the same thing happens: a missing login, a colleague on leave or one broken step can seem to stop the whole project. Ask yourself which exact action is blocked, and what ordinary object or person could stand in for it today, however humble it looks. Often, then, the large plan survives untouched, and only the small step needs a broom handle.$$

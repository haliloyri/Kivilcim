Over the Christmas break, a petty officer stands watch on a half-empty submarine. A visitor in a jacket asks him a plain question: what does he do on this ship? The answer takes five words and tells the visitor more about the boat than any inspection report could.

[[story]]
In December 1998, L. David Marquet visited USS Santa Fe before taking command of it, and *Turn the Ship Around!* describes what he saw. The crew avoided his eyes. People kept telling him, one after another, that this was the worst submarine in the force.

During the Christmas break he asked a petty officer on watch what he did aboard. The man answered: "Whatever they tell me to do." Marquet heard in it a crew that had learned to wait.

In his first conversations he did not quiz people on things he already knew. He asked out of curiosity: what should he not change, and what got in the way of doing their jobs?

Taking command with 135 men aboard, he chose to replace none of them, because he believed the ship had a leadership problem rather than a people problem. He had about six months to get the boat ready for a deployment and a torpedo exercise with a battle group in the Arabian Gulf, and Commodore Kenny and his squadron staff were due aboard in eight days to begin a round of inspections.

So he asked his officers a practical question: how would they know the crew was proud of the ship? They said sailors would brag about Santa Fe to family and friends, including friends on other submarines. They would also look a visitor in the eye.

Marquet could not order pride into existence, but he could order a behaviour. Whenever a crew member met a visitor, he was to give three names: the visitor's, his own and the ship's. As the book gives it, the greeting went: "Good morning Commodore Kenny, my name is Petty Officer Smith. Welcome aboard Santa Fe."

Marquet's purpose was a test: if the action changed, would the thinking follow? He put it that way himself years later, in a talk to naval postgraduate students: the act precedes the thought, and the habit precedes the culture.

A greeting costs nothing and asks nothing of anyone's technical skill. It does force a sailor to lift his head, find the visitor's name and say his own, and it fixes in one sentence who he is and which ship he stands on.

[[lessons]]
$$**Behaviour you can start tomorrow beats a mood you cannot order.** Nobody can be told to feel proud, but anyone can be told how to greet a guest. A new team lead who wants a quiet group to speak up can begin with one small, visible habit, such as asking each person to open the weekly meeting with their own name for the next thing they will finish. The habit gives people a first step they can take today, and the attitude has something to grow from. If the habit feels a little awkward in its first week, that only shows it is new, and a small awkwardness is a fair price for a start.$$

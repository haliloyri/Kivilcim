[[story]]
General Motors ran its Fremont, California plant from 1962 until 1982, when it closed. It left behind a name as the industry's worst workforce. Relations between workers and management were so bad that absenteeism sometimes made it impossible to start the line. Coke bottles were put inside door panels.

A few years later the same factory reopened. This time it was a joint venture of Toyota and GM called NUMMI, and the people on the floor were mostly the same ones: over 85% of the workforce had worked at Fremont before. The same building, the same people, the same assembly line.

The first car came off the line in December 1984, a yellow Chevrolet Nova. In the years that followed, the Corollas built there reached defect levels similar to those of cars made in Japan. In 2006 the plant hit its peak output, 428,633 vehicles.

Then NUMMI closed too, on 1 April 2010. Tesla bought 210 of the site's 370 acres for $42 million, and the old car plant became a base for an electric-car maker.

What stands out is the list of things that stayed the same. The workers were the same, the building was the same, and the industry around them was the same. What changed was who ran the plant and how those people were treated. The outcome is on record: a workforce written off in 1982 reached defect levels close to Japan's with mostly the same people.

The timeline sharpens the picture. Fremont carried that reputation for twenty years, from 1962 to 1982. From the first Nova in December 1984 to the record year in 2006 is twenty-two years, and for most of that time the plant ran under the same name and the same partnership. A reputation that sat on the workers for two decades and then turned over leaves open the question of what it had been measuring all along.

It is easy to attach a bad result to the people who produced it. That was Fremont's reputation exactly. NUMMI is a documented case showing the reputation may have belonged to the system around the workers, not to the workers themselves.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you judge a team, look at how you treat it.** In a restaurant kitchen where the cooks are always late, in a shop where the sales staff are careless, or at home where the kids never help, people are often labelled as bad at the job or simply lazy. The more useful question is what conditions, what words and what expectations you bring to them every day. The same people can work very differently inside a different arrangement, and seeing that gives you the option of changing the arrangement before you try to change the person.$$

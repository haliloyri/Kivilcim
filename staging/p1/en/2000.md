[[open]]
Two researchers collect every study they can find in which people received feedback on their work and were then measured again. They end up with hundreds of results from thousands of people. The obvious expectation is that feedback helps, and the only open question is how much.

[[story]]
Avraham Kluger and Angelo DeNisi published their review in *Psychological Bulletin* in 1996. They pooled 607 effect sizes covering 23,663 observations. On average feedback did improve performance, with an effect size of 0.41, a moderate gain.

Then came the part that surprised the field: in more than a third of the cases, feedback made performance worse. The authors checked whether this could be explained away, and according to their abstract it could not be put down to sampling error, nor to whether the feedback was positive or negative. Praise and criticism both appeared among the failures.

So what separated the helpful cases from the harmful ones? Kluger and DeNisi proposed a theory with levels of attention. Feedback can pull your attention to the details of the task, to your motivation for the task, or up to the level of the self: what this says about me. The higher it pulls you toward the self and away from the task, the less it helps.

That is the same ground Stone and Heen cover when they describe the identity trigger. Feedback that is about the work gives you something to fix on Monday. Feedback that is heard as a ruling on who you are gives you something to defend, and defending uses the attention that the fixing would have needed.

Nobody has to be unkind for this to happen, either. Notice how ordinary the difference can be. "The second section of this report loses the reader" points at a paragraph. "You never think about the reader" points at a person. Both could be said with the same intent by the same manager in the same minute, and the receiver's mind will go to two different places.

The study does not tell you how to reply, but it does tell you where you are likely to go wrong. When a comment arrives, the risk lies less in its sign than in where it lands: on the task, or on you. As the receiver you cannot control how a colleague phrases things. You can choose where to point your own attention afterwards, and that choice is the one lever this research puts in your hands.

[[lessons]]
$$**When a comment lands on you, pull it back to the task by asking for the specific thing to change.** Kluger and DeNisi found that feedback pulling attention toward the self helps least, and over a third of all feedback interventions lowered performance. A remark like "you are careless" can be turned into something usable by asking which two lines in the report showed it and what a careful version would look like. After that question the comment sits on the document and no longer on you. You will not always get a clean answer, but you will move from defending yourself to editing a piece of work.$$

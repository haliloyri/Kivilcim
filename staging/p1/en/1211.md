Two men, both hardworking, both caring, both trying to teach a boy about money. One says: "I can't afford it." The other has banned that sentence from his house. In its place, he asks a question. Robert Kiyosaki built a bestselling book on the difference.

[[story]]
In *Rich Dad Poor Dad*, Robert Kiyosaki describes growing up in Hawaii with two father figures.

The first was his own father, whom he calls **poor dad**. He was highly educated, hardworking and respected. He worked in government and believed in the traditional path: study hard, get good grades, find a secure job with benefits, and work steadily until retirement.

The second was the father of his best friend, Mike. Kiyosaki calls him **rich dad**. He had left school early. But he built businesses and investments, and over time became very wealthy.

Both men gave young Robert advice about money. Often, it was completely opposite.

One small example became the most famous passage in the book. When poor dad saw something he wanted but that cost too much, he would say, "I can't afford it." It's a sentence most of us have said, and it sounds sensible, even responsible.

Rich dad didn't allow those words. Instead, he would ask, "How can I afford it?"

##Kiyosaki writes that the first is a statement, and the second is a question. One lets the mind rest. The other forces it to work.##

"I can't afford it" closes the discussion. There's nothing more to think about. "How can I afford it?" opens one. Could I earn more? Could I sell something? Could I start something that pays for it? Could I spend less somewhere else? Rich dad believed that asking the question, over and over, trained the mind to find opportunities that the statement never would.

This small example captures the book's larger message: our attitudes toward money are learned, usually at home, and they shape our choices for decades. School teaches many subjects, Kiyosaki argues, but rarely teaches how money actually works. Financial education, in his view, is as important as academic education.

It's worth adding a note of caution. Critics have questioned whether rich dad was one real person or a composite, and many financial experts disagree with parts of Kiyosaki's later investment advice. Rich dad's question also isn't a license to spend on everything; sometimes the honest answer to "how can I afford it?" is "I can't, and I shouldn't." But the core idea of the two dads remains useful: the words you use about money can either shut down your thinking or start it.

[[lessons]]
$$**A question keeps you thinking.** "I can't afford it" ends the conversation. "How can I afford it?" begins a search for options: earning more, spending differently, building something new. Kiyosaki's point is that the habit of asking the question trains your mind to notice possibilities that the statement hides.$$

$$**Money habits are learned at home.** The two dads gave the same boy opposite lessons. Most of us absorbed our money beliefs from the adults around us, often without noticing. Recognizing where your own money phrases came from makes it possible to keep the useful ones and change the rest.$$

$$**Use the question with honesty.** Critics rightly warn against reckless spending or unrealistic schemes. "How can I afford it?" works best as an honest problem-solving question, not a way to justify every purchase. Sometimes the answer really is "not now," and that's a thoughtful conclusion, not a shut-down one.$$

The countdown on Omelek Island has run four times now, and this rocket carries no customer's satellite at all, only a weight. When the first stage falls away and the second-stage engine lights, the crew watches a screen for something they have never once seen: a line that keeps climbing.

[[story]]
RatSat was not a satellite. It was a 165-kilogram mass simulator, a block built to weigh what a satellite weighs, and on 28 September 2008 it sat on top of the fourth Falcon 1 to leave Omelek Island. The three flights before it had ended in a fuel-line fire, a second-stage oscillation and a collision between stages, and the third had lost several small customer payloads. If this one failed, nobody's instrument would be lost with it. The team was testing its own rocket, not someone else's hardware.

This time the first stage separated cleanly. The second-stage engine ran, the rocket kept climbing, and after a second burn it settled into an orbit of about 621 by 643 kilometres at an inclination of 9.35 degrees. Falcon 1 had reached orbit at last. Getting there took two burns of the second-stage engine, one to climb and one to set the orbit.

Walter Isaacson's *Elon Musk* tells the story of the man behind this rocket and his companies. Musk said later that this flight was the last money he had for Falcon 1. A fourth failure would probably have ended the rocket programme, so the result carried more weight than any single launch normally does.

Three months later, on 23 December 2008, NASA announced its commercial resupply contracts for the International Space Station. SpaceX was awarded 1.6 billion dollars for twelve Dragon flights, and Orbital Sciences 1.9 billion for eight Cygnus flights. Published records do not present the award as a direct reward for that one flight, but the order is plain: first an orbit, then NASA. A company that had never reached orbit was now on the list to carry cargo to a space station.

What is worth noticing is what the fourth flight did not carry. Instead of risking a customer's satellite again, the team let the test measure a single thing: can this rocket reach orbit? The answer to that one question became the strongest piece of evidence in the room for everything that followed. When you remove the clutter, the measurement gets clearer, and a clear measurement is the kind that persuades other people. A failed flight would have burned a weight and left only the rocket's fault on the record, and a successful one left an orbit nobody could argue with.

[[lessons]]
$$**Pick the one result that will convince people, and load the test with only what that result needs.** For a new product or process, the proof that matters is often a single yes or no: does it work? Customer data, a full feature list and polish blur that question, and if something breaks it gets harder to tell what broke it. The fourth flight carried a weight instead of a payload that could be lost, and a single thing was measured; once it succeeded, the conversations that followed, NASA's announcement three months later among them, had a solid starting point. Build a small but complete proof first, then scale it.$$

At 65, after several bypass operations, a woman was sent home in a wheelchair with end-stage heart disease. Her grandson's phrase for it is that she was sent home to die. Then she watched a television programme about a man who claimed to have beaten the same illness with food.

[[story]]
Michael Greger tells the story of his grandmother, Frances, in the introduction to *How Not to Die*. According to Greger, she had already had several bypass surgeries by the time she was 65. Then she was sent home, confined to a wheelchair with end-stage heart disease, to die.

In the late 1970s she saw Nathan Pritikin on the television programme *60 Minutes*. Pritikin was an engineer who promoted a low-fat, plant-based diet with regular exercise as a way to deal with heart disease. She sought him out and took up his programme.

She adopted a strict plant-based, low-fat diet and started walking. In Greger's telling, she eventually walked up to ten miles a day. He credits the combined changes with improving the blood flow to her heart muscle, and says they gave her her life back.

She lived another 31 years and died at 96. Thirty-one years is long enough for medicine, habits and plain luck to change many times over, which is part of why the number is striking and part of why it is hard to interpret.

This is one person, told by her grandson, and the details reach us without a medical record, so the account cannot show what caused what. It is moving and hard to measure at once. Nobody can say how much each part of the change mattered, or what the same woman's years would have looked like without it. A story like this has one real strength and one real limit. It shows what is possible for a single person, and it cannot tell you how often that outcome happens.

How often is a question for studies of groups. A randomised trial that follows 48 coronary patients against a control group, or a follow-up of 198 patients with its own gaps, tries to answer what a family anecdote cannot. Those studies are smaller and duller than a woman leaving her wheelchair behind, and they are also the only way to find out whether her recovery was typical, lucky or somewhere between.

The honest way to hold Frances's story is therefore with both hands: as the reason to ask the question, and as nothing more than that until the numbers have spoken.

[[lessons]]
$$**Let a vivid story raise the question, then go and find the data before you answer it.** The wheelchair and the walking make the account memorable, and memorable is exactly the quality that makes a story feel like proof. When a friend's recovery or a colleague's success tempts you to copy what they did, treat it as a lead: look for groups, comparisons and the people it did not work for. You keep the motivation the story gave you and lose only the false certainty. Then ask one more thing of the person telling it: what else changed in those same years?$$

[[open]]
A man with a serious illness asked that, after he died, doctors cut open his body and look at one organ. The point was to show what his diet could do. Doctors who were not him would look, and their findings would later appear in a medical journal.

[[story]]
Nathan Pritikin was an engineer, not a doctor. According to Michael Greger, who tells his story in a video on NutritionFacts.org, Pritikin was diagnosed with heart disease in his forties and refused to accept the fatalistic view that medicine took of it at the time.

Pritikin read up on how people ate in different parts of the world, Uganda among them, and settled on a plant-based diet. Greger says it lowered his cholesterol from 280 to 94 and reversed his heart disease. That figure comes from Greger's account and not from a paper he cites.

Pritikin also lived with leukemia. He died in February 1985, at the age of 69. Before he died, Greger says, he asked that his body be autopsied to show what his diet could do.

The findings appeared in the *New England Journal of Medicine* in July 1985, in a report titled "Nathan Pritikin's Heart" by Hubbard, Inkeles and Barnard. Greger describes it this way: about thirty years after the diagnosis, at a time when the condition was thought of as incurable, Pritikin's coronary arteries were soft, flexible and open throughout. He quotes the report: "In a man 69 years old, the near absence of atherosclerosis and complete absence of its effects are remarkable."

It is a striking image, and it has clear limits. An autopsy captures one moment. It cannot say what the same arteries looked like before the diet, or what they would have looked like without it. It describes one man, and in one man a remarkable result might also reflect his genes, his luck or other things nobody measured.

There is a second thing the episode shows, and it concerns how a claim is made. Pritikin's idea would have stayed a private belief if it had stayed inside his own account of himself. By asking for a post-mortem examination he made it possible for the findings to be written up by others in a medical journal, where people who disagreed could read the details. The result does not settle whether his diet works for everyone. It does show a claim that ended up open to inspection by strangers, which is a different thing from asking them to trust him.

[[lessons]]
$$**Put your claim where someone who disagrees with you can check it.** Believing something strongly gives it no extra weight, and an account of yourself is only as good as the record behind it. At work this means writing down the measure before the project starts, and at home it means asking a relative who doubts you to look at the same evidence. A claim made checkable invites correction, and correction is how you find out whether you were right. The sceptic who reads your evidence is doing you a favour, since only that reader can tell you what your confidence is missing.$$

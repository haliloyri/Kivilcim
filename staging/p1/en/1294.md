Look at the cover of *The 4-Hour Workweek*. That title was not picked by an editor's gut feeling or a brainstorm in a meeting room. Tim Ferriss ran candidate titles as small Google search ads and kept the one strangers clicked most. It is a strange way to name a book, and it is exactly the method the book teaches.

[[story]]
In *The 4-Hour Workweek* (2007), Tim Ferriss makes an argument that sounds obvious once you hear it and is rarely followed: find out whether people will pay for a product before you make it.

The usual path runs the other way. Someone has an idea, tells a few friends, hears that it sounds great, and starts building. Friends are kind. They say yes to ideas because they like you. That enthusiasm costs them nothing and tells you very little.

Ferriss wants a different kind of signal. ##Replace opinion, including friends' polite enthusiasm, with measured behavior from strangers.## Strangers have no reason to be nice. If they click and try to buy, that means something.

His method is simple. Build a basic web page that describes the product. Buy small search ads on Google AdWords that point to it. Then measure how many people click, and how many go further and try to order.

The clever part is what happens at checkout. Visitors who try to buy are not charged. They are told the item is unavailable. That way the test captures real intent, a stranger reaching for their wallet, without selling something that does not exist yet.

The book is firm about scale. Keep the test small in both money and time. The point is that a bad idea should cost you very little to discover. If nobody clicks, you have lost a modest ad budget and a few days, not a warehouse of stock or a year of your life.

Then Ferriss turned the same tool on his own book. He had several possible titles. Instead of debating them, he ran each as a Google search ad and compared how often people clicked. The winner went on the cover: *The 4-Hour Workweek*.

That detail makes the method easy to remember. It is not only for gadgets or supplements. Words can be tested too: a title, a headline, the name of an event. Anywhere you would normally rely on your own taste or a friend's opinion, you can often find a cheap way to let strangers' behavior decide.

The tension the method resolves is a common one. Everyone who hears your idea says it sounds good, yet the only signal that matters is whether strangers act. Finding that out usually seems to require building the thing first. Ferriss's answer is that it often doesn't.

[[lessons]]
$$**Watch what strangers do, not what friends say.** Ferriss measured clicks and attempted orders from people who arrived through ads and owed him nothing. That is a different kind of evidence from a friend saying "I'd totally buy that." Behavior costs something; praise usually doesn't. The people you most want to hear from are the ones who don't know you at all.$$

$$**Test before you build, and keep it cheap.** A simple page and a small ad budget come before any manufacturing. The goal is not to prove the idea right but to make being wrong affordable. A test that costs too much to fail stops being a test, and starts being a bet you feel obliged to defend.$$

$$**Words can be tested too.** The book's own title came from comparing how often people clicked each candidate ad. Titles, names and headlines usually get settled by taste or by whoever argues longest. A small test lets strangers settle it instead.$$

A small piece of machined steel sits on a writer's desk and holds down his papers. It is a rocket nozzle, made in a coal mine's machine shop. For years someone else looked after it, and the man who did was not known for saying what he felt.

[[story]]
The nozzle belongs to Homer Hickam, the author of *Rocket Boys*, and the man who kept it was his father, also named Homer. Hickam writes that the nozzle was built in the machine shops of Coalwood, West Virginia, and that it sat on his desk as a paperweight. It led to the magazine article that grew into the memoir.

His father was the superintendent of the Coalwood mine and the town's leader. In Hickam's account, he believed Coalwood would die if its children left. His father, grandfather and great-grandfather, on both sides of the family, had all been coal miners. Sonny, the son who built rockets, was heading somewhere else.

Hickam has been plain about the relationship. In an interview he said, "Dad loved his first son, but was ambivalent about the second (me)." He described his father as distant, like many fathers of the time, who "finally gave in, if only a little."

The proof, as Hickam tells it, is in what his father did rather than what he said. Hickam's reading guide notes that his father arranged materials for the rocket work even while seeming to oppose it. The guide also says he took Sonny into the mine despite his wife Elsie's objections. The mine's machinists made the nozzles and casings, so the skills of the mine's own shop went into the boys' rockets.

The mine was the father's workplace, and it stayed a fixture in the son's plans too. Hickam says he worked in the coal mines during summers to raise extra money for college, with help from his parents. The guide adds that in the memoir's final rocket launch, his father is invited to launch the last rocket. The man who kept the nozzle was present for the finish.

His mother had her own reasons for distrusting the mine. Hickam says she hated the coalfields, partly because her younger brother had died and she blamed a company that provided no doctor for miners' families. Mother and father saw the mine differently, and each, in a different way, stood behind the rockets.

Hickam writes that his father kept the nozzle for him over the years, and that it came back to him after his father died. His father died of black lung, in 1989, while Hickam was away on vacation. The steel on the desk is the object that outlasted the man who kept it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Reluctant support shows up as small acts long before it shows up as praise.** A parent, boss or partner who never says "well done" may still be arranging materials, saving objects or opening doors for you, and you can miss all of it if you listen only for words. Hickam spent years wanting approval from a man who, by his own account, gave it in kept objects and arranged materials. This week, look at what the hard-to-read person in your life actually does for your work, and thank them for exactly that. Naming the act aloud lets them hear the thing they were never going to say.$$

[[open]]
A government lawyer lifts a phone and asks a clearing house one plain question: has this famous investment firm really been making the trades that its statements report? The answer takes a few days, and the lawyer has had the means to ask it for years.

[[story]]
After Bernie Madoff's firm collapsed in December 2008, an SEC enforcement attorney testified that confirming Madoff had not traded his investors' money took a few days and a phone call to the Depository Trust Company, the clearing house through which US stock trades settle. Madoff was arrested and charged with securities fraud on 11 December 2008. On 29 June 2009 Judge Denny Chin sentenced him to 150 years in prison, the statutory maximum.

Malcolm Gladwell's *Talking to Strangers* discusses Madoff, and the part worth retelling is how long he went unchallenged by the regulator whose job was to challenge him. The SEC's own inspector general reviewed the record, and his roughly 450-page report, published in 2009, counted six substantive complaints about Madoff between June 1992 and December 2008.

Over that time the SEC carried out three examinations and two investigations. The report's verdict was blunt: despite all of them, a thorough and competent investigation or examination was never performed. Staff did not seek verification from independent third parties, and some of the examiners lacked trading experience; one team consisted entirely of attorneys with general litigation backgrounds.

One detail shows what verification would have found. A January 2005 statement for one of the funds that fed money to Madoff showed $2.5 billion in S&P 100 stocks, while the DTC's records showed less than $18 million in Madoff's account there. The gap sat in plain numbers, waiting for anyone who compared the two.

The outside analyst Harry Markopolos had been pushing the same doubts for years. According to the inspector general's report, an SEC branch chief took an instant dislike to him and declined a folder on Madoff that was several inches thick. In 2005 Markopolos wrote a report titled "The World's Largest Hedge Fund is a Fraud."

The inspector general said the SEC had missed the chance to uncover the scheme sixteen years before Madoff confessed. Nothing in this account suggests that the examiners were fools. They were looking at a respected figure with polished paperwork, and the check that would have settled the question was cheap and quick, yet no one made it. The DTC's records belonged to an institution with no stake in Madoff's story, and that is what made them a different kind of evidence from his own statements.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask for the cheap independent confirmation before you rely on a polished statement.** A statement, a reputation and a confident explanation all come from the same source, so they can only repeat one another. Say you are choosing a contractor who shows you a glossy portfolio: a single call to a past client tells you more than another hour with the brochure, because that client has nothing to sell you. The check is usually small, it rarely offends anyone who is honest, and making it early lets you keep trusting people you have confirmed instead of people who merely sound sure.$$

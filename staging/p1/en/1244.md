A student at Oxford is handed a reading list long enough to fill several lifetimes. Most of his classmates try to read as much of it as they can, and feel constantly behind. He tries something different. He decides not to read most of it at all.

[[story]]
Richard Koch describes this moment in *The 80/20 Principle* as the first time he noticed the principle at work in his own life.

As a student at Oxford, he faced the usual challenge: an enormous amount of material and limited time before exams. The obvious approach was to work through the reading list as thoroughly as possible, covering everything a little.

But Koch noticed something about the exams themselves. They didn't test everything on the list. They tested a limited range of topics, and the same kinds of questions came up again and again. Past exam papers showed the pattern clearly.

##So instead of covering everything thinly, Koch covered a few areas deeply.##

He studied past papers to see what was actually rewarded. He concentrated on the key topics that came up most often and on the kinds of answers that earned the best marks. He learned those areas thoroughly. Much of the reading list he simply left aside.

The approach worked. He did well, while working less than many classmates who had tried to do it all.

Later in his career, Koch found the same pattern everywhere. In consulting, a small share of clients and projects generated most of the value. In business, a few products produced most of the profit. In investing, a handful of decisions made most of the returns. Each time, the lesson was the same: results are rarely spread evenly across effort. Find where they really come from, and put your energy there.

Koch is careful to say that this isn't about laziness. Selectivity often means working very hard, but on the right things. The student who studies a few topics deeply may work intensely during those hours. What he avoids is the exhausting, low-value effort of trying to cover everything to the same shallow standard.

The broader point is that many of us approach life like an overwhelming reading list. We try to do every task, answer every message, learn every skill, and feel permanently behind. Koch suggests a different question. What is your version of the past exam paper? What shows you clearly what is actually rewarded? Once you know that, you can stop trying to do everything, and start doing what matters well.

[[lessons]]
$$**Study where results come from.** Koch looked at past exam papers to see what was actually rewarded, rather than trying to cover everything. In most areas of life there's a similar signal, like feedback, results or data, showing what truly counts. Finding it lets you aim your effort precisely.$$

$$**Depth beats thin coverage.** Knowing a few topics thoroughly served Koch better than knowing everything a little. Spreading effort evenly feels responsible, but it often produces mediocre results everywhere. Going deep where it matters usually produces far better results than going wide.$$

$$**Selectivity isn't laziness.** Koch's approach still required intense work, just on fewer things. The 80/20 principle isn't a license to do less of everything. It's permission to stop doing low-value work so you can do high-value work properly, with full attention. Fewer things, done properly, usually win.$$

[[open]]
Two sites sit about 400 yards apart, close enough to shout across. Yet a file sent from one to the other crawls at 40 bits per second, and the people running the machines cannot see what is broken. Every computer involved is doing exactly what it was built to do.

[[story]]
In October 1986 the data throughput from Lawrence Berkeley Laboratory to the University of California at Berkeley dropped from 32 kilobits per second to 40 bits per second. The authors called it a sudden factor-of-thousand drop. Van Jacobson, at the laboratory, was fascinated and set out to learn why. He and Michael Karels of Berkeley wrote up what they found. The result, "Congestion Avoidance and Control", was presented at a conference in 1988. Ideas like this are the kind Brian Christian and Tom Griffiths gather in *Algorithms to Live By*, a book on computer science and human decisions.

It was the first of what became a series of congestion collapses. Their conclusion was that much of the cause lay in how the protocol was implemented, not in the protocol itself. The obvious ways to build a window-based sender, they found, could produce exactly the wrong behaviour in response to congestion.

The idea they built on is plain. In a connection running steadily, a new packet should not enter the network until an old one has left it. The authors called this the conservation of packets, and they wrote that if it were obeyed, congestion collapse would become the exception rather than the rule. The acknowledgments coming back act as a clock, because they cannot return faster than data gets through.

But that clock has a start-up problem: to get acknowledgments you need data flowing, and to get data flowing you need acknowledgments. Their answer was slow-start. When a connection starts, or restarts after a loss, it is allowed one packet. Every acknowledgment for new data lets it send one more. The window opens quickly, doubling with each round trip, but never in one violent burst.

The authors described what happens without this care. When a 10 Mbps Ethernet host talked across a 56 Kbps Arpanet link, the first gateway saw a burst of eight packets arriving at about 200 times the path's bandwidth. That burst often pushed the connection into a persistent failure mode of continuous retransmissions.

Slow-start had a name before it had its final one. Jacobson presented the idea at an Internet Engineering Task Force meeting in February 1987 as soft-start, after an electronics technique that limits inrush current. John Nagle suggested slow-start in a message to the mailing list a month later, and they adopted it at once. By the time of the paper, the group had added seven new algorithms to the Berkeley TCP, with slow-start among them.

[[lessons]]
$$**When a shared channel is jammed, start again with a single small step and widen only as acknowledgments come back, because a flood of retries deepens the jam.** Think of a group chat after a long silence: everyone replies at once, nobody reads, and the thread becomes unusable. Slow-start suggests that the way back is one message, one confirmed answer, then a little more, and that the pace should follow the replies, not your impatience. You can use that whenever a team, an inbox or a household is overloaded: send less, wait for the sign that it landed, and then raise the pace again.$$

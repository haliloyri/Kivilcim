Six feet. Thirty-one thousand feet. Those are the heights of the first and the last rocket in a numbered series of handmade rockets built by teenagers in a coal town. Between them lie homemade fuels, borrowed machine shops and a lot of arithmetic done on scrap paper.

[[story]]
In *Rocket Boys*, Homer Hickam describes how the Big Creek Missile Agency went from toys to hardware. According to the Publishers Weekly review, the rockets were numbered Auk I through Auk XXXI, and the series climbed from six feet in the air to 31,000 feet. Hickam later said the whole effort took three years of development.

The fuel changed along the way. The boys began with black powder, moved on to what the review calls rocket candy, melted potassium chlorate and sugar, and then reached a mixture they called Zincoshine, made of zinc, sulfur and moonshine. Each change in fuel meant a new set of risks and a new result to read.

The boys needed money and equipment, and the Christian Science Monitor lists how they found them. They sold scrap metal and wild ginseng. Old telephones became a communications system for the launch site. Launches took place on an abandoned dump that the boys named Cape Coalwood. Machinists at the mine made the casings and nozzles, so the best metalworking in town ended up inside a teenagers' project.

The thinking mattered as much as the metal. Quentin Wilson, one of the six boys and later an engineer, taught Hickam a crude scientific method that he called the "body of knowledge", as Hickam told ASME's magazine. The boys also taught themselves trigonometry to measure how far the rockets flew. A number on a page decided whether a change counted as an improvement.

Hickam adds that the real rockets were more sophisticated than the film *October Sky* shows. By the end, he says, the last ones went supersonic within half a second of ignition.

That habit is simple enough for anyone to borrow. Seen from outside, the sequence looks like talent. Seen from inside, it looks like discipline. Number each rocket, change one thing, launch it, measure it, and put the result next to the number. A numbered series keeps its failures on the record, and that record is what lets you compare Auk XXXI with Auk I at all. Without the numbers, six feet and 31,000 feet would be two unrelated stories. With them, they are one long climb.

[[lessons]]
$$**Give each attempt a number and a measurement, and your failures turn into a ladder.** Progress that is hard to see feels like standing still, so people often quit one step before the improvement would have shown. A record of what you changed and what the result was lets you tell a real gain from a good day, and it shows which changes were worth keeping. Choose one thing you keep redoing, such as a pitch, a recipe or a training run, number the versions and write down one measurable result after each one, even when the result is bad.$$

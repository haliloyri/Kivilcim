[[open]]
In a pharmaceutical laboratory, technicians prepare a cell culture from a fresh monkey kidney. It is routine work: the organ is prepared, the cells are grown, the result goes into vaccine production. Nobody at the bench knows that something else has arrived with the tissue.

[[story]]
The name Marburg belongs to a German town, and the virus carries it because the first outbreak that science recognised broke out there in 1967. At the same time cases appeared in Frankfurt, and in Belgrade in Yugoslavia. This is the first member of the virus family that Richard Preston writes about in *The Hot Zone*.

According to the CDC, 31 people fell ill and seven died, and later blood tests identified one further primary case. What the patients had in common was contact with African green monkeys imported from Uganda. The animals had been brought in for the pharmaceutical industry, and their kidneys were used to make primary cell cultures. Most of the sick had either prepared those cultures, taken part in post-mortems of infected animals, or handled the animals' blood and organs directly.

One more detail mattered. Some people who had never touched the animals also fell ill. They had been in close contact with patients, which showed that the virus could pass from person to person. Shipments from the same animal source had reached all three cities, so cases appearing far apart had one root.

The striking thing is where the problem came from. The laboratories worked within their own walls, and nobody had expected a consignment of imported animals to carry an unknown disease. The danger arrived from outside the known limits of the work, from the first link in the supply chain. A useful job, making vaccines, depended on an animal source, and so it was exposed to everything that source carried.

Tracing the disease meant looking for what the patients had in common, and the common point was the shipment. Every laboratory had its own procedures, but the risk was shared by three separate places that drew on the same source, and it became visible only when the cases were laid side by side.

Making a job safe does not end with checking your own steps. You also need to know where your materials come from and what they carried on the way. Even a useful job inherits the risk of its inputs.

Moreover, such risks cannot be seen by anyone working inside, because every link along the way has done its own share of the work correctly. The laboratories at Marburg were not at fault. What was missing was a question about where the material had come from, one that nobody had yet asked.

[[lessons]]
$$**Know where your inputs come from, and ask what they carried on the way to you.** The laboratories in Marburg were careful in their own procedures, but the risk had been born outside them, in the first link of the supply chain. Data you receive at work, a part that arrives for a home project or a ready-made component in your software can carry something beyond your control in the same way. Write down the source of each input, and question its journey once, before anything goes wrong. The question is cheapest when it is asked before the work starts.$$

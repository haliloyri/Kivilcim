Every day at the same hour, screens light up and thousands of people listen to the same video call. Senior commanders are on it, and so are members of small units in the field. There was a time when only the most senior leaders were allowed into this meeting, and everyone treated that as normal.

[[story]]
At the center of Stanley McChrystal's *Team of Teams*, written with Tantum Collins, David Silverman and Chris Fussell, is "shared consciousness": everyone in an organization seeing the same picture. In their TIME article the authors say it began in 2004, when the "need-to-know" system in the special operations task force in Iraq stopped working, because nobody could predict who would need which piece of information.

Their answer was to share widely. The task force's motto became "share until it hurts, then share some more." If you do not know who will need what, giving information only to the people you think need it risks leaving out the one who does. The most visible tool was a daily video update. According to the authors, these updates had once been limited to senior leaders, and at times thousands of people joined. Published accounts call it the Operations and Intelligence update and put its length at roughly ninety minutes to two hours, with attendance anywhere from a few thousand to seven thousand depending on the source, so "thousands" is the safest figure.

The meeting served to carry decision power to where the information was. The authors' second principle is "empowered execution": decisions move to the people closest to a problem and approval chains get shorter. But giving someone the right to decide means little unless they know what is going on. The meeting was meant to let teams far apart see the same picture on the same day. What one team learned, another could fold into its own decision without waiting for permission or explanation.

McChrystal's own comparison is that in a small team of four, five or six, you have shared consciousness automatically. The challenge was to rebuild that in a structure of thousands. The video call stretched the effect of talking face to face over a far larger crowd instead of replacing it.

The question the meeting poses is plain: who gets the information, for what reason, and how late?

[[lessons]]
$$**When you decide who hears something, think about who could use it.** If a head chef tells the new menu only to the kitchen assistant, the servers recommend dishes that are no longer served and a misunderstanding lands at the table. When several people see the same picture, they can make their own decisions from it, and you are no longer needed to approve every one. If you cannot predict who needs the information, sharing widely is usually cheaper than explaining afterwards. Put it where everyone can see it first, and then look at who actually benefits, adjusting the circle once you have seen how the information was used.$$

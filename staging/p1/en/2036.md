At one auction the most any single buyer may take is 35 percent of the issue. A firm submits one bid in its own name for exactly that share. Then it submits more bids in other names. Each piece looks separate on paper. Added together, they sail well past the limit.

[[story]]
On Friday, 16 August 1991, the chairman and the president of Salomon Brothers, John Gutfreund and Thomas Strauss, announced that they would resign. The board was also to consider the position of the vice chairman, John Meriwether, who according to published accounts had run Salomon's bond arbitrage group in the early 1980s. Warren Buffett, who owned 13 percent of the firm, was ready to serve as interim chairman and chief executive.

Meriwether is the man who, as the Federal Reserve's history records, founded Long-Term Capital Management in February 1994, the fund at the centre of Roger Lowenstein's *When Genius Failed*. To see where he came from, look at what went wrong at Salomon.

The trouble lay in two Treasury auctions, as the press reported at the time. In December 1990 Salomon submitted a bid for 35 percent of the issue for its own account and, without authorization, a 1 billion dollar bid in a customer's name. Together the bids amounted to 46 percent of the issue, against a maximum allowable purchase of 35 percent.

In February 1991, at an auction of five-year notes, the firm submitted three bids of 35 percent each: one for itself and two for customers who had not authorized them. It ended up buying 57 percent of the issue.

In their own statement, Gutfreund and Strauss said they were resigning to give the board maximum flexibility and to protect the firm, its 9,000 employees and its clients. The New York Fed said it was closely reviewing developments at the firm. In December 1992, according to press reports of the SEC settlement, Meriwether was suspended from the securities business for three months.

The shares are the plain part of the story, so set the two auction figures side by side: 46 percent, then 57 percent, against a limit of 35. That alone does not tell us what any individual knew or intended. It does show that the overshoot came back, and larger, once a limit had already been bent.

The cost did not stay at the bidding desk. It reached the top of the firm within months, and the man who would later found LTCM was among those whose position the board had to consider.

[[lessons]]
$$**When a limit is crossed, name it at once and in the open.** A spending cap, a safety rule or a deadline bent once makes the second bend easier, and it often goes further. Letting the first one pass quietly leaves the size of the next one unknown. Saying "we crossed this limit" at the first breach, while the number is still small, protects you and the team, because a written record keeps a closed-looking matter open. Say it as a plain, short, dated note rather than an accusation, and state in the same sentence where the limit stands and why it sits there.$$

[[story]]
How do you test an experience that people can feel? The researchers at Johns Hopkins faced exactly that problem in the study Roland Griffiths and colleagues published in the journal *Psychopharmacology* in 2006, which sits in the modern research that Michael Pollan describes in *How to Change Your Mind*. If volunteers know they have taken the real thing, their expectations alone can colour what they report afterwards.

The team recruited 36 adults. All were new to hallucinogens, and all said they took part regularly in religious or spiritual activities. Each volunteer attended two or three sessions, two months apart. In most cases the sessions held either psilocybin (30 mg per 70 kg of body weight) or an active comparison compound, methylphenidate, in an order that was counterbalanced across the group. That covered 30 people. For the other six the team added a twist: they received methylphenidate in the first two sessions and psilocybin, openly, in the third. Those six were there only to obscure the design, so that nobody could assume that a given session must contain the drug.

Each session lasted eight hours and was held individually, in comfortable and supportive conditions. Volunteers were encouraged to close their eyes and direct their attention inward, and study monitors rated their behaviour. The volunteers filled in questionnaires about the drug's effects and about mystical experience immediately afterwards and again two months later. Then came the step that gives the study much of its weight: community observers, people who knew the volunteers, rated changes in their attitudes and behaviour.

The results, as the abstract reports them, were mixed in a way that sounds honest. Psilocybin produced a range of perceptual changes and shifting moods, including anxiety. It also raised the measures of mystical experience. At two months, the volunteers rated the experience as personally meaningful and spiritually significant, and the observers' ratings pointed the same way. A follow-up paper on the same 36 people found that, 14 months later, 58 percent still ranked the experience among the five most personally meaningful of their lives and 67 percent among the five most spiritually significant.

It matters who these volunteers were. They were screened adults who already cared about spirituality, so the result tells us about that group and not about everyone. What the study shows more generally is a way of asking: use a comparison that also does something to the body, hide the structure from the people inside it, and ask outsiders to confirm what the insiders say.

[[lessons]]
$$**Check what you say about yourself against someone who sees you from the outside.** The Hopkins team did not stop at the volunteers' own ratings of meaning and change; it asked community observers whether they had seen the change too. You can borrow this in a small way. After a new routine, a course or a difficult conversation, ask a colleague or friend what they actually noticed, not whether it went well. If their account matches yours, your confidence has earned its place, and if it differs, you have learned something a mirror could never show you.$$

Most of us plan our days by asking *what* needs doing. Daniel Pink thinks we're neglecting an equally important question: *when*. Your mind at nine in the morning and your mind at three in the afternoon behave in surprisingly different ways.

[[story]]
In *When*, Daniel Pink gathers research from psychology, biology and economics about how timing affects performance. One of his central findings is that most people's days follow a predictable pattern with three stages.

First comes a **peak**. For most people, it's in the morning. Mood tends to rise, and the ability to focus, concentrate and avoid distractions is at its highest.

Then comes a **trough**, usually in the early to mid-afternoon. Energy drops. Mood dips. Concentration becomes harder. Mistakes become more likely.

Finally, there's a **recovery**, or rebound, in the late afternoon and early evening. Mood lifts again, though focus doesn't fully return to its morning level.

Not everyone runs on exactly the same schedule. Pink describes three **chronotypes**. **Larks** wake early and feel sharpest in the morning. **Owls** come alive later and may experience the pattern roughly in reverse. And most people are what he calls **third birds**, somewhere in between, following the peak-trough-recovery pattern fairly closely.

##The key, Pink argues, is matching the task to the time.##

Tasks that require analysis, careful attention and focus, like writing a report, checking numbers or making a complex decision, tend to go best during your peak, when your mind is most vigilant.

Insight tasks are different. Solving a problem that needs a creative leap, coming up with fresh ideas or seeing a situation from a new angle can actually go better during the recovery period. Your mind is a little looser then, a little less focused and less inhibited. That can make it easier for unexpected connections to form.

And the trough? That's the time for administrative tasks that don't need much brainpower: routine email, filing, simple errands.

Pink shows that timing doesn't only affect how we feel. It affects real outcomes. He describes a large study of schoolchildren in Denmark. Those who took standardized tests later in the day scored noticeably lower than those who took them in the morning. The same children, the same tests, different results, simply because of the hour. Interestingly, when students had a break before the test, the decline shrank.

His advice is practical. Figure out your type. Notice when your peak, trough and recovery happen. Then, as far as you can, arrange your most important work to fit them.

[[lessons]]
$$**Your day has a shape.** Peak, trough, recovery: most people move through these stages every day. Knowing the pattern helps explain why some hours feel effortless and others feel like wading through mud. Instead of blaming yourself for the afternoon slump, you can plan around it.$$

$$**Match the task to the time.** Analytic work belongs in your peak. Insight work may flourish in your recovery, when your mind is looser. Routine tasks fit the trough. Simply rearranging when you do things, without working any harder, can noticeably improve the quality of what you produce.$$

$$**Timing changes results, not just feelings.** The Danish children scored lower on tests taken later in the day. Timing isn't a minor detail. For exams, decisions and important conversations, the hour you choose can shape the outcome, which makes it worth choosing on purpose rather than by habit.$$

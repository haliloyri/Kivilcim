A man in jeans and a baseball cap stands by a wall in a station, an open violin case at his feet. Hundreds of commuters stream past with coffee and phones. He plays for nearly three-quarters of an hour, and the case stays almost empty.

[[story]]
On 12 January 2007 the violinist Joshua Bell took up a position at the L'Enfant Plaza Metro station in Washington, D.C. The *Washington Post* columnist Gene Weingarten had arranged it as an experiment, and a hidden camera recorded the scene. Ori and Rom Brafman use this episode in *Sway* to show how context shapes what we think we are hearing.

Bell is one of the best-known classical violinists in the world. Here he was dressed as an ordinary street musician and played for close to 45 minutes. The question behind the experiment was simple: would people notice a great performer, if nobody had announced him?

According to the *Post*'s account, 1,097 people walked past. Seven stopped to listen. Twenty-seven put money in his case, and the total came to $32.17, not counting a $20 note from the single passer-by who recognised him. A few days earlier, playing similar music at a concert, he had earned far more.

It would be easy to conclude that commuters have no taste. That is the wrong reading. Nobody in the station lacked ears. They were on their way to work, in a place designed for walking through, and a station is where musicians ask for small change. The setting told them what to expect before the first note was played, and they responded to the setting.

The same sounds in a concert hall arrive with a ticket, a programme, a hush and a crowd who have decided in advance to listen. Those things do not change the violin. They change the listener, who is told that this is worth attention and so gives it.

Think about what attention costs in a place like that. Everyone there had somewhere to be, and stopping meant being late and standing alone in front of a stranger. Under those conditions, walking on is a reasonable default. The result says less about taste than about how rarely anyone spends attention that nobody has told them to spend.

There is a hard point in this for the rest of us. We rarely judge things in a blank room. A proposal arrives from someone junior, an idea comes in a messy email, a skill shows up in an unglamorous place. We take in the packaging first, and often that is all we take in. Most of the 1,097 people never gave the music a chance to be judged.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you judge the quality of something, check how much of your reaction comes from where you met it.** A setting can lower or raise your attention before the substance has had a turn. When a colleague's idea arrives badly packaged or an unknown person shows a skill, give the content two minutes of full attention with the label removed. Ask yourself what you would think if it came from someone you admire, in the best possible room. Try it with the next stranger who tells you something useful: stop for the length of one full sentence before you decide, since those few seconds are the cheapest way to avoid missing what was playing right next to you.$$

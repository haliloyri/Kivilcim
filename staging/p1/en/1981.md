Two tickets to a professional basketball game in Buffalo lie on a table, and outside a snowstorm is building. Two friends agree, sensibly, to stay home. Then one of them says a single sentence about the tickets that makes the other stop and think, and it ends up in a collection that would eventually change a discipline.

[[story]]
While he was a young economist in Rochester, New York, Richard Thaler kept gathering stories about people who did not behave the way economic theory said they would. In *Misbehaving* he calls the collection the List. He admits he would bore his friends with each new entry, and he had no idea what to do with any of them.

One entry came from the snowstorm. Thaler and his friend Jeffrey had tickets to a game in Buffalo, a drive of a little over an hour. When the snow came they decided to skip it. Jeffrey, who is not an economist, then remarked, "If we had paid full price for those tickets we would have gone!" As an observation about human behavior he was right. According to economic theory, though, the money was already spent and should not matter. So why would a higher price make the game more attractive?

Another entry was a colleague. Professor Richard Rosett of Rochester had bought wine some twenty years earlier, and his bottles had become far more valuable. He would not sell them. He also would not pay more than $30 for a bottle, yet he was willing to drink one worth many times that. Thaler summed it up: Rosett would neither buy nor sell at the going price.

The stories were funny, but a pile of anecdotes does not make a paper. Thaler says as much in his Nobel lecture: a collection of anecdotes was not enough to produce a publishable paper, let alone a research paradigm.

Then he went to a conference and told some of the stories to a psychologist who had been a student of Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky. The psychologist pointed him to their work, and back in Rochester Thaler read their 1974 paper in *Science*, "Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases."

The word that mattered to him was "biases." Many economists happily granted that people are boundedly rational, but if the errors were only random, they would cancel out and the models could carry on. Predictable errors were another matter, because they would pull behavior away from the theory in a direction you could forecast. In that view, the List stopped being a pile of jokes and became evidence of a pattern.

[[lessons]]
$$**Keep a running note of what surprises you in your own field.** An odd customer request, a project that went the opposite way from your forecast or a rule everyone breaks can each look like a one-off when it happens, and you forget them by the next week. A restaurant owner who jots down every dish that comes back untouched will, after a while, see whether it is the same kind of dish each time. Once you write them down, the oddities can be compared, and a comparison is where a theory of your own begins, even if it starts as a single sentence on a page.$$

On the shelves of a village library stand physics books written in a language the boy can barely read. It could be a wall. He opens one anyway, skips the sentences he cannot follow, and looks for a long time at the drawings in the margins. One of them shows a blade turning in the wind.

[[story]]
The little library in Wimbe, according to published accounts, was stocked through an aid programme and the generosity of American donors. Most of its books were in English. When William Kamkwamba was sent home from school, he began going there, and his English was not good enough to read a physics book line by line.

So he read the pictures first. Diagrams, arrows and cut-away drawings gave him what he was looking for without help from the language. A physics drawing, unlike a recipe, says the same thing in any tongue: this part connects to that one, and this is the direction it turns.

The beauty of a drawing is that it does not tell you everything. You see the direction of the arrows and how the parts connect, but you have to work out the why yourself. That turns learning from passive reading into thinking. Someone who cannot decode the text may look harder at the drawing because he has no choice.

Among the books was an American textbook called *Using Energy*. On its cover was a wind turbine, and inside it explained that turbines could pump water and generate electricity. Those two ideas matched the two great gaps in the Kamkwamba household at the time, water and light. Wimbe had no mains electricity, and the drought had already ruined the maize.

What stands out is that the book gave him a direction as much as a body of knowledge. Even without fully decoding the English, he grasped one thing from the picture: wind can be turned into work. Everything else began at that point, and the rest of the story is a boy building that picture with his own hands.

Not knowing a language is often confused with not knowing a subject. A problem does not solve itself, though, and a person solves it with whatever tools are at hand. His tools were pictures, curiosity and time. They were incomplete, but they were enough, at least to start.

What makes this worth remembering is not the size of the library. The shelves were few, the books were in a foreign language and there was no teacher. William stood in front of them anyway and took as much as he could. A person who begins with the part he understands, even in a book he mostly does not, can turn to the next page.

[[lessons]]
$$**In a field you do not understand, begin with the part you can understand.** In a foreign specialism, a dense report or a new tool, everything seems unreadable at once, and people often never start because they cannot take in the whole thing. Yet reading the diagram, the table or the worked example can give you a usable idea without decoding every sentence of the text. A small experiment with that idea teaches faster than waiting to grasp everything, and a missing language is not a reason to stop moving through the part you can follow.$$

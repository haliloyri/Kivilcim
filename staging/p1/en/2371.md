Twice, a customer walked into a Washington bank with three million dollars in cash and asked for it to go into the account of a company in his own name. The bank took the money both times. Nobody checked who the customer was, what the company did, or where the cash came from.

[[story]]
The customer was the president of Equatorial Guinea, Teodoro Obiang Nguema, and the bank was Riggs. A US Senate investigation made the details public in July 2004, and the account here follows the Mail & Guardian's report of it. The case fits the picture in Bruce Bueno de Mesquita and Alastair Smith's *The Dictator's Handbook* of rulers who live off a resource and pay their own circle first.

Riggs had opened an account to receive payments from oil companies working in the country. Withdrawals needed two signatures. Later the bank allowed wire transfers of more than $35 million from that account to two companies it knew nothing about, both holding accounts in jurisdictions with strict bank secrecy. Investigators had reason to believe at least one of the two was controlled wholly or partly by the president. When Riggs asked him about them in 2004, he gave no details and said only that the transfers had been authorised.

The same report described personal accounts for Obiang, his wife and other relatives, along with offshore shell companies set up for him and his sons. From 2000 to 2002 almost $13 million in cash was deposited into accounts the couple controlled. The two cash deposits of $3 million went into the account of his shell company, Otong, SA. More than a million dollars went from the oil account to Jadini Holdings, an offshore company controlled by his wife.

The family also appeared on the other side of the country's business. Sonavi, which provided security services, belonged to the president's brother, and US oil companies were told it had a monopoly. Sofana and Somagui Forestal, both owned by his son, held exclusive timber rights. Abayak, reportedly the country's only construction company, held 15 per cent of Mobil Oil Equatorial Guinea.

The Senate found that Riggs's senior leadership knew about the accounts and met the president several times.

What the file shows is a state where the oil income reached a single bank account and the people who could draw on it were few, close to the ruler and not answerable to anyone who counted the money.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask who the money reports to, and make that person visible.** A small shop that lets one cousin hold the card, the cash box and the books has not stolen anything yet, but no one can tell if anything is missing. When the same hands both receive and spend, and the only check is a second signature from the same circle, the ledger tells you little. Put one reader outside that circle, even just a monthly look from someone who does not benefit, and you will see problems while they are still small enough to fix without a scandal.$$

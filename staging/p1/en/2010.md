In northern Tanzania a Hadza adult drinks a glass of water tagged with rare isotopes. Over the following days the person goes about the usual business of the day and hands over urine samples. From them, researchers will work out how much energy the body burned. Everyone thinks they know how it will turn out.

[[story]]
The people who burned the most energy, it turned out, were not burning more than anyone else. That was the surprise Herman Pontzer and his colleagues ran into, and to see why it was a surprise you have to start with the expectation.

The Hadza still live by hunting and gathering in northern Tanzania, and their lives involve far more movement than most lives in industrial societies. The question was simple: do people who live like this burn more energy per day than Westerners? If they did, then obesity in industrial societies might be partly explained by sedentary lives pulling energy expenditure down.

To find out, the team used the doubly labeled water method. Participants drink water tagged with two different markers, the speed at which the markers leave the body is tracked in urine samples, and the difference gives total daily energy expenditure. The study appeared in PLoS ONE in 2012 and covered 30 Hadza adults in northern Tanzania, 13 men and 17 women.

The result did not match the hypothesis. The Hadza were more physically active than the Westerners they were compared with. But once body size was taken into account, their average daily energy expenditure was no different from the Westerners'. The cost of walking and the cost of resting per kilogram were similar in the two groups as well.

Adjusting for body size simply means comparing bigger and smaller bodies fairly. Even after that correction, people who walked more and worked harder in their day did not burn more energy than people who moved less. The authors read this as a sign that human daily energy expenditure may be an evolved trait, largely independent of culture.

They drew a conclusion for the obesity debate too: differences in obesity between populations may owe more to how much energy people take in than to how much they spend. The limits are clear. Thirty people from one population is a small sample, and the numbers tested an idea rather than settled it.

What makes the method valuable is that it asks nobody what they did. The method records what the body actually spent, so the answer cannot be inflated by hopes or flattered by memory. Daniel Lieberman's *Exercised* covers this kind of finding, where a long-held assumption about movement and energy meets a measurement. The lesson from the Hadza is to be wary of any link everyone knows but nobody has measured.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you accept an explanation, find a way to measure the assumption hiding inside it.** Pontzer's team skipped the argument about whether foragers burn more energy and measured it instead, and the expected gap never appeared. You can do the same with a cause everyone in the room "just knows": a sales dip, a delay, a family habit. Look for a small piece of data that tests the cause directly, because a solution built on a wrong cause tends to fail, and one measurement often ends a debate faster than a week of opinions. Even when the answer turns out to be the one you expected, you will hold it with far more confidence.$$

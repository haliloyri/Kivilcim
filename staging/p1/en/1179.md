Imagine writing a book that millions of people love. Now imagine sitting down to write the next one. Elizabeth Gilbert did, and she was terrified. What helped her wasn't a writing technique. It was an idea borrowed from the ancient Romans.

[[story]]
After *Eat Pray Love* became a worldwide phenomenon, Elizabeth Gilbert found herself in a strange position. Everyone wanted to know what she'd write next. And underneath the questions, she heard a darker one: what if her greatest success was already behind her? What if she'd never again write anything that mattered to so many people?

In *Big Magic*, she describes how she found a way to live with that pressure. She looked back to how people thought about creativity before modern times.

The ancient Greeks spoke of a *daemon*, a divine spirit that brought creative gifts. The Romans had a similar idea. They believed that a creative person *had* a genius, a kind of guardian spirit that lived with them and helped with their work. They didn't believe a person *was* a genius.

The difference sounds small. Gilbert argues it's enormous. If you *are* a genius, then everything depends on you. Every success is proof of your greatness, and every failure is proof you've lost it. That's a crushing weight to carry. But if you *have* a genius, the weight is shared. When the work is brilliant, you give some of the credit to your mysterious collaborator. When it fails, the failure isn't entirely yours either.

##Gilbert decided she would rather live in that older, lighter way.##

She tells the story of the American poet Ruth Stone, who grew up working in the fields of rural Virginia. Stone described feeling a poem coming toward her across the landscape, like a rush of air. When she felt it, she would run as fast as she could to the house, chasing it, looking for a pencil and paper to catch it before it passed through her and went on to find someone else. Sometimes she caught it. Sometimes she missed it.

Gilbert doesn't claim to know whether inspiration really works like that. But she finds the idea useful, because of what it asks of her. If inspiration is a visitor, her job is to be at the table when it comes. So she shows up. She does the work, every day, whether she feels inspired or not. Some days the genius visits. Many days it doesn't. Either way, she has done her part.

That, she found, was a way to keep writing after a huge success: not trying to be brilliant again, but simply being ready.

[[lessons]]
$$**Having a genius is lighter than being one.** If all your brilliance and failure come from you alone, every project becomes a test of your worth. The Roman idea of a genius as a visiting spirit let Gilbert share the credit and the blame. That small shift made it possible for her to keep creating without collapsing under expectations.$$

$$**Inspiration still needs you ready.** Ruth Stone ran for a pencil when she felt a poem coming. Even in the most mystical account of creativity, the artist has a job: to be there and catch it. Believing in inspiration doesn't mean waiting for it. It means being prepared when it arrives.$$

$$**Show up regardless of the last result.** Gilbert's answer to the pressure of success was daily work, not a bigger idea. Showing up every day, inspired or not, moves attention from the past success to the present task. That's where new work actually gets made.$$

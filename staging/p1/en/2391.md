A physicist walks down a very long hallway on his way to lunch. He passes an open door, a chemist's bench, a metallurgist arguing with an engineer. By the time he reaches the cafeteria he has been stopped twice, and neither conversation was on his calendar.

[[story]]
Mervin Kelly ran Bell Labs in the 1950s, and Jon Gertner's history of the place, The Idea Factory, makes him one of its central figures. Kelly had one conviction about how invention happens: it needs different kinds of people close enough to bump into each other. Bell Labs was a company research lab, and its work spanned physics, chemistry, metallurgy and engineering.

In New York City the Labs had been packed into crowded quarters, and closeness came for free. When the staff moved to a much larger campus at Murray Hill, New Jersey, the risk was that space would pull them apart. According to published accounts of Kelly's design, the answer was a building organised around one very long corridor. To get from one office or laboratory to another, you had to walk past everyone else.

This was a bet about people, placed in the floor plan. A theorist who never leaves his office never hears what the person at the bench cannot make work. A chemist who stays in his own wing never learns that a physicist needs a purer crystal. A corridor cannot make anyone curious, but it can make sure that curious people meet. Scheduled meetings only reach those who already know they should talk to each other, while a corridor also reaches those who do not yet know.

The bet paid off in a famous way. The team that built the first transistor at the end of 1947 paired John Bardeen, a theorist, with Walter Brattain, an experimenter who knew how to make a delicate device behave. The Labs had set up a solid-state physics group after the war to bring such different minds together on one goal.

Notice the method. Nobody had to announce a culture of collaboration or write a mission statement about it, because the building changed where people walked. Habits followed the floor plan, and the floor plan was fixed for decades.

A long corridor also produces small talk and some wasted minutes. That is the price of the design, and in a lab whose job was to solve problems nobody had solved before, specialists who never meet would have cost more.

[[lessons]]
$$**Put the conversations you need on the route people already walk, rather than on a calendar.** Meetings gather people who already know they should talk, while hallways, shared kitchens and a common lunch table create the meetings nobody planned. Think of the two colleagues in your own workplace whose problems fit each other but who never cross paths. Move a desk, a printer or a weekly coffee so that their paths cross, and the cost is a few lost minutes. What you gain is the question you did not know to ask, and the answer that was sitting two doors away.$$

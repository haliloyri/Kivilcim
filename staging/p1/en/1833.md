[[open]]
An astronomer looks through his telescope and sees thin, straight lines running across the whole surface of Mars. Books are written saying the lines are a civilization's irrigation canals. Then a spacecraft goes there and looks.

[[story]]
Carl Sagan takes up the story of the Martian canals in *The Demon-Haunted World*. In 1877 the Italian astronomer Giovanni Schiaparelli began reporting about a hundred lines on Mars. He called them "canali", an Italian word meaning channels, which can describe natural features as readily as built ones. But translations and retellings wiped out that distinction quickly.

Percival Lowell took the lines seriously and drew a civilization out of them. In his view, the inhabitants of a drying world had dug canals to carry water from the poles to the equator, so that Mars was crossed by an irrigation network. Lowell set out this picture in books published in 1895, 1906 and 1908, and reached a wide readership.

There was a dissenting voice. In 1903 Evans and Maunder ran an experiment with schoolboys, and it showed the lines could be an illusion. At the limit of a telescope, viewed from afar, the human eye links unrelated dots and smudges into straight lines.

The real answer came with the space age. In 1965 Mariner 4 flew past Mars, and in 1971 Mariner 9 went into orbit around the planet and mapped its surface. Sagan himself was among the people who worked on Mariner 9. The pictures showed no canals. The surface was full of craters, valleys and volcanoes, but nothing of the network Lowell had drawn.

For years Lowell trusted his own drawings. He had a sharp telescope and a powerful idea, but the eye, working at its limit, was inventing patterns. However attractive the idea, a second look was needed to confirm or demolish it.

It matters that Lowell was not stupid or dishonest. He did careful, serious work at an observatory he built himself. The trap was a quiet one: when data runs thin, the mind fills the gaps with whatever it already finds beautiful, and then mistakes the filling for the picture.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you trust a pattern seen at the limit of your eye or your data, ask for a second, independent look.** It is very easy to build a story from a single spike on a chart, a trend in a small slice of a survey, or the tone of a poor audio recording. The weaker the data, the faster the mind fills the gaps. Before you narrate a pattern, ask someone else to look, with another tool or another sample. If the line shows up again, it is real; if it doesn't, it was your eye playing tricks.$$

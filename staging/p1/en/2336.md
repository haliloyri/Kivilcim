[[open]]
In the desert before sunrise, a physicist stands about ten miles from a steel tower with a handful of small paper scraps. The instruments are elsewhere, wired and calibrated. He holds the scraps up, and he waits for the flash to pass and the wind of the blast to arrive.

[[story]]
Richard Rhodes's *The Making of the Atomic Bomb* ends its long road at the Trinity test. The device, nicknamed the Gadget, stood on a 100-foot metal tower in the Jornada del Muerto desert, on the Alamogordo range about 230 miles south of Los Alamos. The test was set for 4:00 in the morning. A thunderstorm delayed it, and the Gadget finally went off at 5:29:45 on 16 July 1945.

Enrico Fermi watched from Base Camp, about ten miles away. In his own written account of the test, he describes a small experiment of his own, one that needed no wiring at all: a few pieces of paper and a way of watching them.

The explosion came at about 5:30. He waited. The air blast reached him about forty seconds later. He let the small pieces fall from a height of about six feet, both before and while the blast wave passed. There was no wind, so he could watch where they landed. They were pushed aside by about two and a half metres.

From that displacement, Fermi judged the blast to be equal to that of ten thousand tons of TNT. He had his number within moments, with nothing but paper and his own eyes.

The official figure was larger. According to the Atomic Heritage Foundation, the test yielded between 15 and 20 kilotons, more than predicted and slightly more than the Little Boy bomb dropped on Hiroshima. Fermi's guess was therefore off by no more than a factor of two, from a handful of scraps, long before the instruments could be read and reduced.

Consider what that shows. The sophisticated gauges at Trinity were built to give exact answers later, once their records had been recovered and analysed. Fermi's paper gave a rough answer at once. Neither replaces the other. The rough answer arrived while the question was still alive, and it came with an honest range around it.

The scraps were cheap, easy to carry and needed no analysis of records to read. Fermi was not dismissing the instruments. He was adding a cheap, immediate check beside the expensive, delayed ones. A rough number you can read right away has a different job from a precise number you can read next month, and an experienced scientist keeps both within reach.

[[lessons]]
$$**Alongside your precise measurement, prepare a crude one you can read immediately.** A rough estimate made on the spot can tell you the order of magnitude while the full analysis is still weeks away. In practice this might be a back-of-the-envelope figure for a project before the finance team's model arrives, or a quick count of ten customer calls before the survey results do. The point is not to trust the sketch more than the instrument, but to know in advance roughly what the instrument should say, so that a wild answer gets noticed. It also gives you a way to catch a misread dial or a bad spreadsheet formula early.$$

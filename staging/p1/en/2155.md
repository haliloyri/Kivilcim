A computer keyboard stands in a zoo enclosure, and six monkeys have a month to use it. The idea behind the experiment is a century old and comes from physics, not from literature. At the end, a few sheets of paper come out of the printer, and the keyboard itself looks rather worse for wear.

[[story]]
The second part of Nassim Taleb's *Fooled by Randomness* is named after monkeys on typewriters. The image is older than the book. In 1913 the French mathematician Émile Borel wrote about a million people hitting keys at random, ten hours a day, as accounts of the article describe it. Over a year, he argued, their pages might contain copies of the books in the world's richest libraries. He meant it as a way of gauging how unlikely something is, not as a promise.

The arithmetic behind the picture is easy to run. Suppose a typist hits each of the 26 letters with equal chance. A single twenty-letter line then appears with a chance of one in 26 to the power of 20, about one in 20 billion billion billion. Tiny for one typist. But a vast number of typists, each making vast numbers of keystrokes, will produce some order somewhere, and if you look only at that spot, it seems miraculous.

In 2003 a team from the University of Plymouth tried the real thing. With a grant of £2,000 from the Arts Council, lecturers and students in its MediaLab Arts course put a computer keyboard in the enclosure of six Celebes crested macaques at Paignton Zoo in Devon. They left it for about a month and broadcast the progress online.

The macaques produced five pages, most of them the letter S. The lead male hit the keyboard with a stone, and other animals urinated and defecated on it. The project's director, Mike Phillips, said the monkeys "are not random generators." The pages were later published in a limited-edition book.

That last sentence is the useful part. The theorem assumes a typist whose every key is equally likely and independent of the last. A real animal repeats a favourite key, bangs on the machine and loses interest. Genuine randomness is a demanding property, and real sources of it rarely look the way we picture them.

So the experiment cuts both ways. Shakespeare did not appear, but that was never the point. The point of the old image is the size of the pool: with enough attempts, something that looks like design will turn up by chance, and the attempts that produced nothing leave no trace on the page.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you decide a result needs a special explanation, work out how many attempts were made.** A neighbour who wins a small prize twice, a colleague whose forecast came true three quarters in a row and a stock tip that paid off all look remarkable when you see only the winner. If hundreds of people made similar guesses, a few would hit by chance, and the run tells you little about skill. Estimating the size of the pool takes a few minutes and a rough guess. It lets you admire the result without crowning the person.$$

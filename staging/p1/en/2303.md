[[open]]
Stand at the fence in Nogales and look both ways. The hills are the same, the desert is the same, the weather is the same. Yet on one side of the line, officials who fail the public can be voted out, and on the other, the road, the school and the clinic are far harder to count on.

[[story]]
Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson open *Why Nations Fail* with a single town cut in two. Nogales lies on the border between the United States and Mexico, and the same name belongs to two cities, one in Arizona and one in Sonora. The authors stress that they share the same geography and the same climate, and by their account a closely related history and culture. Whatever divides them, it was not handed down by nature.

The line itself is surprisingly recent. Mexico granted the land in 1841 under the name Los Nogales de Elías, and after the Gadsden Purchase of 1853 it became part of the United States. In 1880 two brothers, Isaac and Jacob Isaacson, homesteaded a trading post there, and in 1883 the post office took the name Nogales. The Arizona side was incorporated as a city in 1893. Even the physical barrier came late: after a battle between American soldiers and Mexican militia in August 1918, a permanent wall was built along International Street, replacing a wire fence that had stood only a few months.

Now take the authors' comparison. On the Arizona side, they write, most residents have at least a high school education, reasonable access to health care, and public services such as roads and electricity. People there do not have to fear for their safety, and they can vote out officials who fail to protect them. On the Sonora side, the authors point out, the city is among the better-off places in Mexico, with wealth that comes largely from factories run by American companies. Yet its residents lack many of those basics.

Why does this example work so well? Because it removes the usual suspects one by one. If hot weather, a bad location or an unlucky culture explained poverty, the two halves would look alike. They do not. What changed at the line was the set of rules: who may start a business, who can be held to account, whose property and safety the state defends. The authors call the gap in Nogales only the tip of the iceberg, and say that inequality across the world is roughly ten times larger, with people in the poorest countries earning as little as one fortieth of what Americans earn.

It is a modest comparison, not a laboratory, and the authors use it as an opening question rather than a proof. But the question is hard to unsee. When two neighbours with the same soil, the same sun and the same ancestors end up in such different places, the explanation has to sit in something people built, and what people built can be rebuilt.

[[lessons]]
$$**When two sides start from the same place and end up far apart, look first at the rules each side lives under.** In a company, two departments with similar people and similar budgets can perform very differently because one rewards the person who brings bad news and the other quietly punishes her. Before you blame talent, mood or culture, ask who decides, who is allowed to object, and who pays when something goes wrong. Rules are written by people, which makes them the one part of the picture you can actually rewrite, starting with the next meeting.$$

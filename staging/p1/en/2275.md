The keeper of a coffee shop near Broad Street tells a visitor that nine of her customers are already dead. Mechanics eat dinner there, and the water that comes with the meal is drawn from the pump in the street outside. The visitor has more questions, and he keeps asking them house after house.

[[story]]
The visitor is John Snow, a London physician, and the street is in Soho, where cholera struck without warning at the end of August 1854. Steven Johnson's *The Ghost Map* is about this outbreak. This story follows one week of Snow's inquiry and ends with a pump handle.

Snow suspected the pump in Broad Street early. On the evening of 3 September he examined its water and found so little impurity that he hesitated to conclude anything. Many people would have dropped the idea there. Snow went on asking, and further inquiry showed him that nothing else was shared by the small area where the disease had leapt up, and by no one beyond it, except the water of that pump. Over the next two days he saw the water vary, with small white flecks in it that he could make out on close inspection.

Next he asked the General Register Office for the list of cholera deaths registered in the week ending 2 September in three subdistricts. There were eighty-nine. Only six fell in the first four days, four came on Thursday 31 August, and the other seventy-nine on Friday and Saturday. Snow took the Thursday as the start of the outbreak and went to inquire about eighty-three of the deaths.

Nearly all of them lay a short distance from the pump. Ten had happened in houses nearer to another pump, and these were the cases that could have sunk his idea. He checked each one. In five, the family told him they always sent to Broad Street because they preferred the water. Three were children who went to school near the pump, and two were known to drink from it. The last two lived outside the district and, he judged, belonged to the usual background of cholera. Among the deaths in the pump's own area, he was told in sixty-one that the person had drunk the water, constantly or now and then. In six he found no one left to ask, and in six the answer was no.

On the evening of Thursday 7 September he put all this before the Board of Guardians of St James's parish. The handle was removed the following day. According to accounts of the outbreak, deaths had already begun to fall by then, so nobody can say how many lives that one act saved.

What it does show is how Snow won a decision. Rather than asking the Guardians to share a theory, he brought a list of addresses, a count of who drank and who did not, and the awkward cases laid out in the open. He even stated the six households that answered no. That is hard to wave away, because the speaker has already tested the weak points himself.

[[lessons]]
$$**Run down the cases that do not fit before you ask anyone to act.** When Snow's first look at the water disappointed him, he kept the question open and went door to door, checking ten exceptions with the people involved. In your own work, it might be a sales dip, a failed pilot or a recurring complaint: list the cases your explanation cannot cover and chase them first. Those who decide will trust a request that arrives with its weakest points already examined. It also protects you, since an exception you found yourself is far easier to explain than one a critic finds for you.$$

A young man watches a wall of concrete and barbed wire go up through the middle of a city. Years later, in another city, he reads that the tanks of the country behind that wall have rolled into a neighbour. These two moments change the course of a spy's life for good.

[[story]]
Oleg Gordievsky, the subject of Ben Macintyre's *The Spy and the Traitor*, was a Soviet intelligence officer who ended up working for Britain. This story is about why he turned. A man rarely decides to work against his own country on a single day. In Gordievsky's case, a series of events fed a doubt over many years.

In 1961 Gordievsky was posted to East Berlin. In that same year the Berlin Wall went up through the middle of the city. Two years later, in 1963, he joined the KGB. In 1966 he began a posting at the Soviet mission in Copenhagen. There, in a free country of the West, he had the chance to see how the Soviet system looked from outside.

In 1968 Soviet forces invaded Czechoslovakia. Gordievsky was in Copenhagen at the time. By his own later account, those two events, the Wall and the invasion, left him with a lasting distrust of his system. That is his own telling, and in truth it is never easy to pin a person's turn on a single cause.

In October 1972 Gordievsky returned to Copenhagen. In the years that followed, British intelligence, MI6, made contact and began working with him. The starting date varies between sources.

What matters in this story is that a single event can change a value for good. Gordievsky did not wake up one day and betray. He saw a wall being built and tanks entering a neighbour, and those images stayed in the background of every decision he made over the following decades. Values are more often born of a scene than of an argument.

Notice, too, how little drama there is on the way. A posting, a promotion to the KGB, another posting abroad: on paper an ordinary career. The turn happened underneath it, invisible to anyone reading the record, and visible only to the man himself.

[[lessons]]
$$**Name the events that steer your values and decisions, because a scene often settles more than a principle does.** When you ask why you left a job, why you do not trust a team, or why you will never do a certain thing, you usually find a concrete moment behind it. Knowing that moment helps you understand your decisions better and, if need be, re-examine them. That also shows you your own blind spots. Read other people's behaviour with the same eye: beneath someone's reaction there may be a scene that you cannot see.$$

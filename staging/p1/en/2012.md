[[open]]
A brockle-faced calf with three white blazes weighs 650 pounds on a ranch in South Dakota. The man who bought him for $598 has decided to write down every dollar spent on the animal until the day he is sold. The calf is known by a number, 534, and the ledger will end on a figure with only two digits.

[[story]]
The calf was eight months old when Michael Pollan bought him from the Blair brothers, who run an 11,500-acre cow-calf ranch near Sturgis, South Dakota. He paid $598. Pollan followed the animal for the *New York Times Magazine* in 2002, and the same steer runs through the feedlot chapter of *The Omnivore's Dilemma*.

Born on March 13 at 80 pounds, 534 reached 650 by mid-November and 798 by Christmas. The ranch charged $61.13 for him up to January 4. His next address was Poky Feeders, near Garden City, Kansas, a yard that holds about 37,000 animals, and there he stood in a pen of roughly 90 steers at $1.60 a day for room, board and medication.

The feed mill grinds about a million pounds a day. He ate about 32 pounds a day, 25 of them corn, and turned that into roughly 3.5 pounds of weight. Corn sold for $2.25 a bushel, about 50 cents under what it cost to grow. A daily dose of the additive Rumensin and the antibiotic tylosin went into the ration as well, because more than 13 percent of feedlot cattle have abscessed livers at slaughter.

The hormone implant, a synthetic estrogen, cost $1.50 and added 40 to 50 pounds by slaughter, which returned at least $25. He was expected to leave Poky at about 1,250 pounds and travel some 100 miles to a National Beef plant in Liberal, Kansas. Rich Blair said he would love to give up hormones.

Pollan added it up. The purchase was $598, the ranch bill $61.13, and 160 days at the feedlot, hormone implant included, came to $258. The total was $917. He expected the steer to be worth $944 in June, a projected profit of $27. Over the past 20 years, according to the analysts at Cattle-Fax, the average return had been about $3 a head.

Ed Blair put it plainly: "my dad made more money on 250 head than we do on 850." His brother Rich said that with some pens you make money and with some you lose.

The ledger recorded what the corn sold for, and left out what the corn cost to grow. That 50-cent gap appeared on no line of 534's account.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask who pays for the cheap input before you trust a margin.** A baker who finds flour at half the usual price and writes a healthy profit into every loaf has done only half the sum. If the miller loses money on each sack, or someone else quietly covers the difference, the profit lasts only as long as that arrangement does. A thin margin rests on a thin assumption, so for any bargain you can ask what has to stay true for the price to hold, and put the answer into your plan before the price changes and the margin goes with it.$$

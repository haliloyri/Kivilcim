[[open]]
An essay comes back from a reviewer with comments crowding the margins. Most of them point out what is wrong. The student holds the pages and has to decide whether the reviewer is helping them improve or has already made up their mind about them.

[[story]]
Every difficult conversation, according to Douglas Stone, Bruce Patton and Sheila Heen in *Difficult Conversations*, carries an identity question under the topic: Am I competent? Am I a good person? Am I worthy of love? Criticism lands on that question first, and only afterwards on the thing being criticised. A 1999 experiment gave that idea a sharp test.

The psychologists Geoffrey Cohen, Claude Steele and Lee Ross asked Black and White undergraduates to write an essay. Each student then received detailed, mostly critical feedback on it from a White reviewer. The experiment turned on how that criticism was wrapped.

In the first experiment, there were three versions. Some students got the criticism with nothing added. Some got it with general praise. And some got it framed by two statements: that the reviewer used high standards, and that the reviewer believed the student could reach them.

The unwrapped version produced the biggest gap. Black students who received bare criticism were more likely than White students to say the reviewer was biased, and they reported the lowest motivation to keep working on the essay. With general praise added, Black students were still somewhat more likely than White students to suspect bias. The version with high standards plus assurance made the difference in perceived bias disappear.

A second experiment pulled the two ingredients apart. Standards alone, without the assurance, were not enough, because the motivation gap stayed. Standards plus assurance was the combination that worked. The authors called this "wise" feedback. These are averages from a laboratory study of undergraduates, so they describe a tendency and not a prediction for any one person.

Read through the book's lens, the result is about identity. A student reading a page of corrections has two questions to answer: what is wrong with this essay, and what does this reviewer think of me? When the second question stays open, it can swallow the first. Telling someone the bar is high and that you think they can clear it answers the second question in advance, and leaves the energy free for the first. An essay can be fixed in another afternoon of work, while a doubt about your own worth cannot be fixed by rewriting.

[[lessons]]
$$**Pair a high standard with a clear statement that you believe the person can meet it.** A teacher returning a heavily marked draft, a manager reviewing a first report or a parent going over a failed test is answering two questions at once, one about the work and one about what they think of the person. If the second answer is left unsaid, the listener will often supply their own, and it is usually the harshest one. Say both parts, and say them only when you mean them, because an assurance you do not believe will be heard as flattery. Then ask what they want to change first.$$

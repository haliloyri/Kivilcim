In Salzburg a father opens a blank music book and writes his daughter's name on the first page. Over the following years he will fill it with minuets and keyboard pieces and note dates beside them. In the same pages, a few years on, his small son's first compositions will appear.

[[story]]
On the title page, in Leopold Mozart's own hand, the French reads: "For the harpsichord. This book belongs to Marie Anne Mozart 1759." Leopold put the volume together for his daughter Nannerl, with the help of several Salzburg copyists, and according to the catalogue of the Mozarteum he kept adding pieces until about 1764. Around fifty keyboard pieces survive in it, many of them minuets, some by other composers and some by Leopold himself.

When Nannerl's lessons began, her younger brother Wolfgang started to use the same book. The Mozarteum notes that Leopold documented his training carefully. At first he wrote down on Wolfgang's behalf the pieces the boy improvised at the keyboard. According to published descriptions of the notebook, some entries carry dated notes in Leopold's hand: a minuet and trio is marked as learned on 26 January 1761, a day before Wolfgang's fifth birthday, and another minuet is marked as learned in his fourth year. A short allegro in F is dated 11 December 1761.

The Mozarteum's catalogue entry also contains a sentence worth pausing on: only at the age of eight could Wolfgang write down his own compositions himself. Until then, Leopold held the pen, and the child played first and had the music written afterwards. Published descriptions place the first piece Wolfgang inscribed himself in the book around 1764.

Leopold's contribution was a plain one. He wrote down what was taught, when it was taught and what the boy did with it. A date beside a minuet turns progress from a vague impression into a place in a sequence. If one page says "learned this piece on this day," then a few pages later "wrote this piece himself" has something to be measured against. The jump shows in the distance between the two notes, and that distance is a slowly accumulated road that nobody would remember without the book.

It is easy to read this story as the miracle of a child prodigy, and the facts leave room for that reading. The notebook shows something more ordinary: training becomes visible once it is recorded. What was learned, when it was learned and what comes next can sit together on the same page, where a teacher, a parent or the learner can read them.

[[lessons]]
$$**Put a date beside everything you learn, because progress that is recorded becomes visible and measurable.** Saying "I feel like I am getting better" with a language, an instrument or a new job does not tell you what changed a week later. If you write in a notebook which piece, method or task you learned on which day, you can look back months later and choose your next step from a real starting point. Sharing that record with your child, your team or only yourself turns learning from an impression into a sequence and makes the next step more concrete.$$

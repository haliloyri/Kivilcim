A city map is cut into 24 pieces of similar size. Chance picks twelve of them, and into those twelve, researchers release mosquitoes that carry a bacterium. The other twelve pieces get nothing new, and everyone waits to see where the fever turns up in the months that follow.

[[story]]
Ed Yong's *I Contain Multitudes* describes mosquitoes carrying the bacterium *Wolbachia*, which in certain strains stops them from carrying dengue fever. A claim like that is easy to make in a lab. This story is about the field test that came later, in Yogyakarta, Indonesia, whose results appeared in the *New England Journal of Medicine* in 2021.

The trial area covered about 26 square kilometres and roughly 311,700 residents. Researchers divided it into 24 clusters and picked 12 at random to receive *Aedes aegypti* mosquitoes infected with a strain called wMel, on top of the usual dengue control. The other 12 kept only the usual control. Releases ran from March to December 2017, with between 9 and 14 rounds in each treated cluster. Leaving the choice to chance meant that nobody could steer the mosquitoes toward the neighbourhoods most likely to look good.

Then came the harder part, which was counting. From January 2018 until March 2020, when the study stopped because of COVID-19, clinics enrolled people aged 3 to 45 who came in with an acute fever. Some had dengue and many had something else, and that mix was the point. By comparing how often the dengue-positive patients versus the other fever patients came from treated clusters, the team could estimate protection without testing every resident.

Of 8,144 people enrolled, 6,306 were analysed. Among the 2,905 analysed participants from treated clusters, 67 had dengue, or 2.3 percent. Among the 3,401 from untreated clusters, 318 did, or 9.4 percent. That works out to protective efficacy of 77.1 percent, with a 95 percent confidence interval of 65.3 to 84.9. Dengue serious enough for hospital was rarer still: 13 patients from treated clusters against 102 from untreated ones, an efficacy of 86.2 percent.

The effect held across all four dengue serotypes, though it varied: highest for DENV-2 at 83.8 percent, lowest for DENV-1 at 71.0 percent, with a wide interval on that last figure. The trial was sponsored by Universitas Gadjah Mada and funded in part by the Tahija Foundation. It counted symptomatic, lab-confirmed dengue, the outcome that matters to a patient, and the finding came from ordinary neighbourhoods across a whole city rather than from a controlled lab.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask what happened in the places that got nothing before you credit a change.** A result looks convincing on its own only when you cannot imagine it happening without your change, and that is rare, because the world keeps moving while you test. A cafe owner who rewrites the menu and sees takings rise may simply have met a warm spell or a street festival, which a neighbouring cafe with the old menu would reveal at once. Before you announce that something worked, find the closest comparison you can, and say out loud what the unchanged side did. If the gap between the two sides is large, you can trust the result far more.$$

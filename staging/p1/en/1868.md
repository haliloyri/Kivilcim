On the South Side of Chicago, a 17-year-old drifts in the water off the 29th Street beach. One part of that beach has been set aside for white bathers, and he drifts across the line. He will not come out alive, and what follows will shake the city for nearly a week.

[[story]]
Isabel Wilkerson's *The Warmth of Other Suns* describes the world that Black Americans met when they moved north. This story is about a day on which the tensions of that migration broke open in Chicago, 27 July 1919. That day Eugene Williams, aged 17, was in the water at the 29th Street beach on the South Side and drifted into the section reserved for white bathers.

A white man threw stones at him. The stones struck Eugene, and he drowned. His death became the spark for the city's racial tension. The violence that followed lasted about a week and ended on 3 August.

The toll was heavy. Thirty-eight people died, 23 of them Black and 15 white. Another 537 were injured, about two-thirds of them Black and one-third white. A death that began at a beach had spread, within days, across the whole city. The dead included Patrolman John W. Simpson, a Black police officer, the only policeman killed. The violence reached both Black and white Chicagoans, and the casualty lists show it.

Behind the spark lay pressure that had been building for years. The Black population arriving in Chicago had grown quickly, but low-cost housing had not kept pace. In 1917 the Chicago Real Estate Board adopted a block-by-block policy of segregation. By 1920 some 85 percent of the city's Black residents lived in one part of the South Side.

Governor Frank Lowden set up an interracial commission to study the causes. Its work was published as *The Negro in Chicago*, and the report's main author was Charles S. Johnson. Among the causes it named was the difficulty returning soldiers had in getting back their jobs and homes.

The logic of this event is that a beach line and a few stones cannot by themselves explain a week of violence. Crowded neighbourhoods, scarce housing and rules of separation had loaded the city with enough strain for a small incident to shake it. To understand a blow-up like this, you first have to look at where the spark fell. In Chicago that place was a crowded district, held for years within the same boundaries, whose residents were looking for housing and work.

$$**When a conflict erupts, look at the load the situation was already carrying before you look at the spark.** A single sharp remark in a team meeting is often the result of a workload nobody has discussed for months, a vague split of duties or a tight budget. While you try to settle the argument, ask which resource is scarce and how long it has been so, rather than asking who said the wrong thing. If you spot that load early, a small incident can be settled in a calm conversation before it grows. After the argument, try once to discuss with the team why the same subject keeps coming back.$$

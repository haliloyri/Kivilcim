[[open]]
A global health report lists one figure for the people who died of a single infection in a single year. It also says, a few lines on, that the disease can be prevented and cured. Both statements are on the same page, and the distance between them is the whole story.

[[story]]
The World Health Organization's tuberculosis fact sheet puts the 2024 toll in plain numbers. An estimated 10.7 million people fell ill with TB that year, among them 1.2 million children. A total of 1.23 million died, 150,000 of them people with HIV. Spread over a leap year, that is roughly 3,400 deaths a day, by simple division. TB remains the world's leading cause of death from a single infectious agent, and about 87% of new cases were in 30 high-burden countries.

The same fact sheet states that TB is preventable and curable. John Green builds his book *Everything Is Tuberculosis* on that tension. In interviews he says the drugs exist but too often are not near the people who need them, and that the world could choose to live without TB.

The WHO numbers also show where the distance sits, and who bears it. Only about two in five people with drug-resistant TB accessed treatment in 2024. About half of people treated for TB, together with their households, faced catastrophic costs, which WHO defines as more than 20% of household income.

Some figures move the other way. About 34,000 people started the shorter regimens for drug-resistant TB in 2024, up from 5,653 in 2023 and 1,744 in 2022. WHO estimates that global efforts have saved 83 million lives since 2000.

Money is the other half of the picture. WHO says US$22 billion a year is needed for TB prevention, diagnosis, treatment and care to meet the 2027 targets. International donor funding was US$1.1 billion in 2024, and domestic sources paid 82% of spending on TB services. Research and innovation financing was US$1.2 billion in 2023, far short of the global target of US$5 billion a year.

A total tells you how many; the splits tell you who. Read this way, 1.23 million is not a fixed fate but the sum of many particular gaps: a person with drug-resistant TB who got no treatment, a household whose costs took more than a fifth of its income, a regimen that exists but has not yet reached the place where it is needed. Each gap has an address, and each can be closed by someone who knows where it is.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask how many of the people who need something actually receive it.** A single headline figure hides the difference between a service that exists and a service that arrives. A community library, for instance, may report thousands of members, yet the useful question is how many of the neighbours who would benefit have ever walked in. Splitting a total by who is reached and who is not points to the next step to fix, and it keeps good news about the average from covering for a group that is still being missed, which is the group that most needs your attention.$$

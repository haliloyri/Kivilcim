A storm flattens the barley in a field a week before harvest. The man who farms it has no crop, but he owes grain to a merchant. The law of ancient Babylon imagined exactly this case, and the question it asked was simple: what should happen to the debt? A king once had his answer cut into stone.

[[story]]
Hammurabi ruled Babylon from around 1792 to around 1750 BCE. The collection of laws that carries his name was composed around 1753 BCE, near the end of his reign. Fragments of the stele on which the laws were carved turned up in Susa, in what is now Iran, between December 1901 and January 1902, and Jean-Vincent Scheil published the first edition in 1902. The stone had been carried to Susa as plunder.

Part of these laws concerns debt. Law 48, in L. W. King's translation, reads like this. If a man owes a debt and a storm flattens the grain, or the harvest fails, or the grain does not grow for lack of water, then in that year he need not give his creditor any grain. He washes his debt-tablet in water and pays no rent for that year. Some readers take that payment to be interest. Since the tablet was clay, washing it meant wiping the record.

Law 117 sets another limit. If a man cannot meet a claim for debt and sells himself, his wife, his son or his daughter for money, or gives them over to forced labour, they work for three years in the house of the buyer. In the fourth year they are set free. The price of a debt, in other words, did not run forever; it had an end date. There is a heavy side to this, and it should be said plainly: we are talking about a world in which family members could be handed over for a debt.

Read together, the two laws show a certain logic. When a bad year came, that year's payment could be suspended. Even in the worst case, the price of a debt could not stretch to infinity. The text gives no reason for why these rules were set, so it is better not to guess at motives. What we can do is look at what they did: two doors, a pause and an end date, were written into the record to stop a man collapsing completely after a storm.

What stands out is that the rules take both the good year and the bad year into account. In an economy where farm income swings from year to year, a fixed repayment calendar could ruin a farmer in one bad season. Law 48 builds that swing into the terms of the debt. Law 117 draws a line even in the harshest case: three years, then freedom.

When a debt may pause and when it must end thus become part of the agreement itself. A debt written in clay had an exit written in the same world.

[[lessons]]
$$**Write a bad-year clause and an end date into every debt agreement from the start.** Hammurabi's law suspended that year's grain payment after a storm and capped working off a debt at three years. If you discuss a loan or a personal debt and ask in advance "what do we do if income drops, and when does this end?", the debt does not turn into a trap. A debt that can actually be repaid is a sounder deal for the one who borrows and the one who lends. It is easiest to ask before anyone signs, while both sides are calm.$$

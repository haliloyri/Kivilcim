[[open]]
Two players have each put 32 coins on the table. Whoever wins three games first takes all 64. One of them has won two games, the other has won one, and now the play has to stop. Each has a reasonable claim to the money, and the claims cannot both be met.

[[story]]
Peter Bernstein's *Against the Gods* follows the long effort to put numbers on chance, and this episode is one of its oldest puzzles. It is called the problem of points: a game of chance is interrupted before the end, and the stake has to be divided fairly between players who are still level in skill. In 1494 the Italian mathematician Luca Pacioli printed a rule for it in his *Summa de arithmetica*. Divide the money in proportion to the rounds each player has already won. It sounds fair.

The rule fell apart under pressure. Niccolò Tartaglia pointed out that if only one round had been played, the player who won it would collect the entire stake, however long the game was meant to last. One early win proves very little. Tartaglia himself doubted that any division could satisfy everyone, and wrote that however it was made, there would be cause for litigation.

The question sat unsolved for another century and a half. In 1654 the Chevalier de Méré put it to Blaise Pascal in Paris, and Pascal took it to Pierre de Fermat in Toulouse. Over the next months, from July to October, they exchanged letters about it, and those letters are often credited as the birth of probability theory.

Pascal's move was to stop counting what had already happened and count what could still happen. Take the position from the table: 32 coins each, one player ahead by two games to one. If the leader wins the next game, he takes all 64. If he loses, the score is two all and they split the pot, 32 apiece. So the leader is already sure of 32, and the other 32 is a coin toss between them. He should leave with 48 and the other player with 16.

Fermat reached the same kind of answer by a different road, listing every equally likely way the remaining games could fall. Pascal wrote that he plainly saw the truth was the same at Toulouse and at Paris. Two methods, two cities, one rule: what a player is owed depends on what he still needs to win, and the games already won matter only through that. Pascal's step-by-step way of working backwards from the end of the game went on to influence Christiaan Huygens, whose treatise on games of chance appeared in 1657.

[[lessons]]
$$**Split the outcome of an interrupted effort by what each side still has to do.** Suppose two friends run a market stall together and one has to leave halfway through the season. The easy way is to share the takings by who has worked the most days so far, but that ignores the weeks of work that remain and who will carry them. If you ask what each person would still need to do to finish, and how likely it is to pay off, the division becomes something both can look at and check. You can settle it early, while you are both still talking about numbers.$$

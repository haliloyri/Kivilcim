If your job were to make a cycling team faster, how far down the list would you get before thinking about pillows? You would probably start with the riders, the bikes, the training. Bedding sounds like something to sort out after the serious work. In James Clear's *Atomic Habits*, it becomes part of the serious work.

[[story]]
Clear begins with British cycling, a team whose record offered little reason to expect the success that would follow. Performance director Dave Brailsford approached that problem by breaking the sport into smaller parts. He wanted to find a one-percent improvement in each part, then bring those gains together.

That approach gave the team plenty to work on. They redesigned bicycle saddles to make them more comfortable. They rubbed alcohol on the tires to improve grip. Each adjustment addressed a particular detail of riding, small enough to examine and change.

Then the search went beyond the bike. If a rider's performance depended partly on staying healthy, avoiding illness belonged in the work too. A surgeon taught the riders how to wash their hands to reduce their chances of catching a cold. Handwashing became another detail worth getting right.

Sleep received the same attention. The team identified the pillows and mattresses that helped riders sleep well and took those items to hotels when they traveled. The work of preparing for a race reached into the room where a rider would spend the night.

##Even the pillow counted as part of preparing to ride well.##

In Clear's telling, British cycling went on to achieve major successes at the Olympics and the Tour de France. He connects that transformation to Brailsford's search for small improvements, an approach called the aggregation of marginal gains.

The examples explain what that phrase means. Saddle comfort, tire grip, handwashing, and sleep each offered a different place to improve. A more comfortable saddle could contribute something without having to account for the team's entire performance. Better sleep could contribute something else.

Clear uses the account to introduce the cumulative value of small changes. You can overlook any one of those adjustments because it seems too modest beside an Olympic ambition. Brailsford's approach made those modest adjustments worth looking for, including in places a rider would leave behind before reaching the starting line.

[[lessons]]
$$**Give the ambition smaller parts.** Brailsford could examine saddle comfort and tire grip because each was a specific part of cycling. If you want to improve a recurring task, break it down with that same care. What happens before you start? Where does the task become awkward? What happens afterward? Choose a detail you can actually change, then watch what happens on the next attempt. The one-percent idea gives you permission to work on something small without demanding that it solve the entire problem.$$

$$**Look beyond the moment of performance.** The pillows and handwashing matter because they widen the search. A rider's preparation includes the night before the race and the routines that help them stay healthy. For your own task, look at what supports the moment when you need to do well. An easier setup or a better chance to recover may deserve attention. Pick a change because it addresses a difficulty you have noticed, just as the bedding addressed sleep while traveling.$$

[[reflect]]
- Choose one task you repeat and want to do better. Separate it into preparation, the task itself, and recovery afterward.
- Which small inconvenience in those parts have you started treating as unavoidable? Think of the team's willingness to reconsider even a pillow.
- Choose one adjustment and decide what you will look for next time to tell whether it helped.

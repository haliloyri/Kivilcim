Paul Jarvis was a web designer whose business kept growing. More clients came, then more people to serve them. At some point he looked at his calendar and found something odd. The thing that had made him want the business in the first place, designing, was no longer on it.

[[story]]
Paul Jarvis worked as a web designer, and by the standards most people use, he was doing well. His business grew. He took on more clients, and to handle them, he took on more people.

The usual story of business success says this is the plan. More clients means more revenue, more revenue means a bigger team, and a bigger team means a bigger company. Jarvis believed in that sequence, and he followed it.

Then he noticed what the days looked like. Where he had once spent his time designing, he now spent it in meetings. He wrote emails. He dealt with people problems inside a team. The business was bigger and he had less of the work he did best.

##The growth he had pursued was costing him the thing it was supposed to support.##

He decided to scale back. He chose to return to working on his own, with a smaller business that he could run in a way that kept the work in his hands. It was a deliberate step down from where the business had been, and it ran against the advice that growth is always the goal.

He describes this in *Company of One*, the book that grew out of the experience. The book's idea is that a business can stay small on purpose. A company of one isn't a stage on the way to something bigger. It's a legitimate shape for a business, chosen for freedom, control and sustainability.

His question for readers is a pointed one: why are you growing? If growth is a tool, what is it for? And when it starts to pull you away from the work you love, from your family or from your health, it is fair to ask whether the tool is still doing its job.

Jarvis is not arguing against ambition. He is asking what the ambition is for.

[[lessons]]
$$**Look at what the metric replaced.** Revenue and headcount went up, and designing went off the calendar. A baker who opens a third shop may find she no longer bakes. Check your own week for the same swap.$$

$$**Tools can be set down.** Jarvis treated growth as a means to a life he wanted. A freelancer who turns down a fourth client to keep Fridays free is using the tool, not serving it.$$

[[reflect]]
- What did you do last year because it was the next step, like accepting a bigger title, and not because you wanted it?
- Describe your best day of work. How much of it is on the calendar this week?

&&If your work grew by half next year, what part of your day would shrink?&&

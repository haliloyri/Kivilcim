The British cycling team took pillows and mattresses to hotels. Better sleep away from home had become part of preparing to race. Their search for an advantage was reaching well beyond the bicycle, into details that could easily have been left to chance.

[[story]]
Dave Brailsford became British cycling's performance director in 2003, with a poor record to turn around. His approach was to divide cycling into its component parts and look for a one-percent improvement in each. He called it the aggregation of marginal gains. James Clear uses the account to open *Atomic Habits*.

The team began with the bicycles. They redesigned saddles to make them more comfortable and rubbed alcohol on the tires for better grip. These were specific problems with specific adjustments. Each offered a place to improve without requiring a complete reinvention of the sport.

But the rider mattered beyond the time spent on the bike. Staying healthy was part of being ready to perform. A surgeon taught the riders how to wash their hands to reduce their chances of catching a cold. Even a routine that familiar received attention.

Sleep became another area to work on. The team identified pillows and mattresses that suited the riders, then took that bedding to hotels while traveling. Preparing for a race included preparing for the night before it. The search followed the riders through the parts of their day that surrounded the ride itself.

Then there was the team truck. Its interior was painted white so dust that might affect the bicycles would be easier to spot. The change was modest: a different background made a possible problem more visible. It gave the team another ordinary detail they could attend to.

##Even the color inside the truck became part of the work of preparing to race.##

Five years after Brailsford's appointment, British cycling competed at the 2008 Beijing Olympics. In Clear's account, the team won roughly 60 percent of the cycling gold medals available.

[[lessons]]
$$**Make the small obstacle visible.** The white truck interior changed how easily the team could see dust. That is a useful place to begin with a recurring difficulty of your own: notice what keeps getting overlooked. Choose one task where you repeatedly work around the same problem. Could a change in the setup help you catch it earlier? Try an adjustment where the difficulty actually occurs, then look for evidence that it helped. Familiarity can make an inconvenience easy to accept.$$

$$**Give recovery a place in the plan.** The team's bedding traveled because sleep mattered to race preparation. If your plan for improving a task consists entirely of asking more effort from yourself, look at the time around that effort. What makes it harder to arrive ready or recover afterward? Pick one condition you can reasonably improve. The riders' pillows offer a concrete reminder that caring about performance can include caring about the circumstances in which you rest.$$

[[reflect]]
- Think of a goal you are already working toward. Which ordinary inconvenience do you keep compensating for, rather than addressing?
- What would make that inconvenience easier to notice before it becomes a problem? Consider the team's decision to make dust visible.
- Look at the preparation and recovery around the task. Which part receives less attention than the effort itself?

&&Before you do that task again, which overlooked condition could you change, and what would tell you it helped?&&

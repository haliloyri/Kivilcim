[[open]]
Adam Grant did not start with comfort. He started with three words. After Sheryl Sandberg's husband died, the psychologist told her that people hit by a setback tend to slide into three kinds of thought, each with a name. She recognized all of them within a minute. They had been running her days.

[[story]]
The three Ps come from the research of the psychologist Martin Seligman, and Option B uses them to describe how people think after a blow. Grant, who is a friend of Sandberg's as well as the co-author, explained them to her in the weeks after her husband Dave Goldberg died.

The first is **permanence**. It is the belief that the pain will last forever. The thought sounds like a fact, because in the middle of grief it feels exactly like one. Nothing in the moment suggests it will ever lift.

The second is **pervasiveness**. This is the belief that the loss touches every part of life. Work, family, friendships, plans, everything is stained. Yet a hard area doesn't have to spread to the others. Some parts of life can stay steady while one is falling apart.

The third is **personalization**. It is the belief that it was your fault. The mind goes looking for what it should have done differently and treats the answer as a verdict.

##Sandberg saw that she had fallen into all three.## The thoughts she describes were of the form: this will never end, this has ruined everything, and I could have done something. None of them came with evidence. They came with the volume that grief gives to whatever it says.

What Grant offered was a way to answer. Once each thought has a name, it becomes a claim you can question. Permanence can be turned into right now. Pervasiveness into this part of my life. Personalization into the circumstances that made this possible.

Sandberg doesn't present this as a cure. The sadness stayed. What changed was that she could hear the thoughts as thoughts and argue with them, instead of living inside them. Grant's help was to give her the vocabulary to do that.

[[lessons]]
$$**A name makes it testable.** Forever, everything and my fault all feel like facts. After a failed exam, "I always fail" is permanence. Rewriting it as "I failed this one" gives the thought an edge you can check.$$

$$**Check what is still working.** Pervasiveness is usually wrong. After a breakup, list what held up, such as work, friends, the weekly football game. That doesn't deny the pain. It shows where it stops.$$

$$**Fault and cause are not the same.** Asking what contributed differs from asking who to blame. If a project slipped, "the deadline moved twice" looks outward, while "I ruined it" hangs everything on you.$$

[[reflect]]
- When did you last say always or never about a bad day, like a missed train?
- Which part of your life stayed fine while another fell apart?

Sheryl Sandberg holds two sentences in her head at once. In the first, she would give up anything to have her husband back. In the second, she has learned things since his death that she would never have learned otherwise. Researchers who study this see nothing contradictory about holding both.

[[story]]
Option B, which Sandberg wrote with the psychologist Adam Grant, has a chapter about what researchers call post-traumatic growth. The term describes positive change that some people report after adversity.

The research that gave the term its shape describes five areas where the change shows up. Some people feel a stronger sense of personal strength. Some see new possibilities for their lives. Some find that their relationships with other people deepen. Some appreciate being alive in a way they did not before. And some experience a change in their spiritual or existential outlook.

The book doesn't claim that this happens to everyone or that it makes the loss acceptable. That is the tricky part. Growth is not a prize for suffering, and Sandberg says nothing of the sort.

##She wants her husband back, and she can also see what she has learned.## Both statements are true for her at the same time. After Dave Goldberg died, she writes about gratitude for the people who carried her through and about a closeness with friends that she had not known before. She would trade all of it for his return. The growth is real, and so is the wish.

What makes growth possible, in the book's account, is a willingness to face the pain. Growth is not automatic. Denying what happened, or hurrying to find a bright side, tends to block it. Staying with the loss and working with it, with other people around, keeps the possibility open.

That is why the framing matters for friends and families. Telling someone that good will come of their loss can feel like asking them to be grateful for it. Sandberg's account suggests a better order: first the loss, with nothing added. Later, if it comes, the growth, in their own words and their own time.

[[lessons]]
$$**Hold both sentences.** Grief and growth do not compete. Someone who lost a parent can miss them every day and also notice they now call old friends more often. Both are true.$$

$$**Skip the silver lining.** A bright side offered too fast asks the grieving person to do your work. "At least you have your health," said to someone who just lost a job, ends the conversation. "That sounds heavy, want to talk?" keeps it open.$$

There are two ways to start a sales pitch. One begins with the product and works out how to get people to want it. The other begins with a person and asks what is missing in their world. Seth Godin spends a large part of *This Is Marketing* arguing that only the second deserves the name.

[[story]]
Godin sets two ideas of marketing side by side. The traditional one is about persuasion. You find people, you interrupt them, and you use ads, pressure and tricks to get them to buy. The effort goes into the pitch.

His own version starts somewhere else. Marketing, in his words, means making change happen for the people you serve. It starts with questions. Who is this for? What do they want? What is missing in their world? You answer them before you decide what to make, instead of after.

That is why empathy matters so much in his argument. Empathy here is practical. It means understanding what a person believes, what they want, what worries them, and the story they tell themselves about their own life. The better you understand it, the more your work can fit the way they see things.

When that happens, Godin says, the result looks less like selling and more like help. Imagine a person who has been trying to fix a problem for weeks and finds exactly the thing that fits. They don't feel marketed to. They feel understood.

##Marketing, for Godin, is a promise, and the promise only counts once it has been kept.## The promise tells people what change to expect. Keeping it, again and again, builds the trust that no clever ad can replace.

Godin adds a point about what follows. People tend to share what has really helped them. Passing on a good find is something we do without being asked. If your work does solve someone's problem, you won't have to persuade them to tell others.

He also asks for patience. This kind of attention is earned over time, a promise at a time, with the same people. It will feel slower than a loud campaign.

Seen this way, the marketer's job gets simpler and harder at once. Simpler, because there is no trick to learn. Harder, because the work depends on caring about someone's actual situation.

[[lessons]]
$$**Empathy gives you facts.** What a person believes and fears tells you what they will accept. A tutor who learns a student dreads being called on in class offers very different help from one who assumes the student needs more practice problems.$$

$$**A promise has two halves.** Making it gets attention, and keeping it earns trust. A repair shop that promises "ready Thursday" and delivers on Thursday has done more marketing than any banner.$$

$$**Allow the slow route.** Help that is noticed and shared builds slowly. A neighbour who fixes laptops fairly gets recommended in the building chat months later, with no campaign.$$

[[reflect]]
- A product that fixed something you hadn't managed to name, like a budgeting app that finally made sense.
- A promise your own work makes, such as a delivery date or a reply time. Could you keep it every time?
- Someone you serve, a customer or a teammate, whose worries you could not describe in detail.

&&If you spent an hour listening to one person you serve, what would you most hope to learn?&&

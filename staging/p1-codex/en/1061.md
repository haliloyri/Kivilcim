[[open]]
Jerry Uelsmann gave his photography students two different routes to a grade. Some would be judged on how many photographs they produced. Others needed only one image, but it had to be nearly perfect. The assignments sent students toward the same skill with very different things to do.

[[story]]
James Clear tells the classroom story in *Atomic Habits*. Uelsmann divided his students into a quantity group and a quality group. For the quantity students, producing more photographs meant earning a higher grade. Their assignment gave them a reason to keep making work.

The quality students faced a different demand. A single photograph could earn them a top grade, provided it was nearly perfect. They could concentrate on the standard that one image needed to meet, while the other group had a number of photographs to produce.

The quantity students spent their time taking pictures. They worked with composition and lighting, tried darkroom methods, and learned from mistakes. Their choices kept turning into photographs they could examine. Practice brought them into contact with what those choices actually produced.

The quality students spent their time speculating about perfection. In the book's account, they had little work to show for that attention. Thinking about a photograph occupied time that the other students were spending on the act of making one.

##When the course ended, the best photographs came from the quantity group.##

Those students had accumulated experience with the decisions a good photograph requires. They had made choices, encountered the results, and had opportunities to learn. The assignment that rewarded production had also given them practice in producing quality.

Clear uses the account to distinguish motion from action. Planning, strategizing, and learning can help you prepare. Action produces the outcome itself. In this classroom, discussing a perfect image and making a photograph were different uses of time, even though both concerned photography.

Preparation can feel productive while postponing the risk of making something imperfect. A finished attempt removes that distance: now there is something to look at. The quantity students had images that exposed the consequences of their decisions about light and composition. That gave their learning somewhere specific to begin.

[[lessons]]
$$**Make work you can ask questions of.** The quantity students could examine the results of choices about light and composition because they had taken photographs. Give yourself something equally concrete to inspect in the skill you are practicing. Make a manageable piece of work, then choose one feature to examine. What happened when you tried it? What would you change? This keeps practice connected to learning from the result, rather than assuming that a higher count alone will make the work better.$$

$$**Give preparation a destination.** The quality group's thinking stayed focused on what a perfect image would require. If your own project keeps growing in preparation but produces little work, name the version that preparation is meant to enable. Which question genuinely needs answering before you begin? Which could be answered by making a rough attempt? Let the attempt reveal what you need to learn next. You can return to planning with a difficulty you have encountered, instead of another imagined requirement.$$

[[reflect]]
- Choose a project you have been preparing for. What is the last actual piece of work you made for it?
- Name one question that a rough version could help answer. Keep it specific enough that you could inspect the result.
- Which preparation would help you make that version? Notice anything you could leave until you have something to examine, as the photography students did.

&&What could you make today that would answer a question your planning has left open?&&

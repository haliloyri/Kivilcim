[[open]]
A random number generator is a machine built to be boring. It produces zeros and ones with no pattern, the way an honest coin produces heads and tails. At Princeton, researchers sat volunteers in front of one and made an odd request: don't touch it, just want it to produce more of one number. Lynne McTaggart builds a good part of *The Intention Experiment* on what came next.

[[story]]
The lab was at Princeton University, and the question was simple to state. Could a person change the output of a random machine by intention alone? The machine produced zeros and ones in no pattern. Left alone, over a long run, it should end up close to half and half.

Volunteers sat near it. They did not touch it. Their only task was to try, mentally, to nudge the output toward one outcome. The researchers compared those sessions against runs where nobody tried anything.

To see why the setup mattered, think of a coin. Flip it a million times and you expect heads very close to half the time. A shift of a few hundredths of a percent, repeated session after session in the same direction, would be easy to miss by eye. Statistics exist to catch things like that. That is an illustration, not a figure from the lab.

McTaggart reports that across very many sessions, the lab found a small shift away from chance, and that the shift was statistically significant. She is careful to call it small. It was a result you needed the numbers to see.

##What she takes from it is that the mind might not only watch the world but interact with it.## She uses the lab's work as one of the foundations for the intention experiments she goes on to describe, and she insists the researchers followed rigorous scientific method.

This is where a careful reader should slow down. The account is McTaggart's reading of the lab's findings. Outside the book, other labs that tried similar designs have had mixed results, and many scientists are not convinced. A tiny effect in a machine built to be random is also exactly the kind of thing that subtle flaws can produce.

That doesn't make the question silly. It makes it a good one to hold with two hands: interested in what the lab reported, and in no hurry to treat it as settled.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask how big.** A statistically significant result says an effect is probably not pure chance. It doesn't say the effect is large. A diet that helps people lose a few grams over a year can be significant and still useless to you.$$

$$**Look for the second lab.** A striking result gains weight when others repeat it. If one reviewer claims a gadget changed their life, look for a second who tried it.$$

$$**Curiosity and caution can share a desk.** You can enjoy a strange idea and still hold back belief. Reading a horoscope for fun is harmless until you plan a job change around it.$$

[[reflect]]
- A claim you accepted because it came with numbers, like a supplement ad quoting a percentage.
- A surprising result that stopped looking surprising once someone repeated it.
- A coincidence you noticed, such as thinking of a friend right before they called. Did you test it?

&&What would it take for you to trust a surprising result, and who would you ask to repeat it?&&

[[open]]
Two people turn down a cigarette. One describes an effort to quit; the other describes who they are. James Clear puts those replies side by side in *Atomic Habits*. The cigarette goes unaccepted either way, yet the words point to a difference worth examining.

[[story]]
In Clear's illustration, the first person responds:

> No thanks. I'm trying to quit.

The second responds:

> No thanks. I'm not a smoker.

The first reply leaves the old identity in place: a smoker is trying to stop smoking. The second expresses an identity in which smoking no longer fits. Both people make the same choice in the moment. What differs is the explanation of that choice.

Clear connects this distinction to three layers of change. Outcomes concern the results you want. Processes concern the habits and systems you follow. Identity concerns your beliefs about yourself. Each layer gives you a different place to begin thinking about a habit.

An identity-based approach starts by asking what kind of person you want to become. Then comes the part that gives the answer substance: doing small things that support it. A new description needs experiences you can point to when you ask whether it fits.

Take the book's example of reading. You can set out to finish a book, which gives you an outcome. You can also set out to become a reader. Reading supplies evidence for that identity each time you do it. The description begins to rest on something that has actually happened.

As that evidence accumulates, it can change what you believe about yourself. That belief can influence what you choose to do again. Habits help shape identity, and identity helps shape habits; the two can reinforce each other.

Clear compares each action to a vote for the person you become. Some actions support the identity you want. Others may point away from it. But the comparison has room for more than a flawless record.

##You can have a majority without winning every vote.##

Missing a reading session leaves the earlier reading intact. Picking up the book again adds another piece of evidence. A lapse can be part of the record without becoming its final word.

[[lessons]]
$$**Give the label something real to describe.** Becoming a reader is easier to understand when you connect it to an act of reading. Choose a self-description you want to practice, then identify a small behavior that would give it support. You can do that behavior before the label feels entirely comfortable. Reading a page gives you one completed action to acknowledge. That is a more concrete starting point than repeatedly asking whether you qualify as the kind of person you want to become.$$

$$**Keep a missed vote in perspective.** The voting comparison makes a distinction between an action and the whole pattern. When you miss a reading session, be specific about what happened. Remember the sessions you completed, too. You can then decide how to return to the book without treating the lapse as proof that you never follow through. The next choice remains available, and it can contribute evidence even if the previous choice went another way.$$

[[reflect]]
- Which old description of yourself do you repeat when a habit becomes difficult? Notice the difference between describing one experience and describing your whole character.
- Recall a real action that does not fit that old description. Give it credit for what it was, without asking it to prove a complete transformation.
- What kind of person would you like this habit to help you become, with room for occasional misses?

&&What small action could you take today that your old description of yourself leaves out?&&

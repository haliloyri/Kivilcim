Ask a founder who the product is for, and the answer usually comes back in one word: everyone. Seth Godin has heard that answer so often that he built a whole line of advice against it. His fix sounds like a step backward, because it means picking fewer people on purpose. He thinks the smaller number is the safer bet.

[[story]]
Seth Godin's *This Is Marketing* begins its advice with two questions. Who is it for? And what is it for? Most people rush past the first one. The quick answer is that it's for everyone, and that feels safe, because nobody is left out.

Godin sees a problem. A product that has to suit everyone has to avoid bothering anyone, so it ends up with no sharp edges and no champion. Nobody in particular needs it badly enough to tell a friend about it.

His alternative is the **smallest viable market**. That is the smallest group of people you can serve well while still keeping the work going. Both halves of the phrase do a job. Small, so you can understand these people and do right by them. Viable, so the work can pay for itself. He isn't asking you to chase a tiny audience for its own sake.

So how do you find the group? Godin looks past age and income and toward what people want and what they already believe about the world. Their hopes, their worries, their sense of what's normal. Two people with little else in common can share those, and shared beliefs are what make a group recognizable.

This is where he leans on a phrase he returns to again and again: people like us do things like this. A purchase is rarely only about function. It also says who you are and who you belong with. When your work matches what a certain group already values, choosing it needs no defending. It's simply what people like them do.

##A group that feels seen by your work has both a reason and the words to pass it along.## That's how Godin explains spreading. It moves from one person who cares to the next, inside a community that already trusts itself, and you don't have to push it.

There is a cost, and Godin states it plainly. Choosing a group means leaving others out. The people outside it haven't failed to understand you, and you haven't failed them. The work just isn't for them. Saying so clearly even helps the right people recognize that it is for them.

In Godin's telling, marketing is the effort to bring about change for the people you serve. The first decision in that effort is which people. A founder who can answer with a specific group, instead of everyone, has made the choice the rest depends on.

[[lessons]]
$$**Describe them by what they believe.** The group only exists once you can say what it wants and fears. A bakery aimed at "busy parents who want a birthday cake without a two-day wait" has chosen. One aimed at "everyone who likes cake" hasn't.$$

$$**Run both tests.** Small and viable pull against each other. A newsletter for left-handed guitarists may be easy to serve yet too small to sustain. Check both before you commit.$$

$$**Count the people you leave out.** If nobody is disappointed by your choice, it wasn't specific enough. A gym built for new mothers loses the marathoners, and that loss shows the choice was real.$$

[[reflect]]
- A project you describe as "for everyone," like your team's app. Who would actually miss it most?
- Something you recommend without being asked, such as a restaurant or a podcast. What does choosing it say about people like you?
- A moment you turned someone down because the work wasn't right for them.

&&What would you change in your next piece of work if it had to be for one specific group?&&

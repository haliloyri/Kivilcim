In a hospital cardiac unit, some patients had strangers praying for them by name and had no idea. The volunteers worked from a list. The patients and the people treating them were not supposed to know who was on it. Lynne McTaggart opens a chapter of *The Intention Experiment* with this kind of study, and it is a good test of how you handle a claim that sounds odd.

[[story]]
McTaggart's chapter looks at research on distant healing. The idea is simple to state. A person directs healing intention, often as prayer, toward someone who is somewhere else. Can that change anything for the person on the receiving end?

She describes a controlled, blinded trial in cardiac intensive care. The patients were divided into two groups. For one group, volunteers prayed by name from a list. The patients did not know which group they were in, which matters, because it removes the effect of hope or expectation.

McTaggart reports that the group with volunteers praying for them had fewer complications. She treats this as one result among several in the field and argues that the findings across studies are similar enough to take seriously.

##Her sharpest point is a plain one: hard to explain is not the same as not real.## In her argument, people tend to dismiss these studies as matters of belief, when the right question is what the data show and whether they can be repeated.

That is a fair challenge, and it is worth taking up properly. Here is the other half of the picture, which comes from outside the book. Later, larger trials have been less kind to the idea. The biggest of them, with more than 1,800 heart-surgery patients, found no benefit from being prayed for. Critics also point to weaknesses in some of the earlier trials.

So the account has two honest layers. One is what McTaggart argues: that the early evidence is strong enough to deserve respect. The other is that the question was tested more broadly later, and the answer there was much less encouraging.

None of that has to make someone's good wishes foolish. The wish to support a sick person is real. The sensible thing is to keep that wish separate from a claim about what it does to a heart.

[[lessons]]
$$**Blinding makes odd ideas testable.** Because patients didn't know their group, hope couldn't explain a difference. You can borrow the logic: ask a friend to rate your results without knowing which study routine you used.$$

$$**Keep two claims apart.** Hard to explain and not real are different statements. A card trick is hard to explain, yet nobody thinks it is real magic. In the same way, hard to dismiss doesn't make something proven.$$

$$**One early trial starts the question.** A first striking result is a reason to look for repetition. A restaurant praised in one review deserves a second visit before you recommend it.$$

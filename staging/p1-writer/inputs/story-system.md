## Reader experience and factual boundaries

Write for someone reading on a phone who should want to finish this account,
retell a memorable part, and try one useful idea. Earn that response through
the story's details and choices, not inflated claims or motivational slogans.

- Open with the brief's specific tension, surprising detail, or recognizable
  difficulty. Bring a person, action, object, or choice into focus immediately,
  using only what the brief supports. Prefer active verbs and a lively spoken
  rhythm to an abstract introduction or explanation of why the story matters.
  Create a reason to read the next paragraph without revealing the whole
  outcome. Keep reader-facing advice for the later sections. Vary the opening
  to suit the story. NEVER open with an imperative to the reader ("Picture…",
  "Imagine…", "Think of…", "Consider…", "Suppose…", "Ever noticed…"), a
  rhetorical question, or a prediction of what the reader must feel. Start
  inside the scene: the person, the object, the number, or the odd fact itself.
- For anecdotes, let the reader follow the initial situation, the meaningful
  actions, and the outcome. Let concrete details carry the surprise. For
  concepts, develop the book's example or mechanism toward its central insight;
  do not invent an event or force a victory arc.
- Make the supported outcome visible. Choose the one or two result details
  that best answer the opening tension. Avoid both vague "great success"
  endings when the brief supplies specifics and long lists of statistics.
- Build connection through a recognizable difficulty or choice. Do not invent
  thoughts, dialogue, emotions, sensory details, setbacks, or motives for the
  people in the account. Use quotation formatting only for actual quoted words
  supplied as such in the brief; do not present paraphrases as direct quotes.
- Attribute the account to its book/author naturally, usually once. After that,
  tell the account directly instead of repeatedly saying "the author explains"
  or "the book describes." Retain attribution where needed to distinguish an
  interpretation or uncertain claim from an established fact.
- Respect `confidence_note`: omitted or uncertain details are unavailable for
  embellishment. Keep numbers' scope, timeframe, and denominator intact in ALL
  sections and variants. Do not turn sequence into proof of causation, a later
  result into a claim that no earlier progress occurred, or compounding math
  into a promise of real-world improvement.
- Use short, varied paragraphs, usually 2–4 sentences. Each should advance
  the account or add a necessary insight. After delivering the outcome, move
  on; do not recap the same examples merely to reach the word range. Prefer
  sentences someone could comfortably say in one breath. Let concrete details
  carry the emphasis instead of adding commentary such as "That result gives
  the details their scale." Short sentences can give a surprising choice or
  result room to land, without making every sentence clipped or theatrical.
- Keep the sections distinct: story delivers the account; the single lesson
  explains the one implication that matters, anchored in it; reflection is one
  question that sends the reader to their own life; use cards offer natural
  words for a concrete next step (they are shown on the conversation screen,
  not on the reading page).
  Do not make the reader do the same exercise in every section.
- In at least one use card, let the line to say briefly connect a specific
  detail or mechanism from the brief to the proposed action. The reader should
  be able to bring this story into a real conversation, not only repeat generic
  advice. Keep the connection short and understandable without having read the
  story. Do not force an anecdote into every card, speak like a coach diagnosing
  someone, or imply that the listener can expect the protagonist's result.
- End with a manageable next move consistent with the brief: one action,
  decision, or conversation at a clear opportunity, and something to notice
  if useful. Avoid a long checklist, arbitrary commitment periods, guaranteed
  success, or making the reader responsible for an ambitious transformation.
  An application is a proposal for the reader, not another fact about the book.
- Keep the distinctive idea of this story. A library of different books should
  not become a collection of interchangeable advice about habits or hard work.

## Voice: how a paragraph earns its place

The reader is on a phone and gives you about forty seconds before deciding to
stay. Earn that with verbs, bodies and objects, not with explanation. These are
requirements, not preferences:

- Every [[story]] paragraph carries ONE physical beat taken from the brief: a
  hand, an object, a repeated action, a place, a figure said out loud. If a
  paragraph has none, it is commentary — re-derive it from `facts` and
  `scene_details`, or cut it.
- Rhythm budget: each paragraph holds at least one long sentence
  ({VOICE_LONG}+ words) and at least one short one ({VOICE_SHORT} words or
  fewer), and never three sentences of similar length in a row. The story
  should breathe, not march.
- At most ONE paragraph in the entry may close on a one-line aphorism. Three
  seven-word verdicts in a row turn a story into a quote card.
- Prefer the brief's own nouns to commentary about them: "the same balance
  sheet, the same earnings" does more than "the essence does not change" —
  and neither is allowed unless the brief supports it.
- Read the first three sentences aloud. If they could open any article on this
  topic, rewrite them until they could only open this one.
- Never explain the story to the reader from inside the story, and never state
  the lesson before the lesson section.

## Reading time and where the words go

The whole entry is a 3–5 minute read (about 200 words per minute): roughly
{LEN_TOTAL} words across all sections. Spend that budget on the STORY, which
must carry at least 55% of the words. The story is what the reader came for
and what they will retell; everything after it is short.

- Every number, name and example appears in full ONCE, in [[story]]. Lessons,
  reflection and use cards may point back to it in a few words ("the pillows",
  "the lunch break") but must not restate figures, dates or the outcome.
- The lesson adds an implication the story did not already state. If it could
  be deleted without the reader losing anything, replace it. It is not a second
  retelling.
- [[reflect]] holds ONLY the closing `&&question&&`. No bullets.
- [[open]] is a hook, not a summary, and does not reveal the outcome that
  [[story]] delivers. It is not a citation: save the book title, author and
  year for [[story]].
- Never pad to reach a word count. Each point appears once. Before adding a
  paragraph, check that it carries a NEW supported fact or mechanism from the
  brief; a paragraph that says "you may ignore it" / "the decision is yours" /
  "the price is only an offer" for a second or third time must be cut, not
  lengthened.
- If you finish UNDER the range, you have skipped a supported beat or written
  an abstraction where the brief gave an act. Go back to `facts` and
  `scene_details` and render one more concrete beat. Do not add commentary to
  reach the count. Only when the brief itself has nothing left may the entry
  finish at most 10% under the floor — and never explain that inside the text.

## Lesson and question rules (decided 2026-10-28, reading-page redesign)

The reading page is no longer one long scroll of six equal sections. It is a
reading layer (open, story, one lesson, one question, pocket); the three use
cards belong to the "Use in conversation" screen. Therefore:

1. EXACTLY ONE lesson (`$$...$$`) and EXACTLY ONE reflection question
   (`&&...&&`). Never 2–3 lessons, never reflect bullets. Pick the single most
   central implication. If the brief lists several, the others are dropped, not
   merged. The `[[use]]` cards (exactly 3) and `[[pocket]]` stay as before.
2. The lesson must not be short or telegraphic. Bold title, then 3–4 complete
   sentences in this order: the principle, one concrete everyday-life example
   (a reader's work, home or friendships; NOT a second retelling of the story's
   event), and what it lets the reader do or notice. Every sentence is a full
   sentence with its own verb. No stacked fragments.
3. The example in the lesson is invented-but-ordinary (a baker, a repair shop,
   a failed exam). It must not be presented as a fact from the book, must not
   contain numbers or named people, and must not contradict the brief.
4. The question is personal and answerable in one line of writing ("What would
   you change in your next piece of work if ...?"). No yes/no question, no
   advice disguised as a question, no repeat of the lesson's example. It is
   ONE sentence of 15–30 words with one question mark: no second clause that
   asks something else ("...and looking back, what was ...?").
5. Keep the lesson free of machine tells (see "Keep the prose human"). In
   addition avoid: ornamental metaphors ("opens a door", "a lens", "a
   compass"), the "X does not mean Y, it means Z" pattern, and a closing
   moral sentence that only restates the title.
   The bold title is a plain principle or action phrase in the reader's terms
   ("Read a price as an offer"), not an aphorism of the form "X is not a Y".
   The example has one clear actor and a situation a reader recognises at once;
   no unexplained third party (for example a neighbour who for no reason prices
   your shop) and no puzzle the reader must solve to see the point.
6. Story length: aim for the lower half of {LEN_STORY}. A faithful short story
   beats padding, but the story must still carry at least 55% of the entry.

## Languages: write ALL of them

The app shows the user's language, not English. A story that exists only in
`en` looks like a plain old text to a Turkish, Spanish or German reader
because the old plain-story rows stay in those languages. Every story must be
delivered in tr, en, es and de in the same P1 format before it counts as
done. Write each language natively (not word-for-word); the lesson example may
be localized. A book whose original title is NOT English appears as "English
Title (Local Title)" at first mention. If the brief's title is already in
English, use it exactly as given and add no translated title in parentheses.

## Honesty and caveats

- The source text can be unreliable. Never keep an unverifiable or wrong claim
  just because it was in the source; set `confidence` in the brief honestly
  (low when the book gives little) and tell less rather than invent.
- Where outside research contradicts the book's claim (for example later,
  larger trials), state it briefly in the story as an editorial caveat. Do
  not present contested claims as established fact.

## Keep the prose human

These are the tells of machine-written text. Avoid them.

- The "not X, but Y" move ("It isn't X. It's Y.", "Not one big change.
  Hundreds of small ones.", "doesn't just A; it B"): at most ONE in the whole
  entry, and only when the book itself draws that contrast.
- Announcer transitions: "Here is the catch", "Then something striking
  happened", "The answer came faster than anyone expected", "What happened
  next surprised everyone". Just say what happened. The same goes for
  bookkeeping transitions that announce the next point instead of making it:
  "The author also stresses one more thing", "Graham underlines another
  point", "Another important idea is". State the point itself.
- Aphorism endings: do not end every section or paragraph on a polished
  one-line moral. One earned line in the whole entry is plenty.
- Triplets: do not default to lists of exactly three items or three parallel
  clauses. Use the number the facts actually have.
- Template lesson: avoid the stiff "bold claim + restated example + so… moral"
  shape and one-line fragments. Write it as flowing, complete sentences.
- Stock vocabulary: avoid "delve, tapestry, testament, pivotal, intricate, underscore, showcase, vibrant, crucial, enduring, landscape (abstract), stands/serves as, nestled, groundbreaking" and their equivalents in the target language, plus filler such as "in order to", "it is important to note", "at its core", "the real question is", "let's dive in", and generic upbeat endings ("the future looks bright"). Say the plain thing instead.
- Em dashes: at most 2 in the whole entry. Prefer a period or a comma.
- Pseudo-profound lines: no sentence where an abstraction performs an action
  ("the metaphor puts a pause between two reflexes", "the image opens a
  space"). No ornamental metaphor of your own (compass, lens, door, mirror,
  "offer book"). No stock phrases such as "missing the train", "under normal
  conditions nobody would", "that is exactly what people do". No sweeping claims
  about what people or markets always do, and no "generations learned this from
  this book" unless the brief states it as a supported fact.
- Rhythm: vary sentence length. Mix short sentences with 15–25 word ones; never
  run four or more short sentences of equal length in a row ("Same work. Same
  profit. Only the offer changes."). Do not repeat a character's or concept's
  name every sentence: use a pronoun or the role after the first mention in a
  paragraph. In Turkish use at most two semicolons in the whole entry.
- Texture: prefer the brief's own concrete nouns and mechanisms (the balance
  sheet, the closed door, the warning sound) to commentary about them. Give one
  such concrete detail in every paragraph of [[story]].
- Use cards: the line to say must sound like a real person talking, at most
  35 words. Avoid tidy scaffolding such as "What if we…?" or "Let's take a
  moment to…" in more than one card.

## Sources and facts

- Name the book, author, and any year or edition ONLY as given in the brief's
  `book` field. Never add a publication year, a translated title, or a
  "published in <language> as…" clause that the brief does not contain.
- Attribute each claim to the book it actually comes from. If the brief marks
  the source or attribution as uncertain, phrase it as the author's claim, or
  leave the detail out.
- Do not add context, causes, or comparisons ("same hospital, same staff") the
  brief does not support. A study's scope stays exactly as the brief gives it.
- Allegories and concepts are not licences to embroider. Do not add ratios or
  shares ("fifty percent"), personality labels ("a very helpful partner"),
  motives or inner reasoning ("he is sure the firm will sink", "he almost
  begs"), questions the author "stops to ask", or dialogue the brief does not
  contain. Use the allegory exactly as far as the brief states it.
- Reception claims ("the most translated book", "generations of investors
  learned this", a famous person endorsed it) appear only when the brief lists
  them as supported facts, and then in one hedged clause.
- Your own illustrations (a made-up person or object used to show the idea) are
  allowed only in the lesson and use cards, short, and never in [[story]].
  If you would have to mark something as "not from the book", it does not belong
  in the story. Never talk about the writing inside [[story]] ("let's look at an
  example", "this example is not from the book"): it breaks the scene.

## Format (exact markup)

Section tags, alone on their own line, in this exact order:
`[[open]]` `[[story]]` `[[lessons]]` `[[reflect]]` `[[use]]` `[[pocket]]`

Block markers:
- `$$**Title.** body$$` — the lesson card. [[lessons]] needs EXACTLY ONE,
  starting with a **bold** title. See "Lesson and question rules" below.
- `> text` — a quoted line / dialogue, inside [[story]].
- `%%context:<slug> | Situation :: "line to say"%%` — a use-case card.
  [[use]] needs EXACTLY 3 of these. `<slug>` must be one of: meeting,
  oneonone, family, social, self — using at least 2 distinct values across
  the 3 cards. Base these on `use_cases` in the brief. The literal
  `context:` prefix is REQUIRED — write `%%context:oneonone | …%%`, never
  `%%oneonone | …%%`.
- `@@sentence@@` — the single pocket/takeaway line, inside [[pocket]]. One
  only, ≤ 20 words. Carry a distinctive image, choice, or mechanism from this
  story into a useful takeaway. It must make sense on a standalone share card;
  do not rely on "that detail" or another unexplained reference. Keep any first
  step manageable. Reject a line that could close many unrelated stories
  unchanged, including stock advice about "one small change" or "your next
  attempt." A named object alone is not enough: its link to the lesson must
  be clear. Keep the reflective takeaway distinct from the punchline's surprise.
- `&&question&&` — the single closing reflection question, inside
  [[reflect]], at the very end of that section. One only.
- `##sentence##` — at most one highlighted moment, inside [[story]]. Select
  a genuine turning point or earned insight, not a repeat of the opening hook.
- `~~before :: after~~` — an optional before/after contrast (rarely needed).

Inline: `**bold**` (sparingly) and `*italic*` (book titles, an inner voice).

## Also write the conversation and sharing variants

From the SAME brief, also write the short, independent strings for a
separate screen where the reader picks a ready-to-say line. They must not
be near-verbatim copies of the [[pocket]] or [[reflect]] sentences — same
underlying story, different framing:
- `punchline`: one short, natural sentence centered on a single vivid surprise
  or story-specific insight. It should work aloud and on a standalone share
  card without the full story. Avoid a list of details, exaggerated causation,
  vague pronouns, clickbait, and generic inspiration.
- `thirty_sec`: a self-contained ~50–90 word retelling with a clear setup,
  meaningful action, and supported outcome; for a concept, use its example
  or mechanism and resulting insight. Keep only the details needed to make
  the change understandable and memorable. Sound like someone telling a
  friend the story. Usually lead with the person, situation, or surprising
  choice; give attribution in a short, natural clause where useful or needed.
  Avoid a formal reporting preamble such as "The author recounts how [person]
  approached [abstract problem]." Use direct verbs and short, varied sentences
  that are easy to say aloud. Keep the strongest supported outcome and a few
  memorable details; cut the inventory of equipment, dates, or achievements.
  Avoid repeated author references, an unsupported statistic,
  or filler endings such as "What I like about this is...". Do not invent
  personal opinions or experiences for the user who will share the text.
- `question`: NO LONGER SHOWN in the app (decided 2026-10-28; the conversation
  screen shows the three `[[use]]` cards plus the `thirty_sec` retelling).
  Keep the key in the JSON with an empty string `""`. Do not spend words on it.
- `key_contrast`: a 2–4 word label capturing this story's meaningful shift
  or contrast. Use familiar, natural words in the target language; avoid
  abstract jargon and generic slogans. It need not be a literal "X versus Y"
  construction, and must not promise a change the account does not support.

All four strings must stand on their own for a reader who has not opened
the story. Keep them in plain text without markup, hashtags, or promotional
calls to action. Preserve exactly these four JSON keys; do not add separate
social-media fields or duplicate the same takeaway in different lengths.

Before returning the two blocks, silently check: factual support and number
scope; an immediate, lively opening with no book title, author or year in it; a
physical beat in every story paragraph; the rhythm budget (one long and one
short sentence per paragraph, no three similar lengths in a row); at most one
aphorism ending; no talk about the writing inside the story; the lesson as 3–4
full sentences; a single question mark in the reflection; a clear outcome or
insight; no repeated recap; one feasible application and a natural story
connection in at least one use card; a pocket line that could not be swapped
into unrelated stories; spoken rhythm in the short retelling; variants with
different jobs; exact markup and section lengths. Mentally say the use lines
and retelling aloud: replace formal reporting or coaching language with words
someone would use. Fix weak passages rather than appending an explanation or
quality report.

### en — reference (1426)

```story
[[open]]
Mozart's first four piano concertos, written when he was eleven, contain no original music. They are arrangements stitched together from other composers' pieces. The first concerto now counted as a masterwork came at twenty-one, after more than eighteen years of training.

[[story]]
Geoff Colvin's *Talent Is Overrated* (Yetenek Yanılgısı, 2008) builds one of its main cases on Mozart, the standard picture of the born genius. A child prodigy who simply had it. Colvin opens the file and finds a different story.

Wolfgang's father, Leopold Mozart, was a well-known violin teacher. In 1756, the year Wolfgang was born, he published a large book on how to teach the violin. So a method was already written down when his son arrived. Systematic training began in early childhood, by age three at the latest, and kept going for years. From about age six Leopold took the children on tours across Europe, and Wolfgang spent much of his childhood on stage and on the road.

##The earliest compositions are in Leopold's handwriting, not Wolfgang's.## The child could not yet write them down himself, so his father did. Leopold also stopped composing his own music once his son started, a detail Colvin treats as telling.

The next detail is less comfortable. The first four piano concertos, written at eleven, contain no original music. They are arrangements assembled from other composers' pieces. The first work now counted as a masterwork is the Ninth Piano Concerto, written at twenty-one, after more than eighteen years of training. There was early output, but not early mastery.

Colvin is not saying Wolfgang was ordinary. His argument is more measured: explaining genius does not require a talent factor separate from practice. What nobody sees is where the extraordinary begins, because it begins inside a house, over years, not in a concert hall.

One caveat belongs here. Colvin's book appeared in 2008 and its central thesis has weakened since. A 2014 meta-analysis by Macnamara, Hambrick and Oswald found that deliberate practice explained about 26 percent of the differences in performance in games, 21 percent in music and 18 percent in sports. In music, practice explains a lot, but not everything. Building Mozart out of hours alone is as incomplete as building him out of gift alone.

Even so, the neglected side of the story is the practice side. When Mozart is told, the three-year-old is told. The teacher with the book behind him rarely is.

[[lessons]]
$$**Behind early success there is usually an early start.** The neighbour's kid who beats everyone in the pool is often the one who has been driven to training several mornings a week, and from the outside that looks like a gift. Before you marvel at someone's talent, ask what routine stands behind it and who has been working beside them. You will read them more accurately, and you will see your own gaps more accurately too. Praise for a gift often hides a long, quiet schedule that nobody photographed.$$

[[reflect]]
&&What is one area you have written off as "no talent" without ever practising it regularly with someone's guidance?&&

[[use]]
%%context:family | A child you know is praised as naturally gifted at something :: "Even Mozart's first scores were written down by his father. Who works with you on this, and for how long?"%%

%%context:self | You tell yourself "I have no talent for this" :: "I have never really tried it regularly. Let me book one twenty-minute session and see what changes."%%

%%context:social | A friend's success gets brushed off as natural talent :: "How did you learn it? What did you work on in the first years, and with whom?"%%

[[pocket]]
@@Behind a prodigy stands a teacher. Before calling someone a genius, ask who worked beside them for years.@@
```

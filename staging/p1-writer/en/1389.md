John Denver could not write songs. When he and Tony Robbins went back through the times his best ones had come, they found that each followed something physical: a ski run, a flight, a fast drive. The cure for his block was not at a desk.

[[story]]
Tony Robbins says in *Awaken the Giant Within* (1991) that he worked with John Denver when the singer had writer's block. They went back through the times Denver had written his best songs and found a pattern. A whole song would flow through him after he had skied down a mountain, flown his jet or his biplane, or driven his sports car at high speed. Speed was usually involved.

Denver was frustrated about a few things in his life at that moment and had not been doing the same intense outdoor activity. Robbins says that getting back into a strong physiology restored the certainty and flow of his creativity immediately. The capability had been there all along.

That case sits inside Robbins's idea of a state: the sum of millions of neurological processes, our whole experience at a given moment. He says that forgetting a friend's name, or how to spell "house," does not mean you are stupid. It means you were in a stupid state. He is blunt about it, and he holds that acting badly or brilliantly depends on the state of your mind and body, not on your ability.

There are two main ways to change a state, he says: use your body differently, or change your focus. Physiology is the electricity a television set needs, and focus is the channel. ##Emotion, he writes, is created by motion.## Every emotion comes with its own posture, its own breathing, its own pattern of movement and facial expression.

He asks readers to try it. Swing your arms in and out like a bored, humorless symphony conductor, very slowly, with a bored face, and notice how that feels. Then clap your hands explosively, snap them apart with a big silly grin and a loud sound, and the feeling changes at once, he says.

The faces and bodies we use most set the limits. We have over eighty muscles in the face, and if they settle into boredom or frustration, that habit starts to dictate our states. In his seminars, Robbins says, people write down every emotion they feel in an average week, and the average is fewer than a dozen.

[[lessons]]
$$**When you cannot do what you know how to do, change your state first.** A bookkeeper who stares at figures that will not add up late in the afternoon may do better walking up and down the stairs than reading them a second time. The numbers have not changed, but the body that is looking at them has, and that is the part you can move at once. Treat a movement break as part of the work, and notice afterwards what you can see that you could not see before, whether that is a mistake in the figures or simply a calmer way of looking.$$

Outside her teenage son's door, a mother could hear the muffled sounds of a computer game and pictured heaving a giant boulder into the screen. It was a fantasy she'd had before. This time she stayed where she was, and what she felt over the next few minutes changed.

[[story]]
Tara Brach tells this scene in *Radical Acceptance* (2003). Halfway through her son Narayan's eighth-grade year, the two of them were locked in a painful, angry standoff. His grades were sliding, and they fought daily over computer play, homework, phone time and bedtime. She scolded, he tuned out, and his room was turning into a late-night video arcade.

Her anger seeped into everything. She sat in the car stewing when he kept her waiting at a friend's house. She pounced when he forgot to feed the dog or change the cat litter. When he asked her to order a pizza, she answered, "Well, you didn't clean your room, so why should I?"

Lying awake one night, she pictured looking back on his teenage years and seeing that misunderstanding and anger had devoured their time together. It jarred her. She regularly practiced the art of pausing, but she had forgotten it for weeks, and she resolved to pause before their next encounter.

The next evening, half an hour past the agreed homework time, she stood outside his closed door. Through it came the muffled sounds of Everquest, his favorite game. Anger mounted as she imagined him with his eyes glued to the glowing screen, and she saw herself heaving the boulder. Instead, she waited.

In the pause she began to notice her body. The anger was a mounting pressure in her chest and throat, her shoulders and hands were tight, her jaw clenched, her heart pounding, her face hot. Barging in would have been far easier.

Then the sensations shifted. The pressure gave way to a deep soreness, as if a fist were gripping her heart. ##"I'm afraid of something," she realized.## She was afraid that Narayan would fail in life and be unhappy, and that it was all her fault. When the story turned to "It's his fault I feel like this," she almost stormed in, and she brought her attention back to her body. A heavy ache came, her eyes grew moist, and the stories of blame and failure receded. Tenderness grew in their place.

She knocked and heard a mumbled "C'min." Narayan looked up with guilty eyes. "I was just getting off, Mom. What time is it anyway?" His watch sat on the dresser, not on his wrist. "Are you angry? I'm sorry . . . I just lost track." She pulled a chair up close and said, "It's okay, hon, but we need to talk."

What she told him about study habits and their agreements was nothing new. She felt different, aware of her breath, her posture, where her hands rested. When he spoke she listened, and she heard how much he got from mastering the game. When she kissed the top of his head and left, there was a good warm feeling between them.

[[lessons]]
$$**Pause long enough to look under the anger.** Anger often sits on top of something harder to feel, and a short wait can show you what it is. A manager irritated by a colleague's late report could hold off on replying for a minute, notice the tension in the chest, and find the worry underneath that the whole project may slip. The message that gets written then is about the real concern, so it is easier for the other person to answer. A minute of waiting is often all it takes to keep an accusation out of the conversation, and to say the same thing in a calmer voice.$$

His messengers had a strict rule about the night. Never wake him with good news. Bad news was different: a crisis unfolding, a development that threatened the campaign, and they were to rouse him at once, whatever the hour. Meanwhile, his ordinary letters waited weeks.

[[story]]
The man was Napoleon, and Ryan Holiday tells the story in *Stillness Is the Key*, at the start of a chapter called "Limit Your Inputs." As a general, Napoleon made a habit of delaying his replies. His secretary was told to wait three weeks before opening any correspondence at all.

When Napoleon finally heard what a letter said, he loved to point out how many supposedly important issues had resolved themselves in the meantime. They needed no reply. Holiday is careful about what this was not. Napoleon was never negligent, never out of touch with his government or his soldiers. He was choosing who and what got access to his attention.

The night rule completed the system. "Rouse me instantly," he said of bad news, "for then there is not a moment to be lost." The sealed letters and the knock at the door were two ends of the same filter. One let the trivial burn out on its own, the other made sure the urgent never waited.

Holiday sets this against what political scientists in the 1990s began calling the "CNN Effect": breathless twenty-four-hour coverage that leaves leaders able only to react. He quotes Epictetus, who advised being content to appear clueless or stupid in extraneous matters if you wish to improve. Napoleon accepted exactly that cost. Some people were upset with him, and he missed some gossip.

Flooding someone with information, Holiday points out, is an old tactic. Lawyers try to bury the other side in paper, intelligence services drown an enemy in propaganda, and the result has a name: analysis paralysis. His advice is plain. Don't follow the news in real time, be a season behind on trends, and don't let your inbox run your life.

##A century and a half later, Eisenhower refused to be handed unopened mail.## He had noticed that much of what came at him was urgent but not important, while most of what truly mattered was not time-sensitive at all. Sorting his inputs that way let his staff be strategic instead of reactive.

Holiday adds a third voice. In her diary in 1942, Dorothy Day told herself to turn off the radio and put away the daily paper. The important things, Holiday argues, will still be important when you get to them. The unimportant will have shown what they were, or simply vanished.

[[lessons]]
$$**Sort what reaches you into what can wait and what cannot.** When every notification gets the same instant response, the issues that would have settled by themselves consume the same attention as the few that are genuinely urgent. A small shop owner can check supplier emails twice a day while keeping the phone switched on for the delivery driver, building that sorting into an ordinary day. Deciding in advance what deserves to wake you lets you leave the rest waiting on purpose, without the low hum of guilt that usually comes with an unread inbox.$$

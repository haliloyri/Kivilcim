Advice, support, assistance, affection and care: that is the list of what other people can give someone recovering from trauma. Judith Herman wrote the list on purpose, and she left off the one word that most helpers are hoping to be able to add.

[[story]]
Judith Herman, a psychiatrist, published *Trauma and Recovery* in 1992. She describes recovery in three stages: establishing safety, remembrance and mourning, and reconnection with ordinary life. Safety comes first. "The first task of recovery is to establish the survivor's safety," she writes, and it takes precedence over all others.

Before any of the later work, she sets down a principle. "The first principle of recovery is empowerment of the survivor." The survivor, she goes on, "must be the author and arbiter of her own recovery."

Then comes the list, aimed at everyone who wants to help. Others can offer "advice, support, assistance, affection, and care, but not cure." Herman presses the point in practical terms: an intervention that takes control away from the survivor can damage recovery, even when it looks like it serves her interest.

The helper's part has a name in Herman's account. In the second stage the therapist plays the role of witness and ally, in whose presence the survivor can speak of the unspeakable. The survivor also sets the pace. Approaching painful memories too precipitately, Herman warns, leads to a fruitless and damaging reliving of the trauma, so the choice of when to go there stays with the person who lived it.

##A helper's part is real and limited: care can be given, cure cannot.##

The principle does not send the survivor off alone. Herman writes that recovery can take place only within the context of relationships; it cannot occur in isolation. Trauma isolates, she says, and a group can re-create a sense of belonging and bear witness.
The book holds both requirements together. The person is in charge of her recovery, and she cannot do it by herself. Everything a helper does has to fit between those two sentences.

[[lessons]]
$$**Offer your help and leave the decision with the person.** A neighbour recovering from a hard year is better served by someone who brings soup and asks what would be useful than by someone who reorganizes her kitchen while she watches. Care lands better when the person can still say yes or no to it, and being allowed to refuse is part of what makes the offer safe to accept. Before you step in next time, ask what would help this week, and offer only that.$$

In an old photograph, a four-month-old boy is held up by his mother. A yellow star shows on her jacket. The baby's face is tense, and his eyes look somewhere far beyond the camera. He would grow up to be a physician and write a book about attention.

[[story]]
The boy was Gabor Maté, born in Budapest in January 1944, the first child of Jewish parents. Two and a half months after his birth, Hungary came under German occupation. Two days after the occupation began, his mother phoned the pediatrician and asked him to come: the baby had been crying almost without stop since the day before. The doctor said he would, then added: "All my Jewish babies are crying."

In *Scattered Minds*, Maté reads this as a lesson in how infants take in their parents' anxiety. They were loved no less than children anywhere, he writes. The fear still reached them.

That December the family sought shelter in a so-called protected house under the thin cover of the Swiss embassy. Roughly two thousand people were packed into it, and there was hardly anything to feed the baby. His mother handed him to a complete stranger, a non-Jewish woman who agreed to push his buggy to the home of a relative. Years later his mother said she could not have been in a normal state of mind, that she stayed strangely calm.

##When they met again three weeks later, he treated his mother as a stranger.##

The Red Army took Budapest and mother and son were reunited. By her account, Gabor would not so much as look at her for days.

Maté offers the story as an illustration, not as proof. His point is that the bond with a caregiver, and her emotional state, shape a baby's brain. He has attention deficit disorder himself, he writes, and his three children were diagnosed with it too. He is also frank that he cannot know whether he would have developed it anyway without the events of 1944.

One caution belongs here. His view that attention problems come largely from early environment is a minority position in clinical literature, and there is strong evidence for a hereditary role. The book does not rule out genes: it says what is inherited is sensitivity. Diagnosis and treatment belong with a physician.

[[lessons]]
$$**When attention is a problem, ask what someone has been living through.** When a person is scattered or distracted, the eye goes straight to a defect, yet the last few months of their life say just as much. A parent whose child keeps forgetting homework could sit down with the child and write what has changed at home and at school in recent weeks before booking extra tutoring. That gives you context as well as a label, something concrete to say, and you can bring more to the doctor if you decide to see one.$$

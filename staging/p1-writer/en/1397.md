[[open]]
Two rival companies invited Simon Sinek to speak at their education summits, and he accepted both. At each one he also heard the company's own presenters. What he heard in the two rooms had almost nothing in common.

[[story]]
Simon Sinek tells this in *The Infinite Game* (2019). One invitation came from Microsoft, the other from Apple, and both were for events about education.

At Microsoft's summit the presenters spent much of their time on how they would beat Apple. Every talk circled back to the same question: how do we beat Apple? The room was busy with a competitor who was not even in it.

At Apple's summit the presenters talked about how Apple was helping teachers teach. Microsoft hardly came up. Sinek reads the contrast as two mindsets: Apple's speakers cared little about beating a competitor, which he takes as a sign of an infinite mindset.

Summaries of the book use what followed at Microsoft as the example of fixation on a rival. It answered the iPod with the Zune, which did not catch on, and Steve Ballmer dismissed the iPhone as not worth replicating. The summaries read this as a company reacting to Apple instead of choosing its own direction.

From there he builds the idea of the worthy rival. A worthy rival is a competitor who is better than you at something, and so shows you where you could improve. The person across the table becomes a source of information about your own work, instead of a scoreboard. The question shifts from beating them to watching them: what do they do better than you?

He admits he did not always see rivals like this. ##For years he treated the author Adam Grant as a competitor, and after meeting Grant he concluded that the way he had seen him said more about himself than about Grant.##

Sinek says a healthier attitude toward rivals improves resilience, resource management, innovation and personal integrity. A reviewer found the chapter's opening vignette overly romanticized, and the claims about those gains rest on Sinek's word more than on measurement.

[[lessons]]
$$**Study the person who does it better.** A café owner whose neighbour bakes better bread can cut her prices to win customers back, or she can ask what the neighbour does at five in the morning. The second path costs nothing and tells her something specific about her own ovens. Looking at the rival's work, rather than at the rival, turns annoyance into a list of things you could try this month. And you stop letting the rival decide what you do next.$$

The search box now gets questions people once took to a priest, a king or their own feelings. A book says the algorithms behind it may come to know you better than you know yourself. Critics say that is a claim, not a finding.

[[story]]
Google Flu Trends flagged flu outbreaks from search data ahead of conventional health services. Yuval Noah Harari lists it in Homo Deus among his Google examples, next to the Baseline Study and Google Fit, as part of an argument about a belief he calls Dataism.

The term was first used by David Brooks in the New York Times in 2013. In Harari's account, Dataism holds that the universe consists of data flows and that the value of any phenomenon or entity lies in its contribution to data processing. Harari says it began as neutral science and is turning into a religion that claims to say what is right and wrong.

The idea rests on algorithms. He defines an algorithm as a methodical set of steps for calculating, solving problems and making decisions, and he says that organisms are algorithms and that what we call sensations and emotions are in fact algorithms too. 'Algorithm', he writes, is arguably the single most important concept in our world. Writing made it possible to organize societies algorithmically, with each person a step in a larger process, which is the essence of bureaucracy.

From there comes his claim that external algorithms may hack humanity and come to know people better than they know themselves. Google and Facebook algorithms, he says, know how you feel and many other things you hardly suspect.

He pictures people carrying biometric devices that monitor their health, and medicine shifting from healing the sick to upgrading the healthy. Personal data, he writes, is one of the most valuable things people still have to offer, and the value lies in turning experiences into freely flowing data.

The shift in authority fits this picture. It moves from kings, popes and one's own feelings to data. Facing a decision about life, people ask Google or Facebook instead of God or their feelings.

Critics say the claim that algorithms know you better takes the algorithms' own claims at face value and rests on little empirical data. In the book it reads as a possibility the author weighs, not a fact he reports.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask what a recommendation measured about you.** A score or ranking does not know everything about you, only what it was able to measure. Someone who looks at a sleep score from an app, feels rested and still counts the night as bad can ask what the score captured and what it left out, and notice what their own feeling was carrying. They can then use the number as one more observation next to their own rather than as the last word, and when the two disagree they can say plainly which one they trust and why.$$

724 men answered questions about their work, their health, their marriages and their loneliness for decades. When the researchers looked at the records years later, one thing stood out beside money and success, and it did not shout. It kept showing up in the same place.

[[story]]
In *The Good Life*, Robert Waldinger and Marc Schulz describe the Harvard Study of Adult Development. It began in the late 1930s and followed 724 men through their lives. Of these, 268 were Harvard sophomores and 456 were boys from poor neighborhoods in Boston, two very different worlds studied with the same questions.

Researchers interviewed the men, had them fill in questionnaires and looked at their medical records, so that work, health and family life could be set side by side. The answers piled up over years. Waldinger is now the study's fourth director, and Schulz is its associate director.

The study's most striking feature is its length, since few projects keep asking the same people questions for so long. The same people were asked similar questions at different stages of their lives for decades. The book's main finding is that the strength of people's connections with others predicts the health of both body and brain as they age. Success or income alone did not decide a good life, and who stood beside a man mattered as much as the title he held.

##Waldinger points to relationships as one of the strongest signals of health.##

In a talk he has given, Waldinger said that satisfaction with relationships at age fifty predicted health at eighty better than cholesterol did. It fits the book's general line. The first group was all men, a limitation, and the study was later widened to include the men's wives and children.

The practical side is ordinary, and the authors treat it as the point of the whole project. Relationships need upkeep, much as health does. The book's suggestions are small and available to anyone: call an old friend, do not postpone a difficult conversation, send a message. They do not look like investments, but they add up over time and can change what a life looks like years later.

[[lessons]]
$$**Maintain your relationships as regularly as you look after your health.** A person who has nodded to the same neighbors in an apartment block for years may discover, on a bad day, that there is someone to knock on. What the Harvard study shows is that connection is not built once and then left. A call, a visit or an honest talk that you do not put off makes it likelier that the relationship is ready on a hard day, and it makes the first step toward someone feel smaller.$$

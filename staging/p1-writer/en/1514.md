Two-thirds of the companies that made 14-inch disk drives never built an 8-inch one, and the rest got there about two years late. Nobody had been asleep. These firms were doing exactly what their best customers asked, year after year, with a lot of skill.

[[story]]
Clayton Christensen's *The Innovator's Dilemma* (1997) takes its main case from disk drives. In the 1970s the standard drive was 14 inches wide and went to mainframe makers, who wanted capacity above all. The companies that built it listened closely to those customers and gave them what they asked for.

That listening had a direction. The customers pulled the makers along a capacity curve of about 22 percent a year, and Christensen says the leaders were held captive by customers. Passivity or fear of risk had nothing to do with it. Established firms led nearly every sustaining improvement, the kind that makes the existing product better for existing buyers.

Then entrants introduced the 8-inch drive, between 1978 and 1980. It held less than a 14-inch drive and suited minicomputers, not mainframes. A mainframe maker had no use for less capacity. It cost more per megabyte at first, though its total price was lower, and buyers valued its smaller size. Later its cost per megabyte fell below the 14-inch drive's, and it moved into the low end of the mainframe market. Two-thirds of the 14-inch makers never entered the 8-inch market, and those that did arrived about two years behind the startups. By Christensen's account, every 14-inch maker eventually failed.

The pattern repeated. Seagate's 5.25-inch drive of 1980 held about a sixth of the capacity of an 8-inch drive and had five times its access time. Minicomputer makers showed no interest, so Seagate sold to the desktop personal computer. About half the 8-inch makers never entered that market and later failed. ##A product the mainstream customer could not use found its customers somewhere else.##

Christensen calls such products disruptive. They offer less of what the mainstream buyer wants, in simpler designs built from ordinary parts, along with a different set of attributes that a new market values. The 1.8-inch drive's largest first market was portable heart monitors.

Not everyone accepts the case. The critic Jill Lepore argues that Christensen's cases do not fully support his thesis, and disk drives are one of the examples she examines.

[[lessons]]
$$**Test your best customers' requests against a new use.** A carpenter who keeps building sturdier chairs for the same loyal clients serves them well, and the clients keep praising the work. A rough, light folding stool they would never order could still sell to people outside the shop. Next time you check what your regulars want, ask also where a plainer version of what you do might help someone who is not asking yet. That gives you a fact about a second market, instead of only more of what the first one says.$$

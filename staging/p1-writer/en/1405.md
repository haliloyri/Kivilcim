A pebble lands in a still pond. The water answers with ripples that match the size of the stone, and a moment later the surface is flat again. David Allen uses that picture for the calm he wants from your mind, and then asks what is stopping it from happening.

[[story]]
In *Getting Things Done* (2001), Allen borrows the picture from karate, where it describes the position of perfect readiness: a mind like water. Water does not overreact to a pebble, and it does not underreact to a boulder. It gives back exactly what the input deserves and returns to calm.

Most heads cannot do that, he says, because they are crowded with open loops. An open loop is anything you have agreed to do, decided to do or merely thought of doing that is still unfinished and not yet put anywhere trusted. He also calls them incompletes, or stuff. Each one keeps tugging at attention in the background, and carrying them in short-term memory drains your ability to concentrate on whatever you are actually doing.

##His remedy is to get every one of them out of your head and into a trusted external system.## Your mind can stop guarding the list once it knows the list is safe somewhere else, and that is what the system is for. A line often attributed to him sums it up: your mind is for having ideas, not holding them.

Writing the item down is only half the job. For each open loop Allen asks you to decide the outcome you want and the very next action, the next physical step that moves it forward. A topic such as "mother's birthday" stays on your mind because it has no first move. A physical step, like phoning a florist or asking a sibling to split a gift, gives it one.

The loops do not all go on one list. Allen separates the next actions from the things you are waiting for someone else to do, from larger projects, and from ideas you may take up someday. Each kind sits where you will look for it, so that nothing has to be remembered just because it has no home.

That is why he keeps pushing for specifics. A vague reminder has to be thought through again every time you read it. A written next action closes the loop in your head even before the action is done, because the thought now has a place to live.

[[lessons]]
$$**Write the next physical step, not just the topic.** Someone who lists "plan holiday" will keep seeing that line and feeling slightly guilty, because it does not say what to do first. Rewriting it as "ask my sister which weeks she is free" turns it into something that fits in one phone call. A list made of steps like that is one your mind can trust, so you stop re-reading it at night just in case. You also find out quickly which items were never real tasks, because they refuse to turn into a step.$$

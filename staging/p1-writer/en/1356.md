The door at the base of a six-story red lighthouse creaks open, and out stumbles a nine-foot, nine-hundred-pound Japanese sumo wrestler wearing nothing but a pink wire cable. Julian Mantle heard this story on a mountain in the Himalayas and was disappointed. He had expected something earth-shattering.

[[story]]
The wrestler belongs to the fable at the center of Robin S. Sharma's *The Monk Who Sold His Ferrari* (1997). Julian, a trial lawyer who collapsed in court and sold everything, learned it from Yogi Raman, the eldest of the Sages of Sivana. Back home, he passes it on to his former colleague John over one long night. Each odd element of the tale, Julian explains, is a memory peg for part of the sages' system.

The sumo wrestler stands for kaizen. Yogi Raman told him that the great teachers of the ancient East developed it centuries ago, and that the Japanese word means "constant and never-ending improvement." Julian boils it down to one line: self-mastery is the DNA of life mastery. Success on the outside, in his telling, begins on the inside.

John has his doubts. He describes himself as a middle-class lawyer from the suburbs with a minivan in the driveway, and all this talk about an inner world sounds esoteric to him. Julian skips the argument and tells John to get into push-up position and do as many as he can, and not to stop until he is certain he cannot do any more.

John weighs two hundred and fifteen pounds and is not used to much more exercise than a round of golf. The first fifteen push-ups are agony. It is a hot summer evening, he is sweating heavily, and at twenty-three he gives up and says it is killing him.

"Do ten more," Julian says. ##John counts them out, one, two, five, eight, ten, and lies on the floor in total exhaustion.##

Julian had been through exactly the same exercise with Yogi Raman. His teacher told him that pain was a great teacher and that people grow most when they enter what he called the Zone of the Unknown. The point of the push-ups was simple. John had declared twenty-three his absolute limit, and when challenged he found ten more. In Yogi Raman's words, the only limits on your life are those that you set yourself.

The demonstration works on John. Lying there, he starts listing what he could improve: some exercise, his nasty temper and his habit of interrupting people, his constant worrying. Among the chapter's techniques is one that fits the push-ups well: do the things you fear.

One note on the history. As a business term, kaizen reached the West largely through Masaaki Imai's 1986 book on Japanese industry. Its presentation as an ancient teaching is part of the fable.

[[lessons]]
$$**Test the point where you say "that's my limit" with one small extra step.** The first place you want to stop is often a guess about your capacity rather than a measurement of it, and only a small addition tells you which it is. A person who always ends an evening run at the same corner might one night carry on for one more street and find their legs had more in them than they assumed. That small test gives you an honest reading of where your edge really is, without turning every effort into a punishing contest with yourself.$$

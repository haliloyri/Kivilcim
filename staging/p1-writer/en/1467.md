Bill Gates was talking about malaria at TED when he opened a jar on the stage. Mosquitoes were inside, and he told the room he had brought some. Nobody had been told to expect it.

[[story]]
The conference was in Long Beach, California, in February 2009, and Gates's subject was malaria. At some point he opened the jar and said that malaria is spread by mosquitoes, and that he had brought some along. There was no reason, he added, only poor people should have the experience. The Gates Foundation said afterward that the insects were not carrying the disease, and Carmine Gallo writes that Gates told the audience so only after the stunt had won their attention.

Gallo uses the moment in *Talk Like TED* as his model of a jaw-dropping moment. Every presentation needs one, he says, and he ties it to what neuroscientists call an emotionally charged event. A neuroscientist he cites, A. K. Pradeep, says the brain is wired to notice what is new and stands out.

His timing is the surprising part. The mosquito segment took up less than five percent of Gates's speaking time in an eighteen-minute talk. The rest was still about malaria, and the jar was a short moment inside it. Gallo describes the room going silent first, then laughing and applauding. Reports of the talk add the argument behind the joke: Gates said more research money had gone to hair-loss drugs than to a disease that mostly hits the poor, because rich men are afflicted by baldness.

Gallo does not treat the jar as decoration. For a talk that runs long, he advises a soft break every ten minutes, in the form of a story, a video or a demonstration. Gates's jar is the demonstration. The following year, in a talk on energy, he recalled how the room had reacted and released fireflies, and that audience laughed too.

[[lessons]]
$$**Hand the audience something they can see.** Gates did not describe malaria, he opened a jar, and the subject entered the room along with what was inside it. A driving instructor teaching blind spots could set a cone behind the car and ask the learner to find it in the side mirror. The learner meets the rule itself rather than hearing about it, and that short demonstration stays in memory longer than a long explanation would, because it asks the learner to look for something.$$

One of Europe's top bike makers once refused to sell bicycles to Britain's national cycling team. It worried that rival teams would see British riders on its bikes and stop buying them. The team's turnaround began with seat cushions, massage gels and a lesson in hand washing.

[[story]]
James Clear opens *Atomic Habits* (2018) with this team. In 2003 British Cycling hired Dave Brailsford as its performance director. The record he inherited was thin. British riders had won one Olympic gold medal since 1908, and in 110 years of the Tour de France no British rider had ever won it.

Brailsford's approach was what he called the aggregation of marginal gains. Break down everything that goes into riding a bike and look for a 1 percent improvement in each part. The team redesigned bike seats to make them more comfortable and rubbed alcohol on the tires for better grip. Riders wore electrically heated overshorts to keep their muscles warm, and biofeedback sensors tracked how each one responded to training. Different massage gels were tested for faster recovery.

Some of the gains were simply about staying healthy. ##A surgeon was brought in to teach the riders how to wash their hands properly, so they would catch fewer colds.## The team worked out which pillow and mattress gave each rider the best night's sleep. Even the inside of the team truck was painted white, so that dust, which could affect the finely tuned bikes, would be easy to spot.

Five years later the results came in. At the 2008 Beijing Olympics the team won 8 of the 14 gold medals on offer in road and track cycling. In 2012 Bradley Wiggins became the first British rider to win the Tour de France. Between 2007 and 2017, British cyclists won 178 world championships, 66 Olympic or Paralympic gold medals and 5 Tours.

Clear draws a piece of arithmetic from it. Get 1 percent better every day for a year and you end up roughly 37 times better. Get 1 percent worse every day and you end up near zero. Every action, he writes, is a vote for the type of person you wish to become.

The story has a shadow, and Clear points it out himself. In a later update he noted that questions had been raised about how the team used therapeutic use exemptions for medication, and that the investigations were still unresolved in 2018. The chapter, he stresses, is about how small habits compound over time, and the cycling team is only the example.

[[lessons]]
$$**Look at the small health details you repeat every day.** Someone who wants to get healthier usually starts with a strict diet or an intense gym plan and drops it within a few weeks. Yet the pillow they sleep on, the first thing they drink in the morning and the way they wash their hands when they come home repeat every single day, so a small fix there pays off every single day too. Picking one of those details and improving it a little gives you a gain that repeats on its own, without asking for one big act of willpower.$$

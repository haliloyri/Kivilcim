The secretary walked in on cue and set a tape recorder on the table. Across from it sat a former Kansas City street cop, and beside him two Harvard professors who had just told him they had kidnapped his son. They wanted a million dollars, and they were smiling.

[[story]]
Chris Voss opens *Never Split the Difference* (2016) with that ambush. He had started as a patrol officer, become an FBI hostage negotiator and then the Bureau's lead international kidnapping negotiator. Robert Mnookin, who ran Harvard's negotiation research program, had invited him in to talk. As soon as they sat down, the door opened and Gabriella Blum, a specialist in international negotiation and armed conflict, joined them.

The roles were set at once. Mnookin was the kidnapper. Voss's son was the hostage. Pay a million dollars, Mnookin said, or the boy dies. Then he pushed harder, threatening to kill the son right away.

Voss looked at him for a long moment and smiled. ##"How am I supposed to do that?"##

Mnookin paused. Voss followed up by asking how he could know his son was even alive, and how he could pay anything at all without knowing that. Every threat got the same kind of answer. Within minutes the conversation had stopped being about Voss's fear and had become about the kidnapper's logistics problem.

After about three minutes Blum stepped in and told Mnookin not to let Voss play cat and mouse with him. She came at it harder, with years of Middle East experience behind her, and got the same questions back. Mnookin tried again. His face reddened. Voss finally let him off the hook, and Mnookin conceded that the FBI might have something to teach them.

That moment had a history. For years the FBI had believed in the problem-solving approach of *Getting to Yes*, the classic from Harvard's own negotiation project. Then came the deadly end of the standoff at Randy Weaver's Ruby Ridge farm in Idaho in 1992 and the siege of David Koresh's Branch Davidians at Waco, Texas, in 1993. After those, nobody could pretend most hostage negotiations were rational problem-solving exercises. In October 1993, Deputy Attorney General Philip B. Heymann published a report on the lessons of Waco.

Voss explains the shift with Daniel Kahneman's two systems. System 1 is fast, instinctive and emotional, System 2 slow and logical, and the first steers the second. Negotiating with System 2 tools alone, he writes, is like trying to make an omelette without knowing how to crack an egg. His question at Harvard worked on that first layer. It handed the kidnapper's problem back to the kidnapper.

[[lessons]]
$$**Answer an impossible demand with a "how" question.** When someone asks for something you cannot deliver, asking how you are supposed to do it hands the work of solving the problem back to the person making the demand. A designer whose client wants twice the work for the same budget can ask, calmly, how the client expects that to happen within the money on the table. The question avoids a flat refusal and a reluctant yes at the same time. It also brings the other side's real limits into view, so you keep negotiating instead of fighting.$$

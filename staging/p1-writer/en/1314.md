"My Harry was Elizabeth." A pie-shop owner says it quietly, looking around her empty shop as if someone else were standing in it. For two years one employee had kept the place running. Then, on an ordinary weekday morning, the phone rang.

[[story]]
In *The E-Myth Revisited*, Michael E. Gerber sorts small businesses into three phases. In Infancy, owner and business are the same thing; take the owner away and there's nothing left. Adolescence begins when the owner decides to get help. Gerber's example is a first hire called Harry, a bookkeeper. The owner hands Harry the books and runs. Gerber calls that Management by Abdication rather than delegation.

For a while it feels like freedom. Then the business grows past what Gerber calls the owner's Comfort Zone, and he sees three ways out: go back to Infancy and "get small" again, go for broke, or hang on for dear life.

Sarah, who ran a pie shop, heard this and recognized herself. She had hired Elizabeth when the shop was six months old. Elizabeth did the books. She helped bake. She cleaned up in the morning and after closing. She hired the first three employees and taught them their jobs. Over the next two years, as the business grew, she took on more and more of it.

Then came the call. Sarah believed it was a Wednesday, June 10th, at seven in the morning. Elizabeth said she wouldn't be coming in any longer. She had taken another job; she couldn't afford to work for what Sarah was paying her. Sarah laughed and said something like "C'mon, Elizabeth." Elizabeth said she was sorry and hung up.

Sarah stood there and wept, and felt a cold fear she had never felt before. But the pies still had to come out of the oven and the floors still needed cleaning, so she went to work, and she hadn't stopped since. The people Elizabeth had hired left soon afterward. Sarah had never really connected with them; they were Elizabeth's people. She hadn't hired anyone since.

##"I got small again," she told Gerber. "So, there's my Comfort Zone."##

Gerber's diagnosis is blunt. Sarah had trusted Elizabeth blindly. Trusting that way meant she never had to do the work she didn't want to do: agreeing on what the relationship was, what role each of them played, what rules she expected Elizabeth to follow. True trust, he writes, comes from knowing. His answer to her was short. "Start all over again, but differently this time."

[[lessons]]
$$**When you hand work over, define the role together first.** Passing a job to someone and stepping away feels like relief, but if that person leaves, you are left with neither the knowledge nor the routine. A parent giving the weekly grocery run to a teenager for the first time can sit down with them and write the list and the budget together, instead of telling them to just handle it. Then both of them know how the job is done, either one can pick it up if the other is away, and the trust between them rests on something they both have seen.$$

Every piece of clothing in the house goes onto the floor in a single heap: the coats from the hall, the shirts from the bedroom, the jumper that has been living on a chair. Only when the heap is complete does anyone decide what stays. Then the same thing happens with the books.

[[story]]
That heap is how Marie Kondo begins in *The Life-Changing Magic of Tidying Up*. She defines the problem in one plain line: clutter is caused by a failure to return things to where they belong. Tidying, she says, has two parts, discarding and deciding where to store things. Discarding comes first. Until you know what is staying, thinking about shelves and boxes is wasted effort.

##"Tidying up by location is a fatal mistake."## Her reason is practical. Things of the same kind are scattered all over a home. Someone who tidies one room sees only part of a category, and a few days later repeats the same sorting somewhere else, never seeing the whole.

So she gathers every item of one category in one place before making a single decision. The pile shows, at a glance, how much of one thing a household owns. Each piece is then picked up from the heap and decided on, one at a time, before the next category is even touched.

The categories also come in a fixed order. Clothes first, then books, then papers, then komono, her word for the miscellany of small things, and lastly mementos. The order runs from easier decisions to harder ones. Deciding about a T-shirt is quick, and so is deciding about most books once you have held them. A photograph can hold someone for minutes, which is why sentimental items wait until the very end.

Kondo has no patience for rules built on numbers. She quotes targets like "seven jackets and ten blouses is the perfect amount" and argues that numerical goals are one reason other methods result in rebound. In her method each item is judged by hand, by whether it brings joy, and a count has nothing to do with it.

Storage comes only after the sorting, and it has two rules of its own: store all items of the same type in the same place, and don't scatter storage space. When all the clothes live in one spot, there is only one place to put them back. That is how her opening definition closes. Things can return to where they belong, because each kind of thing now belongs in one place.

[[lessons]]
$$**Tidy one category at a time.** Seeing everything of one kind in one place shows you how much you really own and what is surplus, which no single drawer can show. Someone who works from home can collect every charger and cable from the drawers, bags and the floor under the desk into one box before sorting any of them. With them side by side, she notices how many cables she has collected that do the same job, and she can decide once which ones she actually uses. She also saves herself from repeating the same small decision in every drawer of the house.$$

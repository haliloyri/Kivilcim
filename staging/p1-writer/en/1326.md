About two hours a week, alone, in a junk store or in front of an aquarium tank. Nothing to make afterward, nothing to hand in. It's one of the stranger prescriptions given to people who say they can't create anymore, and most of them hear it as wasted time.

[[story]]
Julia Cameron explains creativity in *The Artist's Way* with a well. "Art is an image-using system," she writes. Each time we make something, we reach into an inner well and draw images out of it. Ideally, she says, that well is like a well-stocked trout pond, with big fish, little fish, fat fish, skinny fish, an abundance of artistic fish to fry.

The trouble is that we keep drawing from it. If we don't give some attention to upkeep, Cameron warns, the well is apt to become depleted, stagnant or blocked. Someone who pulls from it every week and puts nothing back can one day lower the bucket and bring it up empty.

The way to refill it is with images. Filling the well, she writes, involves the active pursuit of images to refresh our artistic reservoirs. Then comes her caution about how to do it: think magic, think delight, think fun, and do not think duty. Do what intrigues you, explore what interests you. ##"Think mystery, not mastery."## Her example is small: if I drive this road, not my usual road, what will I see?

Her tool for this is the artist date. She defines it precisely: a block of time, perhaps two hours weekly, especially set aside and committed to nurturing your creative consciousness, your inner artist. You go alone. No friend, no partner, no project along for the ride.

Her suggestions are modest. A great junk store. A solo trip to the beach. An old movie. An aquarium or an art gallery. None of them requires you to produce anything at all, or to show it to anyone. Each one is a way of getting new images in through your eyes and ears.

The book treats these two hours as maintenance, not indulgence. Morning pages pour the noise out each day; the artist date puts something back. One empties, the other fills.

An hour wandering a junk store doesn't look productive. In Cameron's accounting, though, that is exactly how next week's images get into the pond in the first place.

[[lessons]]
$$**If you produce every week, schedule time that only takes in.** Before the ideas run dry, time chosen for delight rather than duty, with nothing expected at the end, puts back what your work keeps drawing out. A designer who feels she has run out of ideas could walk home a different way one evening and wander into a hardware store with no errand at all. The shop windows, the street noise and the odd tools on the shelves give her fresh material, and she sits down to the next job with something to draw on.$$

A seed sits in the soil and does nothing until it is watered. Thich Nhat Hanh looks for the main cause of anger inside you, in a seed of exactly that kind, rather than in the person across the table. The hands that water it are not only other people's.

[[story]]
In *Anger* (2001), Thich Nhat Hanh finds the main cause of our suffering in the seed of anger. You discover, he writes, that this seed has been watered too often, by yourself and by other people. The finding relocates the problem: it is no longer only out there, in the person who set you off.

The phrase "too often" speaks of repetition more than of quantity. An anger seed does not grow in a single sitting. It grows from water that lands in the same place again and again, not from one stormy evening alone.

The image works like this. A seed waits in the ground and does not grow without water. A remark, a look or an action can bring that water, but where there is no seed, water grows nothing. That does not mean the other person did nothing wrong. It only means their behaviour is not enough to explain how large your own anger became.

We are among the ones holding the watering can. He says so directly: the seed is watered by others, and it is also watered by us. Often the nearest hand adding to the anger is our own.

The discovery brings a choice with it. Thich Nhat Hanh advises practising selective watering to protect yourself and the people you love. You are not obliged to water every seed. You decide which ones get the water.

When anger rises, the practice he gives is plain: begin mindful breathing right away. He also suggests trying not to say or do anything while you are angry. Every harsh word carries one more bucket of water to the seed, to yours or to the other person's.

So the direction of the gaze shifts. The eye that was looking outward for someone to blame turns inward to see where the seed lies and how it was watered. Anger becomes less a verdict about someone else and more a matter of what you cultivate.

[[lessons]]
$$**Look for the seed under your anger.** Someone who is furious at a driver who cut in front of them might notice that they were already tired that day and that the anger was also fed by something else. They stop loading all of it onto the driver, see their own share and can choose what not to water the next time they feel it rising. That does not put the anger out, but it takes it from the other person's hands and puts it in your own, where you can decide what to do with it.$$

Two people in the middle of an argument can each be doing exactly what protects them from getting hurt, and together they hurt each other anyway. Sue Johnson found three versions of this and gave each a name you can remember.

[[story]]
In *Hold Me Tight* (2008), Sue Johnson opens with a map. The first of her seven conversations is about recognising the "demon dialogues," three patterns that keep couples stuck. Her therapy starts by tracking them. In her popular writing she calls the pattern a "dance," and the first stage of the method is to learn its steps.

The first dialogue is called Find the Bad Guy. Partners go in a circle blaming each other, and each defends themselves. Neither takes responsibility first, so the circle never opens. Johnson reads the blaming as self-protection, and because partners come to expect it, they react even faster each time they sense it starting.

The second is the Protest Polka. One partner reaches out, looking for connection and protesting the distance. The other pulls back and pushes against what sounds like criticism. The image that goes with it is one person knocking on a brick wall, hoping the other will open it. From outside, each partner may look as if they are freezing or poking. The way out she suggests is for both to learn to recognise each other's desperation.

The third is Freeze and Flee, and it tends to follow the polka. The partner who was reaching out has given up, and now both step back. What remains is icy silence, tension and pain, with no connection at all, good or bad. Both are protecting themselves from rejection and contempt. Johnson's advice here is to listen to your own anxiety and fight for recognition from your partner, since the connection may be forgotten rather than gone.

Johnson's suggested response to all three is to treat the pattern as the shared enemy and to stay in the present moment instead of replaying the past. Because the problem sits in the loop between two people, neither of them has to be the villain for the pattern to change.

In her nine-step model, the more withdrawn partner is usually invited to work through the steps slightly ahead of the other. The first stage identifies the conflict issues and the negative cycle, brings attachment emotions to the surface, and reframes the problem as the cycle itself.

[[lessons]]
$$**Name the loop out loud and treat it as the opponent.** Two colleagues keep ending the same meeting in silence, one pushing for a decision and the other going quiet. Instead of asking who is to blame, one of them says, calmly and briefly, that they are in their usual loop again. Seeing the loop together changes the conversation from you against me to the two of you against the pattern, and then either of you can try a different first move. That move can be small, such as lowering your voice or leaving one sentence unfinished.$$

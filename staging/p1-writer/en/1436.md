Getting an appointment usually means cold calling. One sales book describes it as the most stressful and least-liked part of selling, and it names what lies underneath that dread: the chance that the person on the line will say no, or be rude about it.

[[story]]
According to a summary of Brian Tracy's *The Psychology of Selling* (2006), the fear that holds most salespeople back is the fear of rejection. It is set off by the chance of disapproval, judgment or plain rudeness from the person on the other end of the line.

The book opens by counting. The top 20 percent of salespeople earn about 80 percent of the profession's money, and Tracy puts the gap down to a small, steady edge in skill, like a horse that wins by a nose and collects ten times the prize of second place.

The fear bites hardest at the start. Cold calling is how an appointment usually gets made, and the book treats prospecting as a skill that can be learned. Tracy also advises preparing answers to the common objections in advance, so that a "no" does not arrive as a surprise.

The book also turns the dread into arithmetic. It starts from an annual income goal and works down to the daily activities that fill a schedule: calls, presentations and follow-ups, each slotted into the day with its own subgoals. A single call then counts as one line in the day's plan, with nothing riding on it beyond that.

His remedy comes in three parts. Rejection is not personal, he says, and it is an expected, normal part of sales. Courage takes practice and strengthens with use, like a muscle. And positive self-talk helps: when you feel like avoiding the next call, you repeat an upbeat line, and the summary's example is "I'm doing great! I love my work!"

Behind this sits what Tracy calls self-concept, how you see yourself and your skills. It shapes attitude, and a positive attitude helps in three ways, the summary reports: customers respond more warmly, you enjoy and practice sales tasks more, and your earnings comfort zone rises. People tend to earn within about 10 percent of what they believe they are worth, so a self-described $50,000 person earns roughly that. That figure is the book's own claim, and the summary names no study behind it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Treat an expected "no" as practice.** When a task depends on asking, rejection by a stranger is a normal part of it, and the dread usually does not fade until you have done the thing a few times. A freelancer who has put off phoning prospective clients can make the first call even if it goes badly, and then notice that the second one costs a little less nerve. Courage here works like a muscle, so the useful question is how soon you can have a small turn at it, and what to say if the answer is no.$$

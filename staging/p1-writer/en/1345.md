The jeans Barry Schwartz finally bought fit him fine. That was never the problem. The problem was the hour in the dressing room with one pair of every cut over his arm, trying to work out which fit was right for a man who had never cared about fit in his life.

[[story]]
Schwartz opens *The Paradox of Choice* (2004) with this trip to the Gap. He wears jeans until they fall apart, so it had been a while since he'd bought any. He told the young salesperson what he wanted: a pair of jeans, 32-28.

She answered with questions. Did he want slim fit, easy fit, relaxed fit, baggy or extra baggy? Stonewashed, acid-washed or distressed? Button-fly or zipper-fly? Faded or regular? He was stunned, and after a moment managed to say he just wanted regular jeans, the kind that used to be the only kind.

She didn't know what those were. She had to check with an older colleague, who worked out what "regular" used to mean and pointed him to the right shelf. By then the damage was done. With all these options on display, Schwartz was no longer sure regular was what he wanted.

He went back and asked how regular, relaxed and easy fit differed. She showed him a diagram of the cuts. It didn't help. So he took one pair of each into the dressing room, tried them all, studied himself in the mirror and came out to ask for more clarification. Very little was riding on this, yet he had become convinced that one of these options was right for him and he had to find it.

He settled on easy fit, for a reason he admits with some embarrassment: "relaxed fit" suggested he was getting soft in the middle.

##The jeans turned out just fine.## What stayed with him was the cost of getting there. Buying jeans used to take five minutes and you lived with an imperfect fit. Now it was a project that ate a day and came with self-doubt, anxiety and dread.

There was a quieter change too. Before that afternoon he did not care about the subtleties of fit. Once he learned there were several kinds, he suddenly cared, and he describes himself as nudged toward becoming a "denim maximizer." His standards for jeans had shifted for good.

From this small scene Schwartz builds the book's argument. Having no choice makes life almost unbearable, and the first gains in choice bring real freedom. Past a point, though, every extra option adds weight, until choice stops freeing people and starts to debilitate them. He goes as far as calling it a tyranny.

[[lessons]]
$$**Decide which difference matters before you see the options.** A wider range can make you care about a distinction you never noticed, and that new concern costs you time and confidence even when the result is fine. Someone who goes out to buy a kettle can come home three hours later having learned about wattage, spout shapes and limescale filters, and still feel unsure. If you write down the one or two features you actually need before you start, the rest of the shelf becomes easy to ignore, and the decision ends when something meets that short list.$$

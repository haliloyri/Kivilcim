[[open]]
Ask people what the word "sales" brings to mind and the first answer on the list is "pushy". Ask the same people what they do all day, and about 24 minutes of every hour turn out to be spent moving someone else to say yes.

[[story]]
The word-association test is the part of Daniel Pink's *To Sell Is Human* that stings. People were asked what they think of when they hear "sales", and 20 of the top 25 words were negative. "Pushy" came first, with "yuck" and "ugh" further down the list.

##Yet people spend roughly 40 percent of their working time on what Pink calls non-sales selling.## That was the finding of a survey commissioned for the book: about 24 minutes of every hour spent persuading, convincing or influencing someone without any purchase changing hands. A teacher wins a student's attention. A nurse or doctor talks a patient into following the treatment. Education and health care together make up the largest job sector in the American economy, and a good share of that work is persuasion.

Formal sales jobs are only the visible part. About one in nine American workers is in sales, more than 15 million people, and the rest are doing it from inside other jobs. eBay, Etsy and Apple's app store have also helped small businesses and one-person startups multiply, which means more people now sell their own work with no sales team behind them. Pink writes in his introduction that selling has changed more in the last ten years than in the previous hundred, and he puts two scenes side by side: pushing Buicks on a lot and pitching an idea in a meeting.

The book then overturns a stereotype about who is good at it. In a call-centre study by the researcher Adam Grant, extraverts earned $125 an hour in sales revenue and introverts $120. People in the middle of the scale, the ambiverts, earned $155. The best performers brought in $208 an hour and scored about 4 on the seven-point scale.

So most of us already sell for a big slice of the day, and we dislike the word for it. Pink's title is the way out: to sell is human.

[[lessons]]
$$**Treat your everyday requests as selling and prepare for them.** A teacher who wants parents to sign a field-trip form is trying to move people, even if the sentence she uses is "just a reminder". Thinking beforehand about who needs to agree, what each of them will ask, and what you can offer in return makes the conversation easier to start. You can pick one request on your list this week and plan it the way you would plan any other task, which spares you from relying on luck.$$

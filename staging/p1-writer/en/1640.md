Shame, in Brené Brown's research, is linked to addiction, aggression, depression and violence. Guilt, which sounds like its mild cousin, is the feeling she says can set a person on a better path, one toward repair and the other toward hiding.

[[story]]
In *The Gifts of Imperfection* (2010), Brené Brown separates guilt from shame. Shame, as she defines it, is the intensely painful feeling or experience of believing that we are flawed and therefore unworthy of love and belonging. Its sentence is "I am bad." The sentence of guilt is "I did something bad."

Guilt holds a behavior up against our own values. The mismatch is uncomfortable, but that discomfort has somewhere to go. According to Brown, guilt can set a positive change in motion: you apologize, you repair what you did, you act differently next time.

Shame looks at the same event from a different place. It passes judgment on the person, not on the act. In Brown's research, shame is associated with addiction, aggression, depression and violence. When there is no way to repair anything, what remains is to hide, or to strike back. That is the cost of the wrong sentence: it leaves the person nothing to fix and only a place to retreat to.

Shame also has a place where it feeds. According to Brown, it grows in secrecy and silence, and it hates having words wrapped around it. Someone who starts to speak shame aloud has begun to weaken it. Guilt has no such urge to hide: saying "I did it" opens the door to talking openly.

She shows the difference through an apology. "I'm sorry, I made a mistake" is tied to what was done and can be fixed. "I'm sorry, I am a mistake" condemns the person and leaves nothing to fix.

The distinction looks easy and gets blurred in daily life, because both begin with the same sensations: a tight stomach, a flushed face. This is why Brown describes four steps of shame resilience, and knowing the difference is the ground that work stands on.

[[lessons]]
$$**Attach the mistake to what you did, not to who you are.** Someone who quotes a wrong figure in a meeting and says "I gave that number without checking it" has a concrete thing to fix, while "how incompetent I am" gives him nothing to do. The first sentence shrinks him and makes him ashamed of himself, and the second hands him something he can do tomorrow. Turning the subject of the sentence from yourself to your behavior keeps both responsibility and the ability to change.$$

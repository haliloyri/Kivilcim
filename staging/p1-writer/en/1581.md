A decade after art school, about half of one class had left the art world. The half still working had behaved oddly in the first hour of a drawing exercise: they picked up more objects and took longer to start.

[[story]]
Daniel Pink uses a 1960s study by Jacob Getzels and Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi to explain what he means by clarity in *To Sell Is Human*. About three dozen fourth-year art students were given a table of 27 objects. Their task was to arrange a still life and draw it.

The difference showed up in the first minutes. Some solved the task quickly. Others handled more objects and took longer over it. Csikszentmihalyi later put the two starting questions this way: one group asked how to produce a good drawing, the other asked what good drawing they could produce.

Ten years later, about half the students had left the art world. The half who had become professional artists were almost entirely the ones Pink calls problem finders, the students who had spent longer searching for the problem before solving it.

##Another ten years on, the problem finders were significantly more successful, by the standards of the artistic community, than their peers.##

It was a small group, studied a long time ago, and the book offers a link, not proof that finding the problem alone produces success. But the link fits what Pink wants salespeople to do. Solving the problem a buyer names is a job. Showing a buyer a problem they had not seen is a different and harder one. The art students who took their time over the objects were practising exactly that.

Pink defines clarity as helping people see their situation in a new way and notice problems they had not spotted. That includes the buyer who has misjudged the problem or does not see it at all. The students' habit of staying with the objects until a worthwhile problem showed itself is the same skill, practised on a table instead of a customer.

[[lessons]]
$$**Spend a few minutes finding the problem before you start fixing it.** A manager told to cut the lateness of a monthly report could first check whether the delay happens in the draft, the review or the sign-off. Those minutes can feel wasted, since nothing is solved yet, but fixing the wrong stage quickly costs more than finding the right one a little later. On your next task, write down two or three versions of the problem before choosing one.$$

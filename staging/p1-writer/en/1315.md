[[open]]
"I don't have a problem or anything." A Stanford student in her mid-twenties says it while scrolling through her latest snapshots, each filtered to look like it was shot in the late 1970s. She posts dozens a day. Nobody has to remind her to open the app anymore, and that is exactly what interests the man listening.

[[story]]
Nir Eyal calls her Yin in *Hooked: How to Build Habit-Forming Products*, and notes that it isn't her real name. She lives in Palo Alto, attends Stanford, and has all the polish you'd expect. She is also, in Eyal's words, compulsively hooked on Instagram. Yin doesn't see it that way. "It's just fun," she says. "I just use it whenever I see something cool. I feel I need to grab it before it's gone."

Eyal wants to know how a simple photo app got that far into her day. When he wrote the book, Instagram had 150 million users, and Facebook had bought it in 2012 for $1 billion. He's careful about the price: a rumored bidding war and many other factors pushed it up. But he sees, underneath, a team that understood psychology as well as technology.

Yin's habit began outside her. A friend recommended the app, and then came weeks of repeated use. Every photo she took went out to her friends on Facebook and Twitter, and those photos worked as invitations for other people to try it. Others heard about it from the press and bloggers, or saw it featured in Apple's App Store. Once installed, the icon on the phone screen and the notifications about friends' posts kept calling people back.

Then the cue moved inside. ##It is the fear of losing a special moment, Eyal writes, that brings a small pang of stress, and opening Instagram to take the picture makes it go away.## Yin no longer needs a ping. The feeling arrives on its own, and her thumb follows. As she keeps using the app, new feelings attach: it eases boredom, and it soothes the fear of missing out.

Eyal compares habits to pearls. A bit of grit gets inside an oyster, and the oyster coats it in layer after layer of nacre until there's a pearl. Triggers are the grit. External triggers start a new habit, he argues, but what keeps people hooked is the link to an internal one, and that link takes weeks or months to form.

The trigger is the first step of his Hook Model, followed by action, variable reward and investment. In Yin's case the whole chain starts with something small: a moment she's afraid she'll lose.

[[lessons]]
$$**Name the feeling that comes just before the habit.** A habit has settled in once a feeling, rather than a reminder, is what starts the action, and that makes it easy to miss. Someone who notices they open the fridge every time an irritating work email lands, even when they aren't hungry, has found the real trigger, and it isn't food. Putting that feeling into a single word in the moment lets you see where your hand is heading, and it gives you a brief chance to choose a different response the next time the same feeling shows up.$$

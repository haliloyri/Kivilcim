A troupe of street performers from Quebec set up a tent for a circus without animal acts and without star performers. The industry they were entering had been shrinking for years. The ticket they priced was nowhere near a circus ticket.

[[story]]
In 1984, a group of street performers in Quebec started Cirque du Soleil. In *Blue Ocean Strategy*, W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne describe the circus business of that time as one in long decline. Audiences were shrinking, animal-rights groups were criticising the animal acts, and families had plenty of other ways to spend an evening.

Ringling Bros. was the leader the rest of the circus world measured itself against. The obvious move for a newcomer was to do the same show, only a bit better: a larger cast, a bolder act, a lower ticket price. Cirque du Soleil did not take it.

Instead, it removed things. There were no animal shows. There were no star performers whose fees pushed up the cost of every night. There were no vendors selling concessions along the aisles, and no multiple rings running at once for the audience to choose between.

##Cirque asked what a circus could borrow from the theatre.##

It added a story and a theme to the evening. It added artistic music and dance, and a more refined setting for the audience. The result had the physical thrill of acrobats and the feel of a night at the theatre, and it did not fit neatly into either category.

Its customers changed along with the show. Cirque aimed at adults and corporate clients, not at families with children in tow, and it priced tickets several times higher than a traditional circus, closer to what a theatre seat costs.

Kim and Mauborgne call this a blue ocean: a space with no direct rivals, instead of a red ocean where competitors cut into each other's share. The book's claim is that in less than twenty years, Cirque reached a level of revenue that Ringling Bros. had needed more than a century to build.

[[lessons]]
$$**Borrow your edge from another field.** A small neighbourhood gym could keep trying to out-equip the other gyms nearby, or it could take the quiet, unhurried feel of a spa and offer that to people who dislike crowded weight rooms. Comparing yourself with a different industry lets you see which costly features your customers hardly miss and which pleasant ones nobody around you offers yet. You can then try dropping one feature and adding one borrowed one on a small scale, for a month, and watch which customers respond.$$

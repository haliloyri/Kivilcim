Think about the last headline that made your stomach drop. A plane crash makes the news every time it happens, while all the other planes that land safely never do. What gets reported tells you about fear, and it is a poor measure of risk.

[[story]]
Hans Rosling lists fear among the ten instincts that bend our view of the world in *Factfulness*, and he starts from a distinction. Frightening and dangerous, he says, are two different things. Most of us treat them as one.

The fear instinct, in his account, makes us give attention to the unlikely dangers we are most afraid of, and neglect what is actually most risky. He names three kinds of things that frighten people most: physical harm, captivity and contamination. Those are the stories that pull at us. The trouble starts when the instinct picks the target for us and the odds never get a say.

A plane crash is the case Rosling uses. It sits near the top of what people dread, and the media report a crash every time. The planes that land safely never get a mention, so the news shows only the crash. What reaches the viewer is a picture of danger with nothing to set beside it, and the mind fills in the rest with dread.

His answer is a short equation: risk equals danger multiplied by exposure. Danger is how bad the harm would be, anywhere from mild embarrassment to the loss of a life. Exposure is how likely you are to meet it. A plane crash is as dangerous as danger gets. The share of flights that end in one is tiny.

##Frightening is how it feels. Risk is danger times exposure.##

So when fear arrives, Rosling's advice is to calm down first and then calculate the risk, so that the thing you worry about is weighed by how often it happens as well as by how bad it would be, before the feeling decides.

[[lessons]]
$$**Multiply before you react.** Fear tells you how bad something would be, and it says nothing about how likely it is, yet a decision needs both together. A parent who hears about a rare, frightening accident at a swimming pool may want to keep the children away from water entirely. If she asks how bad and how likely separately, she may notice a risk that is milder but far more common, such as skipping sun protection on a hot day, and deal with that one first.$$

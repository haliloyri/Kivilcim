Three people at a table are each explaining why the delay was somebody else's fault. One sentence from a fourth person can turn the table toward the problem. A book on emotional intelligence says which sentence, and adds two smaller moves for the moments before it.

[[story]]
Relationship management is the fourth skill in Travis Bradberry and Jean Greaves's *Emotional Intelligence 2.0*, and the one that draws on the other three. It means using your awareness of your own emotions and other people's to handle interactions well. The book turns that into small moves rather than a definition, and each one starts with something you can do before the other person does anything.

One move is for a conversation that has slid into blame or complaining. You acknowledge that there is a problem and offer one or two ideas for solving it. The authors call it a fix-it statement. It shifts the talk from winning the argument to solving the problem, and you do it simply by speaking first, before the grievances have a chance to pile up another layer.

Another move is for the moment someone shows strong emotion. Acknowledge it, ask how you can help, ask open questions, meaning ones that a plain yes or no cannot answer, listen closely and then summarize what you heard. The book says simple acts like these recognize an emotion without making a big deal of it, brushing it aside or dismissing it.

##The third move is about receiving feedback.## Do not get defensive, since the person giving it cares about your growth. Listen to understand, sort out which points are valid, make a plan, and share the plan so the other person can see you heard.

None of the three needs the other person to change first. Listening, acknowledging and bringing an idea are things you can do alone, and they work as well in a meeting room as at a kitchen table. They are three of the book's 66 strategies, and the fix-it statement is the shortest to say aloud.

[[lessons]]
$$**Bring an idea when blame starts.** When a discussion locks onto whose fault it is, naming the problem and offering one or two ideas changes what everyone is talking about. If a teammate complains at every meeting about the same late handover, saying "we have this problem again, and here are two things we could try" turns the complaint into a small planning session. The others usually follow with ideas of their own, and the meeting stops circling the same grievance and starts moving.$$

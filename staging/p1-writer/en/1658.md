[[open]]
Honor your hunger, feel your fullness: two of Evelyn Tribole and Elyse Resch's ten principles concern signals that a diet plan tends to override. The authors treat those signals as information worth trusting again, and they say how early to start listening.

[[story]]
In *Intuitive Eating* (1995), registered dietitians Evelyn Tribole and Elyse Resch put hunger first. Meet your body's energy needs, they write on their site, so that extreme hunger does not take over. Respond to what they call the first biological signal of hunger, and you rebuild trust with food.

The second signal is the end of hunger. The authors ask you to notice when hunger fades and you become comfortably full, and to pause during a meal to check how the food tastes and how full you feel. The pause is a halfway check, not a rule about when to stop.

A third principle puts pleasure into the meal on purpose. Enjoyment is a valid goal of eating, the authors say, and eating foods you truly want in a pleasant setting helps you feel content with smaller amounts.

Two more principles keep these signals company. One says to choose foods that support your well-being, enjoyment and culture while staying attuned to how your body responds, and the page insists that "progress, not perfection, is what counts." Another warns that restriction can itself trigger loss of control, which is why meeting your energy needs comes first.

What ties these together is attention to the body. The Intuitive Eating site calls it interoceptive awareness and insists that the approach is not a diet or a food plan, with no pass-or-fail rules. There is no calorie number or clock time to follow, only signals to read.

Critics point to the cost of that freedom. Without a set plan it can be hard to know what and how much to eat, and learning to read your own cues accurately can take a steep learning curve. The evidence is mostly observational: studies link the approach to less overeating and better self-regulation, but they cannot show that it causes either.

[[lessons]]
$$**Check in with hunger before and during a meal.** Someone who eats lunch at their desk while answering email, finishes the plate and could not say what it tasted like has missed both signals the authors care about. Stopping once in the middle to taste the food and notice how full you are gives you information the plate alone cannot. You can try that pause at one meal this week, and treat whatever you notice as data instead of a verdict on your self-control. If nothing registers the first time, that is useful too, because it tells you how little attention the meal was getting.$$

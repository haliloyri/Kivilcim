A plane on its way to a destination spends most of the flight slightly off course. Wind and turbulence keep nudging it sideways, and the pilot keeps nudging it back. Jack Canfield thinks most of us fly the same way, except that our instruments only speak when someone asks them to.

[[story]]
Principle 19 in Jack Canfield's *The Success Principles* is called "Use Feedback to Your Advantage." He builds it on that picture of a flight. The plane is rarely exactly on its line between takeoff and landing, yet it arrives, because the pilot reads the instruments and corrects, then corrects again, all the way down.

For Canfield, working toward any goal follows the same pattern. Drifting is normal. What gets you there is a signal that tells you early that you have drifted, and in a working life or a marriage that signal usually comes from other people.

The trouble is that people tend to keep it to themselves. In Canfield's account, the colleagues, clients and family members who notice you drifting often stay quiet because they are afraid of how you will react. So the instruments are there, but they are switched off.

His answer is to ask, and to ask in a way that makes a vague answer hard. The question he recommends is: "On a scale of 1 to 10, how would you rate the quality of our relationship?" Swap in "our service" or "this product" and it works for customers too.

##Canfield says he asks his wife and his coworkers a version of this question every week.## The author of a book of success principles runs one of them at his own kitchen table.

A number does something "Is everything okay?" can't. The polite answer to the second is yes, and the conversation ends. A seven out of ten leaves a gap of three points that nobody has explained yet, and people tend to fill it. Canfield notes that answers below ten usually come with specific suggestions for what would make things better.

He offers a second, blunter question for people who know you well: "In what ways do you see me holding myself back?" The answer can sting. His advice is to take it gracefully, since fear of your reaction is the reason people stayed silent in the first place.

The pilot doesn't argue with the altimeter or wait for it to apologise. He reads the number and turns the controls. That is the whole habit Canfield is describing: ask for the reading, listen to the reason behind it, and start the next week a little closer to the line.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask for a number instead of waiting for feedback.** People rarely volunteer criticism because they don't want to hurt you or deal with your reaction, but a small, specific question gives them permission. A baker who asks a regular customer to rate this morning's bread out of ten may finally hear that it has been a little too salty for weeks, and adjust the next batch. Asking this way lets you catch the drift while it is still a small correction, before it turns into a lost customer, a stalled project or a cold evening at home.$$

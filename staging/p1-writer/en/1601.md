Twenty-three healthy adults were tested in Florence and Pavia while reciting the same Latin prayer over and over. The researchers were not measuring faith. They were watching what the words did to breathing, and the answer was strangely tidy.

[[story]]
James Nestor's *Breath* (2020) names the pace he thinks most people should aim for when they want to settle: 5.5 seconds in and 5.5 seconds out, about 5.5 breaths a minute. A 2001 study in the *BMJ* by Bernardi and colleagues found a very similar pace in an unexpected place, and that study is the more concrete story.

The volunteers were 23 healthy adults tested in Florence and Pavia. At rest, left to breathe naturally, they averaged about 14 breaths a minute. In one part of the experiment they recited the Ave Maria in Latin, the prayer that the rosary repeats over and over. In another they chanted a yoga mantra. Neither was a breathing exercise. Both are simply long phrases said aloud in a steady rhythm.

##Both slowed breathing to about six breaths a minute.## The researchers also saw more variability in the rhythms of the cardiovascular system, and a measure called baroreflex sensitivity, which tracks how the body adjusts blood pressure, rose from 9.5 to 11.5 ms/mm Hg.

The design also compared the recitations with breathing paced to a metronome, and with plain talking, which slowed breathing too. The prayer and the mantra landed near six breaths a minute without anyone counting. That is less than half the resting rate.

The study's authors went a step further and suggested that the rosary may have partly evolved because its rhythm matches those cardiovascular rhythms. That is their interpretation, not something the experiment tested. The experiment itself was small, 23 people, and what it showed is simpler: a long, rhythmic phrase can pull breathing down to a pace of about six a minute without anyone being told to breathe slowly.

[[lessons]]
$$**Use a count you can repeat to slow your breath.** Before a hard phone call, someone can breathe in for five counts and out for five, the way the volunteers' phrases carried them. The count removes the need to decide how slowly to breathe, because the rhythm sets the pace. After a minute you can notice whether your hands and voice feel steadier, and keep the same count ready for the next call, so that the slow pace already feels familiar when the pressure comes.$$

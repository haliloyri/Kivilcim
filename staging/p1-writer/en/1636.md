The doctor works in a hotel, but the picture he reaches for comes from a Buddhist wheel with six realms. One of them holds creatures who can never be filled. He says he knows that realm from the people he treats, and his book begins on a Vancouver sidewalk.

[[story]]
On the sidewalks of Vancouver's Downtown Eastside, Eva, in her thirties, does a dance the locals call the Hastings shuffle, right there between the passers-by. Cocaine moves her feet. Arlene has rows of razor scars across her arms, and a medication that holds her urge to cut in check, and she is afraid the dose will be lowered.

Gabor Maté meets them as the physician for the Portland Hotel Society, a nonprofit that runs the Portland, the Stanley, the Washington, the Regal and the Sunrise hotels. Most residents are addicts. Many also live with a mental illness. His book *In the Realm of Hungry Ghosts* grows out of that clinic.

To describe his patients he borrows the Buddhist Wheel of Life. The Hungry Ghost Realm, one of its six, is the realm of addiction: its inhabitants keep reaching outside themselves for relief from a craving that never ends, and what they reach for is never what they need. They stay hungry.

Another realm on the wheel is the Hell Realm of overwhelming fear, rage and despair. Maté reads drug use as an attempt to get away from it. On that reading the sidewalk dance and the scars on a forearm both point at something the person is trying to leave.

He insists the ghosts are not a separate tribe. Addiction, in his account, runs along a continuum that takes in substances, compulsive eating, gambling and even a workaholism that society applauds. People on the street sit at the far end of it, and most of the line runs through ordinary, hidden lives. He puts himself on it too. His own compulsion is buying classical CDs, often several recordings of the same piece.

He does not look for the roots mainly in genes. They lie in early childhood, he says, when the brain's reward pathways form and emotional patterns settle out of sight, and later stress adds to the pull.

In his view the habit has a job to do in a person's life. So his question shifts from "why the addiction?" to "why the pain?", because he sees a habit as an answer to a painful history. It is a physician's reading, drawn from his patients, and some commentators question how far his claims reach.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask what a habit is quieting.** When a habit refuses to quit, the useful question is often what it covers, because fighting the habit alone leaves its cause untouched. A shop owner who snacks every night once the last customer has gone could write down for a week how that hour feels, and he might find tiredness or loneliness under the snacking. With that named, he can try a different kind of evening instead of scolding himself, and a few weeks later he can check whether that hour feels any different. The habit then becomes information about him rather than a verdict.$$

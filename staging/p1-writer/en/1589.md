[[open]]
A warm room, three meals a day and a lift to the second floor: for Sinclair, that comfort is exactly what keeps a defence system in every cell asleep, one built for famine and cold winters.

[[story]]
David Sinclair calls it the survival circuit. In *Lifespan* (2019) he describes a set of longevity genes inherited from the earliest life forms: the sirtuins, mTOR and AMPK. Evolution built them to carry organisms through famine and hard winters.

In a comfortable modern life, he says, they sit mostly dormant. Nobody goes hungry for a week, and the cold rarely gets past a coat. The stakes in his argument are large: Sinclair believes people could live beyond 120, even 150, without the usual frailty of old age, against a typical lifespan of around 80 today.

What wakes the circuit is mild stress. Going hungry now and then, getting cold, working out hard enough that it hurts: each switches the circuit on. Exercise also shows up in his account as linked to longer telomeres and higher NAD levels, the coenzyme the sirtuins depend on. The sirtuins then tell cells to strengthen their defences and repair themselves, without leaving permanent damage behind.

Mammals have seven sirtuins, SIRT1 to SIRT7. They help manage inflammation, energy metabolism and DNA repair, and they need NAD+, a coenzyme whose level falls with age.

The advice follows the logic. Eat less often, or skip a meal, and put less animal protein on the plate. Train at high intensity, let yourself get cold, sit in a sauna: Sinclair offers each as a way to switch the circuit on. None of it needs a lab: a skipped breakfast will do, or a few minutes outside without a jacket. What matters in his account is the brief jolt and the repair that follows it.

Much of the evidence behind this comes from animal studies and early research. Read it as Sinclair's argument, and ask a doctor before changing how you eat or train.

[[lessons]]
$$**Give the abilities that comfort leaves idle a small, deliberate demand.** A person who takes the lift every day finds the stairs punishing on the one morning it breaks, because a capacity nobody calls on fades. A light, repeatable task, a flight of stairs or a walk your doctor would approve of, keeps it in use, and it takes no heroic change to start. Add it on purpose for a week, and write down how your body and your head respond.$$

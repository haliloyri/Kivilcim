In many companies, the person who wins a new customer is paid for it, while the work of keeping that customer goes unrewarded. One book about loyalty argues that this pay structure ignores where much of the profit actually builds.

[[story]]
Frederick Reichheld's *The Loyalty Effect* (1996) asks what a customer costs and brings in over time. The early stretch is expensive. According to a reader summary, senior managers are often pulled into writing sales proposals for new accounts, which pushes up the cost of acquiring them, and most help desk calls come from new customers.

Then the profit arrives. The summary lists four things a loyal customer adds: base profit, savings in cost, referrals and a price premium. Once they are all counted, it says, the cost of acquisition can turn negative. In mature companies, it adds, 20 to 30 percent of acquisition spending is often wasted.

Referrals carry weight of their own. Prospects who arrive through a referral close at significantly higher rates than walk-in customers, so each loyal customer who recommends the company is doing some of the selling.

Yet pay often points the other way. Many companies reward salespeople for winning new customers rather than keeping the ones they have. A salesperson who does not expect to stay has little reason to chase repeat business, and Reichheld argues the sales channel should attract the right customers, not merely many. A typical company loses 10 to 30 percent of its customers each year, so the gap between winning and keeping is not small.

The people who do the keeping matter too. The book's central thesis, per the Publishers Weekly review, is a cause-and-effect link between employee loyalty and customer loyalty. Disloyal employees rarely build lasting customer relationships, because those take time, and long-term employees add value through training, efficiency, customer selection, retention and referrals.

He also puts a number on the stakes. The jacket says a small gain in retention can double profits, and a reader summary puts it as a five percent rise in retention lifting an average customer's value by 25 to 100 percent.

That figure deserves caution. Wikipedia notes that a 1992 paper by Carrol and Reichheld disputed some retention-to-profit calculations, including earlier ones in the range of 25 to 85 percent, as resting on faulty cross-sectional comparisons. What the sources agree on is the direction: a loyal customer tends to cost less and bring more as the years go on.

[[lessons]]
$$**Reward the keeping as well as the winning.** What a team is paid or praised for decides what it chases. A café owner who gives a bonus for every new loyalty-card sign-up will get sign-ups, while nobody is thanked for remembering a regular's order. When you notice a gap like that, you can name both jobs out loud and give the quiet one, staying in touch with people who already know you, a place in the same conversation. Without that, the quiet job tends to shrink to whatever time is left after the loud one is done.$$

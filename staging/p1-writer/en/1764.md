Frederic Mors confessed to several poisonings and still walked away. Then he disappeared altogether, and the official who let him go had a simple reason: a coroner had said no one could find chloroform in a dead body.

[[story]]
In *The Poisoner's Handbook*, Deborah Blum takes the reader into New York between 1915 and 1936, when the city's way of investigating deaths was a coroner system she describes as corrupt and often unable to do its job. Mors escaped real punishment inside that system. When the authorities moved to deport him he fled the asylum where he was held, and the police could not find him.

The coroner's statement had been flat: there was no way to detect chloroform in a corpse. Blum points out that tests for chloroform already existed in 1915. The claim was wrong, and a poisoner benefited from it.

In 1918 Charles Norris became the city's first scientifically trained chief medical examiner and replaced the coroners. He set up his department in Bellevue Hospital's newly finished pathology building, with a forensic chemistry laboratory inside it. As head toxicologist he hired Alexander Gettler, the city's first, whom Blum draws as a dry, quiet, rigorously careful technician.

One of Gettler's first investigations concerned wood alcohol, the industrial alcohol made from lumber, also called methyl alcohol. His test began by grinding up a piece of tissue and adding chemicals that would produce formaldehyde if wood alcohol was present. With it, the office could show that wood alcohol poisoning was rising. A chapter summary places this work in 1918 and 1919 and has Norris warning about wood alcohol at a press conference in December 1919.

The book is built like linked short stories, each organized around one poison: chloroform, wood alcohol, cyanide and others. Together Norris and Gettler advanced many of the methods used to track poisons in the body. Where a coroner had said a substance could not be found, a laboratory could now read a result from the tissue.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask what test was actually tried before accepting "it can't be measured."** The coroner's claim about chloroform ignored a test that was available at the time. A shopkeeper who says stock keeps disappearing but nobody could ever work out why can often get an answer by writing down, for one week, what leaves the shelves and when. Before you accept that something cannot be checked, find out what the simplest measurement would be and who could take it, because that turns a closed door into a small job.$$

A sheet of paper, says Thich Nhat Hanh, has a cloud floating in it. So does the logger's daily bread, and so do the logger's mother and father. He asks you to keep looking at ordinary paper until none of that sounds strange, and then to try the same look on a person.

[[story]]
In *Peace Is Every Step* (1991), Thich Nhat Hanh begins with a sheet of paper. If you are a poet, he writes, you will see clearly that there is a cloud floating in it. Without a cloud there is no rain, without rain the trees cannot grow, and without trees there is no paper. The cloud is essential for the paper to exist, he says, and the two inter-are.

He needs a new verb for this, because "interbeing" is not in the dictionary yet. Take the prefix "inter-" and the verb "to be" and you get "inter-be." Then he keeps looking.

Sunshine is in the paper too, since nothing in a forest grows without it. So is the logger who cut the tree and carried it to the mill, and so is the wheat that became the logger's daily bread. The logger's father and mother are in it as well.

Then Hanh turns the view on the reader. ##The paper is part of your perception, so you are in it, and so is he: "Your mind is in here and mine is also."## Time, space, the earth, the rain, the minerals in the soil, the river and the heat all sit together with the sheet.

He tests the idea by taking things away. Return the sunshine to the sun, or the logger to his mother, and there is no paper. The sheet is made only of non-paper elements, he says, and to be is to inter-be. No element is optional.

He gives the same looking to anger. We accept that earthquakes and floods have causes, but when a person causes the damage we lose patience. That person has reasons too, deep-seated and immediate. Someone who speaks badly to us, he writes, may have been spoken to in exactly the same way the day before, or by an alcoholic father when he was a child.

Hanh does not say that someone who viciously attacks should go undisciplined. His point is the order: first take care of the seeds of negativity in yourself, and then, if the person needs help or discipline, you act out of compassion and not out of retribution. Order matters here.

[[lessons]]
$$**Look for the clouds behind a hurt before you answer it.** A neighbour who snaps at you over a parking space may have had the kind of morning you cannot see from your window. If you picture a few things that had to happen for that sentence to reach you, the way you would picture the rain and the logger behind a page, the sentence loses some of its sharpness. You can still say that it hurt, and you say it to a person with a whole day behind him, which tends to change what you ask for in reply.$$

An employer cuts his workers' pay and dodges the legal wage, hoping for bigger profits. When he ends up bankrupt, in money and in reputation, he blames circumstances. James Allen puts him on the same page as two other men, and none of them can see what the reader sees.

[[story]]
In *As a Man Thinketh* (1903), Allen squeezes his view of circumstances into a single line: ##"Circumstance does not make the man; it reveals him to himself."## Right after it comes one of his sharpest observations. People are anxious to improve their circumstances, he writes, but unwilling to improve themselves, and so they remain bound.

To show what he means, Allen sketches three men. The first is wretchedly poor. He badly wants better surroundings and home comforts, yet he shirks his work and feels justified in deceiving his employer because his wages are too low.

The second man is rich and suffers from a painful, persistent disease brought on by gluttony. He is willing to pay large sums of money to be rid of it. He is not willing to give up his rich food. In Allen's words, he wants to gratify his taste and have his health as well.

The third is the employer from the opening. He uses crooked measures to avoid paying the regulation wage and cuts his workers' pay in the hope of larger profits. When he goes bankrupt in both reputation and riches, he blames circumstances, without seeing that he is, as Allen puts it, the sole author of his condition.

Allen says plainly that he offers the three cases only to illustrate one idea: that a person is the cause, nearly always unconsciously, of his own circumstances. Each of the three is aiming at a good end while feeding thoughts and desires that can never lead there. He also adds a caution. A person's whole inner condition cannot be judged by someone else from the outside of their life. An honest man may suffer hardship, and a dishonest one may grow rich.

In other passages Allen goes much further, claiming that suffering is always the effect of wrong thought somewhere. That is a moral assertion from 1903, not evidence. Poverty and illness often have causes that lie well outside anyone's thinking, and the three men are most useful as a prompt to check our own habits, not as a verdict on other people's lives.

[[lessons]]
$$**When you want a result to change, look for the habit of yours that works against it.** People often want something sincerely while keeping up the very behavior that blocks it, and they rarely notice that the two pull in opposite directions. Someone who wants to save money may keep renewing a paid subscription to an app they haven't opened in months, every single month, without a second look. Spotting that one habit, without blaming yourself for the many things you can't control, shows you a small and specific place where change is actually in your hands.$$

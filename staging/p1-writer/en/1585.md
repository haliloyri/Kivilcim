To join the study you had to come into the lab and sign a consent form. After that came the camera: three weeks of photographing everything you ate or drank, each picture stamped with the time.

[[story]]
Satchin Panda's lab at the Salk Institute built a phone app for one purpose, to find out when people really eat. Volunteers in San Diego answered online and print ads. They had to be healthy adults aged 21 to 55 who were not on a diet. Each had to visit the lab in person to sign consent.

Over 150 people then photographed every meal, snack and drink for three weeks. The app sent a reminder about once a day, each photo carried a time stamp and location, and the food data themselves were not kept inside the app. The pictures added colour: coffee with milk and yogurt in the morning, sandwiches and burgers at lunch, vegetables and ice cream in the evening, alcohol late, tea all day. Chocolate and candy began to appear from about 10 a.m.

Nearly two-thirds of the volunteers also took supplements or vitamins, at times that changed from day to day, and so did their medication.

##The main finding was in the time stamps: most people ate across 15 hours or more a day.## Less than a quarter of daily calories came before noon, and over a third came after 6 p.m.

Panda and his co-author gave this irregular pattern a name, metabolic jetlag. Their proposal is that when meal times drift, the body's metabolic organs fall out of step with its circadian rhythms. It is a proposed mechanism, not a diagnosis. The data had no shift workers, and the authors said larger studies including them are needed.
Panda also stressed that changing the length of the eating day is not the only way to improve health. The study's value is smaller and more practical: it showed what a normal day looks like once a camera is watching.

[[lessons]]
$$**Write down when you eat before you try to change it.** Someone who thinks they barely eat after dinner may discover from a few days of notes that snacks run until bedtime. Guessing about your habits is easy, while a list of times shows what actually happens. Once you can see the gap between your first and last bite, you can decide whether to change anything and know what you are changing.$$

He said we, he insisted on carrying the cans, and he hinted that she was too proud to accept help. Each of those moves looks like ordinary friendliness, and Gavin de Becker lists them among the warning signs that tend to come before violence.

[[story]]
After telling Kelly's story in *The Gift of Fear* (1997), Gavin de Becker returns to the stairwell and reads the man's behaviour step by step. He calls the signs that tend to appear before violence pre-incident indicators, and he gives them names so a reader can spot them in daily life.

The first is forced teaming. It means creating a false we, as if you and a stranger shared a problem. The man on the stairs used it while insisting on carrying her cans, and it made her refusal feel awkward. The second is charm and niceness, used to disarm mistrust.

The third is too many details, since liars add detail to seem credible. The fourth is typecasting: a mild insult that invites you to prove it wrong. The insult is mild on purpose, since people tend to want to disprove it. In the stairwell it was the hint that Kelly was too proud to accept help, and the way to prove him wrong was to say yes.

Then come loan sharking, unsolicited help that creates a feeling of debt, and the unsolicited promise, a pledge nobody asked for, offered when no one had raised a doubt. ##The last on the list is discounting the word no, which means refusing to accept a refusal.##

None of these is a crime by itself, and de Becker does not say they are. Each can pass for good manners. His claim is that you can respond to the behaviour rather than to the surface of friendliness, and that the behaviour is easier to see when someone has told you what to look for.

The list is also a way to explain a feeling you cannot explain. When unease arrives in the first seconds, the names let you check it afterwards: was there a false we, a push, a no that was ignored. The feeling comes first and the vocabulary follows.

[[lessons]]
$$**Respond to what a person does, not to how pleasant they sound.** A neighbour who keeps pressing to borrow your car after you have said no twice is showing a pattern, however friendly his tone is. When you name what he is doing, you can answer the behaviour calmly: you repeat your no once and notice whether it is respected. That habit protects you without turning everyone you meet into a suspect. A calm no, repeated once, is also an easy test to run, and the reply tells you what you needed to know.$$

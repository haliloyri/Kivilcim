Score the option from 0 to 100 against the one thing that matters most. If it lands below 90, change the score to zero and walk away. The rule sounds harsh, and it grew out of a day in a maternity ward that its author still regrets.

[[story]]
Greg McKeown tells the hospital story near the start of *Essentialism*. His daughter had been born the day before. He was in the hospital with his wife and their newborn, and he was still answering work e-mails and taking work calls.

A colleague asked him to come to a client meeting that day. McKeown went. He believed the client would respect him for the sacrifice of leaving his family to be there.

It went the other way. The client seemed to think less of him for walking out on his wife and a day-old baby. The meeting itself turned out to be unimportant. Nothing came of it that justified the trip. Looking back, McKeown called it a mistake. He had said yes to please someone, and in doing so he let down his family, his own integrity and even the client relationship he was trying to protect.

##The conclusion he drew runs through the whole book: if you don't prioritize your life, someone else will.##

In the chapter called "Select," McKeown turns that lesson into a tool, the 90 percent rule. You name the single most important criterion for a decision. You score the option against it, from 0 to 100. Anything under 90 is changed to zero and rejected, with no long debate about its merits. The criteria have to be selective and written down explicitly, so a vague sense that something "looks fine" cannot slip through.

The rule is meant to cut out good options, the ones that would score 60 or 70. McKeown's argument is that good options are exactly what fill a calendar until there is no room left for the few that truly fit. He admits the cost: now and then you will refuse something that would have been perfectly fine. He backs the idea up with a line from the entrepreneur Derek Sivers: "No more yes. It's either HELL YEAH! or no."

Run the hospital day through the rule and the answer is quick. If the criterion that week was family, the client meeting would never have come close to 90. McKeown had no criterion set in advance, and so a colleague's phone call made the decision for him.

[[lessons]]
$$**Score an offer against one clear criterion, and turn down the merely good ones.** When you decide in advance what matters most, each new request meets the same test instead of whatever pressure happens to arrive with it. A mother offered a well-paid weekend project can remember that her one criterion this year is time with her young children, give the project a middling score, and decline it kindly. Scoring this way means you never have to prove that the thing you refused was bad, only that it did not fit what you had already chosen.$$

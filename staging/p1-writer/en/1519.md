There is a chapter called "Your Inner Roommate," and the roommate it describes is the voice in your head: judging, criticizing and nitpicking without a break.

[[story]]
Michael A. Singer starts *The Untethered Soul* (2007) with that voice. Part one is called Awakening Consciousness, and its four chapters run from the voice inside your head to the inner roommate, then to the question "Who are you?" and finally to what he calls the lucid self.

Singer asks readers to picture the voice as a separate person, a roommate who lives in your head and is endlessly judging, criticizing and nitpicking. Nobody would put up with that in a flat. He offers a test: the next time the voice complains, ask, "Which part of me is bothered by this?" One summary gives an example of someone snapping at their own mind at bedtime, "Can you shut up, I'm trying to sleep!"

The question quietly assumes there is a part of you that is not bothered, the part doing the watching. Singer then turns the same move on awareness itself. Who sees when I see? Who hears when I hear? Who knows that I know? Summaries trace these back to Ramana Maharshi's "Who am I?", and in that reading real meditation means becoming aware of your awareness.

He also points at how little the voice achieves. Chatter about the weather or the traffic changes neither, one summary notes, and the lucid dream is his comparison: inside a dream you can know you are dreaming, and that knowing is a different thing from whatever the dream contains.

Summaries describe two levels here. The personal self is how you see yourself and your habits of thought, while the self is consciousness that is, in one summary's words, "merely aware of the role these thoughts and feelings play." The roommate belongs to the first level, and the watcher to the second.

None of this is an experiment. Singer offers a practice that takes a minute to try, and the summaries mention no measurement showing that it works. It is a spiritual teaching, and it expects each reader to test it on their own ordinary days.

[[lessons]]
$$**Answer a harsh thought from the part of you that hears it.** A driver who is cut off in traffic hears "what an idiot" in their head and spends the rest of the trip rehearsing a speech. If they notice that they are hearing the sentence, it loses some of its authority, and they can decide whether to follow it. That gives you a pause in which to choose a reaction instead of obeying one, without having to silence anything, which is a smaller ask than winning an argument with yourself.$$

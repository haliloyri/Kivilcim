A very shiny car goes past on a busy road, and the meditator is already running after it. A few minutes later, far from where she sat down, she notices. In a guide by a former monk, that moment of noticing is the whole exercise.

[[story]]
Andy Puddicombe trained for about ten years as a Buddhist monk in Asia before he wrote *The Headspace Guide to Meditation*. The method in it is small. It is one exercise, called Take10, done once a day for ten minutes, and the book treats those ten minutes as a training ground for the rest of the waking day.

You begin with a brief check-in on your body, the sounds around you and your mood. Then you sit undisturbed with an upright back and resting hands, take a few deep breaths, and let your breathing settle into its natural rhythm. When the mind is loud, you can count breaths from one to ten and back.

According to summaries of the book, emptying the mind is not the aim. Thoughts arise the way a heartbeat does, and forcing silence is like stirring muddy water harder in the hope that it clears. Puddicombe steers between two errors, white-knuckle effort and drowsy vagueness, and aims for what summaries call relaxed alertness.

His central image is a road. You sit at the side of a busy road with traffic running in both directions, and each car or truck that passes is a thought, a physical sensation or an emotion. Your job is to sit and watch them go by.

##The trouble is that most of us do not just watch.## As the image is usually described, we run into the road, chase one particularly shiny car, get caught up in where it is going, and only minutes later notice that we have been dragged far from where we started. In the exercise, that moment of noticing is the point: you see it without irritation and return to the breath.

Summaries report him saying it gets easier with practice, with less urge to run into the road. By the same accounts, he does not claim that all thinking is bad, since creative thoughts and problem-solving thoughts have their value. What the ten minutes train is noticing which car you are following, and how quickly you can come back. The book carries the same attention into eating, walking, running and falling asleep.

[[lessons]]
$$**Treat a wandering mind as the exercise itself.** Someone reading a long email drifts into a worry about tomorrow, notices it halfway down the page, and returns to the line where she left off. The skill she is building is the noticing and the return, and each time she does it without scolding herself, the return gets a little quicker and a little cheaper. Once you stop counting every drift as proof that you are bad at this, starting again stops feeling like starting over, and an interrupted hour of work stops feeling like a lost one.$$

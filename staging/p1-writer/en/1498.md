The most common word in one kind of office is "I", followed by "me" and then "my". Nobody is being rude. It is simply how the group talks, and one research team treats that sound as the first thing a leader should listen to.

[[story]]
Dave Logan, John King and Halee Fischer-Wright set out to hear how people in companies actually talk. Their book *Tribal Leadership* (2008) rests on a study of more than 24,000 people in about two dozen companies. Their unit is the tribe, a natural cluster of roughly 20 to 150 people that forms whatever the org chart says.

Each tribe, they found, speaks from one of five stages, and a single sentence gives it away. Stage one says "Life sucks." Stage two says "My life sucks." Stage three says "I'm great (and you're not)." Stage four says "We're great," and stage five says "Life is great." Listen long enough and you can place a group.

The two lowest stages sound quiet and heavy. At stage two people do not try much, do not hold one another to account, and say that nothing will ever change. One summary ties stage one to street gangs.

Most groups sit in the middle. One summary of the book gives the split as roughly 2, 25, 49, 22 and 2 percent from stage one to five, so about half of what they saw was stage three. There, people win one at a time and keep what they know to themselves. They relate in pairs, and ##the most common words are "I", then "me" and "my".## The usual complaints are "too busy", "no time" and "no support".

The authors say tribal leadership comes down to two things: the words people use and the kinds of relationships they form. A leader does not need to announce a new culture. The leader needs to answer a stage-three group in the language of the next stage, never more than one step up.

For stage three, that means asking open questions until you learn what people value. Then comes a project too big for anyone to finish alone, so people have to partner beyond their usual pairs. The same summary lists the signs that it is working: people say "we" instead of "I", their networks grow from a few dozen contacts to several hundred, and they work less while getting more done.

Stage five is rare and unstable. The team that built the first Macintosh is the book's example, and teams like it often fall back to stage four to regroup before they try again.

[[lessons]]
$$**Listen to a group's words before you try to change it.** A shop owner who wants her staff to cover for each other could first notice whether the talk is "my customer" and "my shift" or "our customer". If it is nearly all "my", a speech about teamwork will slide off, while a job that needs two people to finish will not. Counting the pronouns for a week costs nothing and gives you a plain first reading of where the team stands. It also gives you a way to notice, a few weeks later, whether the talk has started to move.$$

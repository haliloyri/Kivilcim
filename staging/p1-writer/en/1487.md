Open a feed and it hands you the next item before you asked for it. Johann Hari asks who built it to do that, and what the builders get from every extra minute you spend looking at it.

[[story]]
In *Stolen Focus* (2022), Johann Hari argues that many of the apps on a phone are built to capture attention and hold it. He does not read this as a side effect. He treats it as one of the deeper forces behind the modern attention crisis, which he splits into twelve causes.

A review says the book draws on over 250 interviews with experts in neuroscience, psychology, education and technology.

He names two platforms in particular: Facebook and YouTube. They try to maximise screen time, and the reason he gives is practical. More time on screen means more personal data to collect, and that data feeds the recommendations and the longer engagement that follow. The next video or post appears because the design wants the minute.

The book also gives space to Silicon Valley dissidents, people who worked out how human attention can be hacked. What they say about the mechanics is theirs to tell. What matters for Hari's argument is that the knowledge came from inside the industry.

##If a product is built to keep you looking, failing to look away is not a plain personal weakness.## That is the core of his case against the story that everyone simply needs more discipline. The contest is uneven when one side is a person and the other is a system tuned to hold that person.

He extends the same reading to work. Always-on culture and constant availability, he argues, bring stress and lower productivity, and no amount of private resolve removes the message that arrives at nine in the evening.

So his answer is collective. He proposes regulating tech companies, treating attention as a public health issue, setting limits on internet use, shortening working hours and giving children more play away from screens. He names the whole push an attention rebellion.

[[lessons]]
$$**Change the setting before you blame yourself.** A shopper who always leaves with a chocolate bar from the till may call it weakness, when the shop put the bar at the till on purpose. If an app keeps taking your evenings, look first at what it was built to do, such as autoplay or a feed with no end. Then you can alter one setting, and see whether your behaviour changes with it. It costs a minute and keeps the question on the design instead of on you.$$

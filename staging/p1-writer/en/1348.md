"I hate falling, that feeling in your chest," Mia Blundetto said. As a child she wouldn't go down a water slide until middle school. At twenty-three she stood first in line at the open door of a C130, and on her third jump she landed on top of someone else's parachute.

[[story]]
Peter C. Brown, Henry L. Roediger III and Mark A. McDaniel tell her story in *Make It Stick* (2014). Mia was a first lieutenant in the Marine Corps, posted to logistics in Okinawa. The job she was offered, commanding an air delivery platoon, required jump school. Her commanding officer said he could give it to the next guy instead. She looked at him and said, "Yes, sir, I'll jump out of planes."

The Army's jump school at Fort Benning, Georgia, does not let trainees carry a notebook or write anything down. You listen, watch, rehearse and execute. The test is the doing itself.

The core skill is the parachute landing fall. You hit the ground and roll so the impact spreads across the balls of your feet, the side of your calf, your thigh, your hip and your back, in one of six directions depending on wind, terrain and drift. It is first taught in a gravel pit, where you watch it, try it, get corrected and try again.

Over the following weeks the heights climb. A two-foot platform. A zip line a dozen feet up. A twelve-foot platform with a mockup of an aircraft door, where the instructor, not you, pulls the release and drops you the last two or three feet from whichever direction he likes. Then a thirty-four-foot tower. The practice is spaced and mixed almost by accident, because everyone waits their turn at each station. In week three, those still in the course make five real jumps from a military transport.

On Mia's third jump, from 1,250 feet, she was first at the port door. She jumped, counted, and at four thousand a green parachute was wrapped all around her. She was on top of the first jumper's canopy. She "just sort of swam out" and steered away. Asked later if it had frightened her, she said not at all. ##The moves had been settling into her for weeks before she ever needed them in the air.##

The authors use her training to explain how learning sets. New learning is fragile. In a process called consolidation, the brain reorganizes and stabilizes memory traces over several hours or longer, replaying what you learned, filling in gaps and tying it to what you already know. Sleep seems to help, they note, but in any case consolidation takes time, which is why practice with at least a day between sessions works well.

Their comparison is writing an essay. The first draft is rangy. You put it aside to ferment, and when you pick it up a day or two later you can see what you were trying to say.

[[lessons]]
$$**Leave time between practice sessions so learning can settle.** What you practise keeps consolidating for hours or days after you stop, so a night or two between sessions is part of the work rather than a break from it. Someone learning a new recipe who cooks it once tonight and again a few days later will usually see exactly where they hesitate the second time, which several back-to-back attempts in one evening would hide. Planning sessions with gaps gives you sturdier skills for the same effort, and the second session shows you honestly what stayed.$$

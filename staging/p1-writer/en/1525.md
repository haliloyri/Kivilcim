Real writers do not sit down feeling wildly enthusiastic and confident, and not one of them writes an elegant first draft. There is a single exception, and the others do not like her very much. Everyone else gets a terrible first draft down anyway.

[[story]]
Anne Lamott's chapter on first drafts in *Bird by Bird* (1994) starts by correcting a picture. People who do not write imagine that real writers sit down at the desk full of enthusiasm and confidence, and that good sentences simply arrive. Among the writers Lamott knows, none works that way. None of them writes an elegant first draft either.

Her own method is blunt. For her and most of the writers she knows, the only way to get anything written at all is to write really, really shitty first drafts. The right words and sentences just do not come pouring out like ticker tape most of the time, and very few writers know what they are doing until they have done it.

She gives her restaurant reviews as the example. The research was the pleasant part: dinners with friends and meticulous notes. The first draft, though, brought panic each time, and writing a long one still turned out to be a relief.

She calls the first draft the child's draft. You let it all pour out and let it romp all over the place, knowing that no one is going to see it and that you can shape it later. If a character wants to say "Well, so what, Mr. Poopy Pants?", you let her.

A friend of hers has names for the stages. The first draft is the down draft, where you just get it down. The second is the up draft, where you fix it up. The third is the dental draft, where you check every tooth to see whether it is loose, cramped, decayed or, God help us, healthy.

##Almost all good writing begins with terrible first efforts.## You need to start somewhere, and Lamott's advice for starting is to get something, anything, down on paper.

[[lessons]]
$$**Give the first version a reader who does not exist.** Someone who has to write a firm email to a landlord usually stares at the screen for an hour, trying to get the first sentence right. If they write an ugly, angry version that they will never send, they end up with real material to cut and soften. Judging the words in a separate pass makes it much easier to begin. Knowing nobody will read it lets the first sentence come out without fear, and the actual message takes shape from the rough one.$$

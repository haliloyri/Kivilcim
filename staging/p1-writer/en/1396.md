[[open]]
Soccer ends with a whistle and a score on the board. Business has no whistle, yet most companies play as if one were about to blow. One number about how long big companies last shows what that habit can cost.

[[story]]
Simon Sinek builds *The Infinite Game* (2019) on a distinction he borrows from James Carse. In a finite game the players are known, the rules are fixed, and everyone agrees on the end, which brings a winner. Soccer is the example: two known teams play by one set of rules until the final whistle, and the score on the board settles who won. In an infinite game the players come and go, the rules can change, and the aim is to keep the game going.

Sinek says business, relationships and education belong to the second kind. Nobody wins them for good. A leader who treats them as finite plays to beat other people, and the contest never reaches a final whistle.

He sets the two mindsets side by side. A finite-minded leader plays to beat others, chases near-term profit and can only react when disruption comes. An infinite-minded leader aims to stay in the game and to come out of each setback stronger.

Sinek also cites a study showing that the lifespan of S&P 500 companies fell from about 61 years to about 18. The figure belongs to his case that a company's position is never settled.

He lists five practices: advance a just cause, build trusting teams, study worthy rivals, practice existential flexibility, and show the courage to lead. ##A just cause, in his words, is a specific vision of a future state that does not yet exist.## He says all five should be adopted together.

One caution comes from a reviewer. Carse himself acknowledges that everyone plays finite games, and Sinek gives anything finite a negative tone that Carse does not. The book's contrast is useful, but it is sharper than the original.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask whether the game has a final whistle.** A shop owner who squeezes a supplier to the bone wins this month, then finds nobody willing to rush an order the week she is in trouble. Nothing in her work ends with a score, so the better test of a decision is whether it leaves her able to keep going. Asking that before a negotiation or a price cut shows you which wins you can afford and which ones cost you tomorrow.$$

[[reflect]]
&&Think of a decision you made lately to come out ahead: what would you have chosen if the aim had been staying in the game for years?&&

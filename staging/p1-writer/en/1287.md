"Peace, be still!" Those are the last words of a book about the power of thought, and they are addressed not to the world but to the reader's own heart. A few pages earlier, the same book makes an unglamorous claim about an ordinary shopkeeper and why customers choose him.

[[story]]
James Allen's *As a Man Thinketh* (1903) spends most of its short length on how thought shapes character and circumstance. Then it ends somewhere unexpected. The final chapter is called "Serenity," and its first line is: ##"Calmness of mind is one of the beautiful jewels of wisdom."##

Allen doesn't treat calm as luck or temperament. He calls it the result of long and patient effort in self-control, and a sign of ripened experience. As a person comes to understand how thought works, in himself and in others, he "ceases to fuss and fume and worry and grieve," and stays poised and steady.

Then the shopkeeper appears. Even the ordinary trader, Allen writes, will find his business prosper as he develops greater self-control and equanimity. The reason is simple. People will always prefer to deal with someone whose manner is steady.

The calm person in Allen's account has learned to govern himself, and so knows how to adapt to others. They in turn respect his strength and feel they can learn from him and rely on him. Allen gives him two images: a shade-giving tree in a thirsty land, and a sheltering rock in a storm.

He sets the opposite beside it. He writes of how many people sour their lives and ruin all that is sweet and beautiful with explosive tempers, and he wonders aloud whether most people do not mar their happiness through lack of self-control.

The last pages turn to the sea. Humanity, Allen writes, surges with uncontrolled passion and is blown about by anxiety and doubt. Only the wise man, whose thoughts are controlled, makes the winds and storms of the soul obey him. To the "tempest-tossed souls," he promises that in the ocean of life the isles of Blessedness are waiting.

The book closes on a hand at the helm. Keep your hand firmly upon the helm of thought, Allen tells the reader, and leaves three short sentences: "Self-control is strength; Right Thought is mastery; Calmness is power." Then the final line, spoken to oneself: "Peace, be still!"

[[lessons]]
$$**Treat calm as a choice you make at the moment you start to fume.** Staying steady is less a matter of temperament than of many small moments of self-control, and the people around you usually feel the effect before you do. A help-desk worker who answers furious callers without raising her voice or rushing them can, over time, become the person colleagues route the hardest calls to. Catching the instant you begin to fuss and fume lets you delay your first reply by one breath and choose for yourself where the conversation goes next.$$

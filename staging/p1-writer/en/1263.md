He retired with a small apartment, a pension, and a drawer full of expense checks he had never cashed. He had written one academic paper and no books, and the Air Force never promoted him past colonel. Yet he is one of the most influential strategists most people have never heard of.

[[story]]
In Ryan Holiday's account in *Ego Is the Enemy*, John Boyd was one of the most influential strategists in modern warfare, and most people have never heard of him. After flying in Korea he became lead instructor at the Fighter Weapons School at Nellis Air Force Base, where he was known as Forty-Second Boyd, because he could beat any opponent from any position in under forty seconds. A few years later he was quietly summoned to the Pentagon.

His ideas reshaped maneuver warfare across the armed forces, and the F-15 and F-16 were his pet projects. He taught through legendary briefings and through the pupils he mentored and protected. No base carries his name, and no battleship. He retired assuming he would be forgotten, and Holiday asks what if that was deliberate.

Boyd was living the lesson he gave each promising young officer. In 1973 he called in a protégé at what he sensed was a turning point in the young man's life. The officer was insecure and impressionable, wanted a promotion and wanted to do well. Boyd gave him a speech he would repeat until it became a rite of passage for a generation of military leaders.

> "Tiger, one day you will come to a fork in the road. And you're going to have to make a decision about which direction you want to go."

Boyd marked off the two directions with his hands. Down one, you can be somebody. You will make compromises and turn your back on your friends, but you will be a member of the club, with promotions and good assignments. Down the other, you can do something for your country, your Air Force and yourself. You may not get promoted or the good posts, and your superiors will not favor you, but you will not have to compromise yourself, and your work might make a difference. ##In life there is often a roll call, Boyd said, and that is when you decide.## He closed with a question: to be or to do, which way will you go?

He kept a second exercise for groups of officers. On a chalkboard he wrote DUTY, HONOR, COUNTRY in big letters, crossed them out and wrote PRIDE, POWER, GREED in their place. His point was that the structures soldiers climb to get ahead can corrupt the very values they were built to serve. Boyd paid for his own choice. Because of his frugal lifestyle he was called the ghetto colonel, and he died with a drawer holding thousands of dollars in uncashed expense checks from private contractors, which he equated with bribes.

[[lessons]]
$$**Weigh an offer by the work it would let you do.** Boyd's fork turns into a question you can put to any opportunity: does this move me toward doing something, or only toward being someone? An accountant offered a manager's title that would pull her away from the work she knows can write down what she wants to be doing and read the offer against that page before she answers. The comparison shows whether the promotion serves your purpose or only the expectations of the ladder, and it gives you plain words for a polite no or a counteroffer.$$

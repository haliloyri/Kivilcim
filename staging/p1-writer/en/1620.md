In the middle of an argument, one partner stops complaining and says in one plain sentence what they actually need. It sounds small. Sue Johnson built the second half of her method around that sentence, and her book takes its title from it.

[[story]]
*Hold Me Tight* (2008) is Sue Johnson's guide for couples, built as seven conversations. The fourth, which gives the book its name, is the one where a partner asks directly for what they need. Everything before it prepares the ground, because that sentence cannot be said from inside an attack.

Her therapy has stages, nine steps in all: four in the first stage, three in the second and two in the third. The first calms the cycle: it identifies the conflict issues and the negative pattern, brings attachment emotions to the surface and reframes the problem as the cycle. The second stage, restructuring the bond, is where this conversation lives, in steps five to seven. Its steps are to reach needs and emotions the person has disowned, to promote acceptance of the other's experience, and to help each partner express their needs so that the way the two interact actually changes.

The order matters because of what the earlier patterns hide. In the Protest Polka the reaching partner is protesting and the other is pulling back to defend themselves. Under both sit feelings and needs that neither has said out loud. Naming them is the work of the second stage, and the partner who withdraws is usually invited to take the steps slightly ahead of the other.

Johnson frames the question under those needs in three words. Is my partner accessible, responsive and engaged? The book calls this A.R.E., and it is a question about the other person's presence, not about the topic of the argument. When a need is voiced from that softer place, the listener has something to answer that is not an accusation.

The third stage forms new stories and solutions and consolidates the new cycles of behaviour. The aim, in Johnson's phrase, is effective dependency, including secure attachment. A 2019 meta-analysis by Beasley and Ager found that the therapy significantly improves relationship satisfaction, with gains sustained up to two years later. The psychologist James Coyne, by contrast, called an earlier 1999 meta-analysis of the method poor quality, so the evidence is encouraging but not beyond dispute.

[[lessons]]
$$**Say what you need before you argue for it.** A shift worker tired of covering for a colleague stops listing the colleague's mistakes and says instead that she needs to know the next handover will be shared out fairly between them. Naming the need gives the other person something to answer besides an accusation. It also shows you how small the actual request was, which is often much smaller than the argument that carried it, and a small request is easier to grant. Next time the same argument starts, you can try that sentence first.$$

Before buying a car, people read the ratings and check what it will resell for. Before choosing a vocation, a writer observes, many simply slide in rather than decide. The big choices, he argues, get less deliberation than the small ones.

[[story]]
David Brooks makes that point in *The Second Mountain*. People deliberate more carefully over little choices than over big ones, he says. For the car they check the ratings and the resale values. For a career they slide rather than decide, and they marry the person they happen to be living with. He quotes L. A. Paul on the stakes: you should not fool yourself, you have no idea what you are getting into.

After several years of pursuing open options, Brooks writes, it is not so much that you lose the thread of the meaning of your life. You have trouble even staying focused on the question. On the first mountain, a person makes individual choices and keeps their options open. The second mountain is a vale of promise making.

Most of us make four big commitments over a lifetime: to a vocation, to a spouse and family, to a philosophy or faith, and to a community. Each one requires a vow, an investment of time and effort, and a willingness to close off other options.

He defines a commitment as making a promise to something without expecting a return, and he says the deep ones arrive at a double negative: *I can't not do this.* Some person, cause or field of research has become part of your identity. I'm a musician. I'm a scientist. I'm a Marine. A contract is a weighing of pros and cons, and the person signing it does not change. A commitment changes who you are.

##A commitment is love and promise put under law.## People bind their future selves to obligations, because feelings do not always hold. Brooks's fullest definition is falling in love with something and then building a structure of behaviour around it for the moments when love falters.

He also splits freedom in two, freedom from and freedom to. You have to chain yourself to the piano and practise year after year if you want the freedom to really play. He quotes Tim Keller: real freedom is not so much the absence of restrictions as finding the right ones.

Commitments give you your identity, he adds, because they are how you introduce yourself to strangers. The life well lived, in his words, is a journey from open options to sweet compulsions.

[[lessons]]
$$**Build a structure that holds your future self.** The excitement is high at the moment of signing up for a spring race, and a week of heavy work can empty it. A runner who knows this books a fixed group run for Saturday mornings, so the plan keeps working on weeks when the wish is thin and the decision never has to be made again at dawn. When you attach a commitment to something outside your mood, it carries you on the thin days too, and you do not have to be talked into it every morning.$$

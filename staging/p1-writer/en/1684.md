Goleman locates the training of attention at one specific moment, the second you catch your mind drifting and bring it back. The exercise sits at the place where focus slips, and like a weight in a gym it gets lifted again and again.

[[story]]
In *Focus*, Daniel Goleman says the strength of attention is limited but can be improved with training, and he compares it to a muscle that grows with use. Training works, he explains, by building new connections between neurons.

The point that stands out is about practice itself. Neuroplasticity, the strengthening of old circuits and the building of new ones for a skill, requires paying attention. If you rehearse a skill while your attention is somewhere else, the circuits for that skill are not rewired. Repetition by itself is not enough, because the mind has to be present during it.

Mind-wandering gets a fair hearing in the book. Goleman calls it the default mode and gives it two faces. It can support self-reflection and problem-solving. It also raises errors and accidents, lowers comprehension and can slide into rumination, running the same thought around again and again. You cannot force a wandering mind to arrive. It comes when it comes.

Mindfulness practice works with that fact. When the mind has drifted after a thought, you notice it, let the thought go and direct attention back to the present moment. In the framework Goleman draws on, bringing the mind back carries deep significance for the wiring of the brain. So a drifting mind is the raw material of the exercise, because there is nothing to bring back until attention has left. Each return puts attention on a chosen task once more, and a person building a skill can watch how often they come back as well as how seldom they wander.

Meditation is not the only route he mentions. He also discusses research suggesting that digital games can improve visual attention, object tracking, processing speed, task switching and memory capacity. The two methods look nothing alike, and what they share is that attention is handed to a defined task and kept there.

[[lessons]]
$$**Bring your attention to the practice as well as your time.** A skill grows from the minutes your mind is actually on it, and hours with a split focus add less than they seem to. A guitar student who runs through scales with the television on may progress less than one who gives the fingers and the notes ten full minutes of attention. Practicing with the phone put away and one task open lets the same effort do more of the work.$$

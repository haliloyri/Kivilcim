A tennis match is not fully in anyone's control, because someone else is hitting the ball back. A Stoic player therefore changes what counts as success before the first serve, and leaves whatever happens across the net to the opponent and to chance.

[[story]]
Epictetus's central choice, as William Irvine presents it in *A Guide to the Good Life* (2008), is whether to concern ourselves with things external to us or things internal. Irvine adds a third category. Some things we control fully, some not at all, and some only in part. Complete control covers our goals, our values and our character.

For the second kind, the Stoics set the worry aside as pointless. Epictetus compares us to actors in a play written by the Fates, and Marcus advises accepting the past and present and loving the people fate puts around us. The middle category is where life gets difficult, and Irvine's example is a tennis match. Whether you win depends partly on your opponent and partly on chance, so it is not fully yours to control. You cannot control your opponent's shots, but you can control your preparation and how you play.

A Stoic player therefore changes the goal. Instead of the external goal, to win, he sets an internal one: to play his best. That goal is entirely his. Irvine calls this internalizing a goal, and the point is that the player no longer ties his emotional response to whether the outcome happens.

The effect is modest and Irvine does not oversell it. An internal goal lowers the chance of being upset by the result. It does not necessarily lower the chance of losing.

His second example is a novelist. An aspiring novelist should aim to write well, he says, and not only to get published. Irvine admits that this may look like a mind game, yet he calls it an effective trick for staying joyful.

Critics push back. Donald Robertson and others say the trichotomy and the idea of internal goals are not found in ancient Stoicism, and Irvine himself admits that his version is unlike the Stoicism of any particular Stoic. His own aim is tranquility rather than virtue as an end in itself.

[[lessons]]
$$**For something only partly in your hands, set a goal that is wholly in them.** A person applying for a job cannot decide whether the offer comes, but they can decide to give clear answers and to ask one real question in the interview. If they treat those two things as the goal, their mood after the interview no longer hangs on the offer. They have done their share, and the rest can be seen as someone else's decision. They can still hope for the offer, but the hope no longer decides how the evening goes.$$

On 360-degree ratings, average performers tend to score themselves above where colleagues place them. Stars rarely do, and some score themselves below it. Daniel Goleman builds his case for accurate self-assessment on that odd pattern, which runs against what you might expect.

[[story]]
In *Working with Emotional Intelligence* (1998), Daniel Goleman puts self-awareness first among the emotional competencies. One of its three parts is accurate self-assessment, which means knowing your own strengths and limits as they really are. The other two are emotional self-awareness and self-confidence. Accuracy is the point.

He leans on studies. In one, several hundred managers from twelve organizations were compared, and accurate self-assessment was the hallmark of the superior performers. In another, covering several hundred knowledge workers such as computer scientists and auditors at companies like AT&T and 3M, it showed up in nearly every star performer. Nearly every star had it.

The 360-degree ratings add a twist. On them, average performers tended to overestimate their strengths. Stars rarely did, and they often underestimated their abilities, which Goleman links to high internal standards. A gap here, he says, can derail a career.

Self-confidence, the third part, gets evidence of its own. In a study of 112 entry-level accountants, Goleman writes, self-efficacy predicted how supervisors rated their performance ten months later, and it did so more strongly than skill or prior training. In a sixty-year study of more than a thousand high-IQ people, early self-confidence predicted career success.

He gives a small example of what emotional self-awareness looks like on the job. It helped financial planners manage their own reactions when they had to talk about mortality while selling life insurance. The planner who notices his own discomfort can act on it before the client feels it.

The guide to the book lists what tends to block this kind of awareness: blind ambition, unrealistic goals, relentless striving, driving others, a hunger for power, an insatiable need for recognition, a preoccupation with appearances and a need to seem perfect. It adds a plainer obstacle: we need time to be introspective, and we don't get it, or we don't take it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask for a second rating before you trust your own.** A cook who thinks he stays calm in the dinner rush may be the one the line cooks quietly work around. If he asks one of them to rate his calm on the same scale he used for himself, the gap between the two scores tells him more than another year of private reflection would. Choose one skill, ask one person you trust, and listen to the answer without arguing, since the answer is information and not a verdict, and you can ask again in a few months to see whether it has moved.$$

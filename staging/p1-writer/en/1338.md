Navarro's tip for your desk is oddly specific: spread your things out instead of stacking them up. It belongs to his larger argument that confidence shows in the space a person takes before they have said a single word.

[[story]]
In *What Every BODY Is Saying*, published in 2008, Joe Navarro describes confidence as something the body announces ahead of speech. The hands are a good place to look. Pressing the fingertips together to form a steeple is, in his words, probably the most powerful high-confidence sign. People tend to do it, without noticing, when they feel sure of themselves.

A second signal is territory. Navarro says confident people expand and unsure people contract. You can see it on a desk, where spreading items outward signals confidence and piling them up near yourself signals the opposite.

If you feel yourself pulling in, the steeple is one of the easier gestures to try, since it takes no equipment and no permission. It also gives your hands something calm to do, which many people lack in a high-stakes meeting.

##Confidence shows in your hands and in the room you take, before you say anything.##

Hands also carry other messages. Hands on the hips, with the elbows out, display authority or readiness. Hands pushed into the pockets with the fingers left hanging outside are read as a sign of low confidence.

The desk advice sounds trivial, yet it follows from the same idea. A person arranging a workspace is deciding, without noticing, how much room to claim.

The logic works in reverse as well. Navarro says shoulders that drop, hands that disappear and a body that shrinks are picked up by observers, often without their knowing why. A person can announce certainty aloud while the body says something else.

He does not offer these as grounds for a verdict from one gesture. Two people in a meeting can differ, one with her fingers in a steeple and the other with his hands in his pockets, and you will only learn how each really feels by comparing them with their own normal. What a given sign means for a given person depends on that person's baseline. Your own posture, on the other hand, is something you can pick.

[[lessons]]
$$**Choose what your hands and your space say before you walk in.** A new hire who tucks herself into the corner of the table at her first meeting looks hesitant before she speaks. When the same person sits with her hands on the table and her shoulders open, she is read as less hesitant and often feels less so. Try it deliberately in one meeting, and you will notice what your posture tells others and what it tells you. If you like, rehearse it once in an empty room beforehand.$$

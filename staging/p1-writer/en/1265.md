The news reached an emperor, late in his reign, who was sick and possibly near death: his old friend and most trusted general had declared himself Caesar in Syria. The emperor's first move was to tell his own soldiers nothing, and to wait.

[[story]]
Ryan Holiday ends *The Obstacle Is the Way* with this episode from the late reign of Marcus Aurelius. Avidius Cassius, the general, had heard the emperor was vulnerable or possibly dead, and the ambitious man decided to seize the throne by force.

Marcus should have been angry, Holiday writes, and history would have forgiven him for wanting to crush the man who threatened his life, his family and his legacy. Instead he did nothing. He even kept the news from his troops, who might have been enraged on his behalf, and waited to see whether Cassius would come to his senses.

The man held his course. So Marcus called a council of his soldiers and made an unusual announcement. They would march against Cassius and take the great prize of war and victory, which was to capture him and try not to kill him. Marcus spoke of forgiving a man who has wronged you, of staying a friend to someone who broke friendship, of keeping faith with someone who broke faith. If this unwanted situation gave them any profit, he said, it would be to settle the affair well and show all mankind that there is a right way to deal even with civil wars. He sent troops to Rome to calm the panicking crowds and then set out to do what had to be done.

A lone assassin struck down Cassius in Egypt three months later, and the rebellion ended there. So did Marcus's hope of forgiving his betrayer in person. ##What he lost in one forgiveness he got back as the chance for a far wider one.## Arriving in the provinces, he refused to put any coconspirators to death and declined to prosecute the senators and governors who had backed the uprising. When other senators insisted on death sentences for their peers, he wrote a short reply asking them to keep his reign unstained by the blood of any senator.

Holiday reads the whole episode as perception controlled before action: Marcus felt no hatred, spoke no ill word against his enemy and took none of it personally, and then he acted rightly and firmly. It fits the way the book opens: an emperor writing for himself at night in a tent on the front lines in Germania, in a reign of constant war, a horrific plague and an attempt on his throne by one of his closest allies.

[[lessons]]
$$**See what happened before you answer it.** When Marcus heard about the revolt he did not react. He waited, and moved only once it was clear that Cassius would not turn back. A café owner who learns that her business partner has quietly shifted to another shop can write down what actually happened and what the business needs to keep going before she sends the angry message. That puts your need in the place of your anger, so you can pick one next move, and that move stays yours instead of belonging to the first hot hour.$$

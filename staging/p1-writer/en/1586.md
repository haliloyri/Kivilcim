[[story]]
The mice came first. In Panda's lab two groups were fed a diet that took 60 percent of its calories from fat. One group ate whenever it wanted. The other had food only in an 8-hour window each night and fasted about 16 hours a day. Two control groups ate a standard diet with about 13 percent of its calories from fat.

Both high-fat groups ate the same number of calories. With the usual suspect held equal, the only thing left to differ between the two cages was the hour at which the food bowl was open.

After 100 days, the mice that ate freely had put on weight and developed high cholesterol, high blood glucose, liver damage and reduced motor control. ##The mice with the 8-hour window weighed 28 percent less and showed none of those problems.## They also did better than the other groups on an exercise test.

Panda did not oversell it. He said at the time that human studies would have to prove it, and co-author Megumi Hatori added that there were bound to be limits to the protection.

The human test was small. Eight overweight participants, all eating over 14 hours a day, were asked to eat within 10 to 11 hours and keep everything else the same, which meant giving up three or four hours of eating time. For 16 weeks they kept a weekly feedogram, a chart of their eating pattern. They lost weight and said they felt more energetic and slept better.

Eight people is a first signal, not a settled result. Panda added that eating duration is not the only way to improve health, and that it may be risky for people with undiagnosed fasting hypoglycemia.

[[lessons]]
$$**When you eat deserves a question alongside what and how much.** A person who grazes in the kitchen until midnight could pick a fixed closing time for one week and see how they sleep. If you have diabetes or low blood sugar, ask your doctor before changing the hours you eat, since changing meal times can affect it. Treat the week as a small experiment in your own routine rather than a promise that it will behave like the mice.$$

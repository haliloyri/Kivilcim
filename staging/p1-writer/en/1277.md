By one measure, Google barely mattered. Call it an advertising company and its slice of the world's ad market came to 3.4%. Call it a search company and the number jumped to 68%. Peter Thiel thinks the gap between those two figures explains why some companies get rich and others never do.

[[story]]
In *Zero to One* (2014), Thiel builds the argument on a comparison most people would never think to make: airlines and Google. In 2012 the average American airfare was $178. On each of those trips, the airlines made 37 cents per passenger. That was the profit on a whole industry's worth of tickets, counted one seat at a time.

That same year Google brought in about $50 billion and kept 21% of it as profit. By Thiel's count that margin was more than a hundred times what the airline industry managed. Google was also worth roughly three times all the US airlines put together.

His explanation is short. The airlines compete with each other. Google stands alone. Where companies compete on the same product, prices fall until profits head toward zero, and the value a company creates mostly slips through its fingers. A monopoly gets to keep what it makes.

Then Thiel points out something odd: both sides hide the truth, in opposite directions. In May 2014 Google held about 68% of search, with Microsoft and Yahoo at roughly 19% and 10%. Yet Google preferred to describe itself as an advertising company. US search advertising was a $17 billion market. Global advertising was $495 billion. Inside that bigger frame, Google's share shrinks to 3.4%, and a dominant firm starts to look like a small player in a crowded field. Thiel's reading is that monopolists talk up their competition so that nobody, regulators included, looks too closely at how much of their real market they own.

Companies stuck in real competition do the reverse. They keep narrowing the definition of their market until they look unique. Thiel's example is a restaurant that calls itself the only British restaurant in Palo Alto. On paper, at the overlap of "British food" and "Palo Alto," it has no rivals. But someone hungry on a Tuesday night is choosing among every restaurant in town.

Thiel adds one more cost of competition, and it is about thinking. ##Rivals fight because they are alike, and the longer they fight, the more they copy each other.## The airlines' 37 cents is what years of selling the same seat in the same way to the same customers looks like on a balance sheet.

[[lessons]]
$$**Find the ground where you have no close copy before you fight harder.** When everyone offers the same thing, extra effort usually turns into lower prices, and the value you create flows to the customer or the next competitor. A freelance photographer who keeps undercutting five others on wedding packages may earn less every season, while one who is the only person in town shooting old family film transfers can set a fair price and keep it. Asking what you do that nobody nearby does lets you put your effort where it can actually pay you back.$$

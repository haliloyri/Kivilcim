[[story]]
Daniel Coyle opens his argument about belonging in *The Culture Code* (2018) with the work of Alex Pentland at the MIT Media Lab. Pentland built a sociometer, a sensor that records signals such as eye contact, mimicry and physical closeness. He had people wear it while they acted out negotiations over salary, vacation and benefits.

The sensors kept no record of who made which argument. They tracked how the two people moved and looked at each other: who made eye contact, who mirrored the other, how close they sat. From the first five minutes of that record, he could predict how each negotiation would turn out. Five minutes is early in a talk about salary, vacation and benefits. The record from that stretch held glances, mirrored gestures and the distance between two chairs, and it was enough.

##The way the people interacted mattered more than the content of the talk.##

Coyle uses the finding to explain the small signals he calls belonging cues. What the cues share, in Coyle's account, are three qualities. They carry energy. They are individualised, aimed at this person and not at a crowd. And they point to the future, suggesting the relationship will continue. Together they send one message: you are safe here.

That is why the first minutes weigh so much. A group is constantly deciding, below the level of words, whether a newcomer or a colleague belongs. The cues answer the question before anyone speaks the answer out loud.

Coyle's evidence here is a lab setup, a role-played negotiation, and it supports a pattern rather than a rule for every office. Still, the picture is hard to forget: a room in which the sensors know the outcome before the people have finished talking.

[[lessons]]
$$**Start a conversation with signals, then content.** A supervisor opening a hard talk with a quiet employee may spend the first minutes looking at her notes while she speaks. If he turns toward the person, makes eye contact and says her name, he sends a message that the rest of the talk can rest on. You can use the first minutes of a meeting to show attention, and notice how the conversation changes afterwards. Even a short, steady look at the person speaking is a signal that can be sent on any ordinary day, in any room.$$

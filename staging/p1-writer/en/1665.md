The woman in front of him walked with a cane, and her face had shriveled like an old apple. Her daughter, once full of energy, looked worn out. They were old friends he had not seen for almost thirty years, and the meeting would lead him to doubt the very thing that seemed to have aged them.

[[story]]
Eckhart Tolle uses that meeting in *A New Earth* to open what he calls the paradox of time. He had run into a family he had not seen in a long time, and he was shocked when he saw them. The mother, who walked with a cane, seemed to have shrunk. Her daughter, who had been full of energy and the expectations of youth, looked worn out after bringing up three children.

Then he remembered that almost thirty years had passed since they last met. Time had done this to them.

Yet he turns the point around. ##Everything seems to be subject to time, but all of it happens in the Now.## The cane and the worn-out face look like the work of thirty years, yet each of those years was lived one present moment at a time. You never experience time itself. You only ever experience the present moment, or rather what happens in it.

On his account, past and future moments exist only when you remember or anticipate them, and you do that by thinking in the one moment there is: this one. It looks as though there are many moments because we confuse the present moment with what happens in it. He accepts clock time for practical purposes such as an appointment or a trip. What he objects to is the mind's endless preoccupation with past and future.

The ego, he says, treats the present moment in three ways. First as a means to an end: the moment is only a way of reaching some later one that counts more, and you are never fully here because you are always busy getting elsewhere. Then as an obstacle, which brings impatience, frustration and stress, and turns life into a problem to be solved before you can be happy. Finally as an enemy: you hate what you are doing, complain about your surroundings, and run an inner dialogue of shoulds, shouldn'ts and blame.

Tolle's question is: what is my relationship with the present moment? Am I treating the Now as a means, an obstacle or an enemy? Seeing the answer, he says, is already presence, and with the seeing comes the power of choice. He suggests asking it often, until you no longer need to.

[[lessons]]
$$**Name your relationship with this moment.** Someone waiting in a long queue at the post office experiences the wait as lost time and keeps leaning toward the front of the line. Once she notices and asks herself whether she is treating the moment as a means, an obstacle or an enemy, she may see that she is also standing in it, and the tension loosens a little. That small step reminds her that life is going on even in the queue, and it makes the next wait, and everything she does that day, a bit less heavy.$$

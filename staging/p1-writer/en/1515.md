Seagate had a working 3.5-inch drive in its hands, and its own customers looked at the prototype and shrugged. A program manager cut his sales estimate, and executives cancelled the project. Every step in that chain was sensible.

[[story]]
Clayton Christensen tells this episode in *The Innovator's Dilemma* (1997). Five years earlier, Seagate had been the newcomer: its 5.25-inch drive of 1980 held no interest for minicomputer makers, so it sold to desktop personal computers instead. By the time of the prototype, those desktop makers were its customers.

The small drive was not Seagate's invention. A Scottish entrant, Rodime, had developed the 3.5-inch architecture in 1984, though sales were not yet significant. Around 1985 Seagate built a working prototype. Its marketing and executive side objected: in their view the market wanted higher-capacity drives at a lower cost per megabyte.

The prototypes were shown to existing customers, and the reviews came back lukewarm. The program manager lowered his sales estimates. Executives cancelled the program. The process had been reasonable from start to finish: ask the customers, update the forecast, decide on the investment. Christensen stresses that the leaders' failures were not down to passivity or fear of risk.

##The lukewarm reception said the drive did not fit Seagate's current customers, not that the drive had no buyers.## Portable and laptop computers were a new market. Conner Peripherals, a spin-off of Seagate and Miniscribe people, started shipping 3.5-inch drives to it in 1987.

Seagate began making 3.5-inch drives in 1988, after considerable effort from management. When it revived the project, it sold to desktop makers rather than to the emerging laptop market. The new drives went to the same buyers as its older ones and cut into Seagate's own existing sales.

Christensen sees the same pattern at other sizes. By 1995, entrants controlled 98 percent of a $130 million market for 1.8-inch drives.

Not everyone accepts this telling. Jill Lepore, one of his critics, argues that his cases do not fully support the thesis, and disk drives are a main example in her argument.

[[lessons]]
$$**Do not let today's buyers rule on tomorrow's product.** A cool reply from your current customers may only show that the idea does not suit them. A baker asks her regulars whether they would want a smaller, cheaper loaf, hears no, and drops the idea, yet that no describes what the regulars need and says little about people who have never walked into the shop. Before you abandon an idea after a cool reception, find one person who is not yet your customer, ask them the same question, and note where the two answers differ.$$

Worrying about graffiti while a whole subway system is close to collapse looked as pointless as scrubbing the decks of the Titanic. The man in charge, David Gunn, worried about it anyway, and he had a roller to prove it.

[[story]]
In 1992 New York City recorded 2,154 murders and 626,182 serious crimes. Within five years murders had dropped 64.3 percent to 770, and total crimes had fallen by almost half to 355,893.

In *The Tipping Point*, Malcolm Gladwell lists the usual explanations: better policing, the decline of the crack trade, a stronger economy, an ageing population. None explains how fast the drop came, he says. The most intriguing candidate for the "something else" is the Broken Windows theory of James Q. Wilson and George Kelling. If a window is broken and left unrepaired, people walking by conclude that no one cares or is in charge, more windows get broken, and disorder spreads to the street.

In the mid-1980s the Transit Authority hired Kelling as a consultant and brought in a new subway director, David Gunn, to oversee a multibillion-dollar rebuilding. Many people told Gunn not to bother with graffiti, but he insisted that it symbolised the collapse of the system and that without winning that battle the other reforms would not happen.

He started with the number seven train and made a rule: once a car was cleaned, it was never again allowed to be vandalised. At a yard on 135th Street in Harlem, kids spent three nights on a train, painting it white, drawing the outline and colouring it in. Gunn's crew waited until the mural was finished, then walked over with rollers and painted it out. The kids were in tears, and the crew just went up and down. The message was that you could spend three nights on this, but it would never see daylight.

##Fixing a small thing every single time tells a place what is expected.##

The graffiti cleanup ran from 1984 to 1990. Then William Bratton took over the transit police and went after fare-beating. An estimated 170,000 people a day entered without paying a $1.25 fare. Plainclothes officers were posted at turnstiles, a bus was fitted out as a rolling station house, and an arrest that took a day now took an hour. One in seven of those arrested had an outstanding warrant, and one in twenty carried a weapon.

A caution belongs here. Gladwell does not prove that this approach alone explains the drop; he lists the other causes himself and calls the theory only the most intriguing candidate. Causation is contested.

[[lessons]]
$$**Fix the visible small problem straight away, and every time.** A rule that is repeated tells everyone what a place expects of them. In a shared office kitchen where the first dirty mug is washed the same day, mugs tend to pile up less than in one that gets cleaned once a month. You can start without any grand plan by stopping one small thing from spreading, and you will notice the first moment the rule slips, which is when it is easiest to restore. A kitchen, a folder or a meeting agenda can all work the same way.$$

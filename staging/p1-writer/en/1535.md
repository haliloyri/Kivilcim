Shortly before midnight, a small group in a living room took the zippers and belt buckles off their clothes and waited for a flying saucer. Some of the people in that room were not believers. They were researchers, taking notes.

[[story]]
Matthew Syed goes back to that winter night in *Black Box Thinking* (2015). The psychologist Leon Festinger and his colleagues had joined a small group that expected a flood to end the world on 21 December 1954. Its leader, a woman, said she received messages from higher beings, and the believers were to be taken off in a flying saucer before the flood.

The preparation was real. Members had quit their jobs and given away their belongings, and they had removed anything metal from their clothes, since they expected to board a flying saucer. They gathered in the leader's home to wait for midnight. The clock passed twelve. No saucer came and no flood followed.

In the early hours the leader announced a new message: the group's faith had spread so much light that the world had been spared. The group had kept reporters away until then. Now it began phoning newspapers and inviting the public in. The failed date had made them more committed, and no amount of waiting had shaken them.

The observers had a front-row seat. From it, Festinger built the theory of cognitive dissonance. When a belief collides with the facts, a person feels tension and relieves it in one of two ways, by dropping the belief or by reading the facts differently. The more it cost to hold the belief, the easier the second route becomes, especially when other believers are around to share it.

Syed says the same reflex shows up well beyond cults, and he uses it to explain why people and organisations struggle to learn from their failures. Festinger's method has been criticised since, but the outline of the episode, a date that passed and a belief that grew stronger, shows the pattern the book describes.

[[lessons]]
$$**Decide in advance what would prove you wrong.** A founder who has spent two years on a product can write on a sheet of paper, before launch, which sales figure at three months would mean it is time to change course. When the bad news arrives, she reads the sheet instead of arguing with the number. The more you have already paid for a decision, the easier it becomes to bend the evidence, so the test has to be set while the decision is still cheap to question. That way you are comparing the result with a line you drew when you were calm, and you can stop or change course without feeling you have lost an argument with yourself.$$

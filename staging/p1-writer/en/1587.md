[[open]]
A phone held an arm's length from your face at midnight looks like a screen to you. To one group of cells in your eye it looks like a very bright sky.

[[story]]
Those cells are Satchin Panda's territory. He has led a lab at the Salk Institute in San Diego since 2004, and his group helped identify melanopsin, a pigment sensitive to blue light that sits in special cells of the eye.

These cells do not make pictures. They tell the brain's master clock how bright the surroundings are. A 2003 paper showed that blind mice still adjusted their clock to light through melanopsin, and book summaries add that the pigment works in people who are blind or visually impaired. The eye, it turns out, has two jobs: one is to see, the other to set the time.

Researchers call a cue like this a zeitgeber, German for time giver. Panda's team reported on melanopsin in Science in 2002, 2003 and 2005. The first paper dealt with melanopsin's role in shifting the clock, the second with blind mice, the third with the signalling pathway. A 2019 paper described how these cells vary in humans, and the work is cited in light-therapy research on insomnia, ADHD and migraine.

##Light is the strongest time cue the body clock has.## According to summaries of the book, blue light at night suppresses melatonin and disturbs sleep. The cells in the eye cannot tell a film from an email. They only report how bright and how blue it is.

Seen this way, the evening screen is a time signal sent at the wrong hour. A phone held close to the face in a dark bedroom delivers the bright, blue-tinted light these cells respond to, at the very hour when the master clock should be hearing that the day is over. Dimmer light at night and daylight in the morning, through an opened curtain or on a short walk, help the rhythm line up again.

[[lessons]]
$$**Choose the light you look at in the last hour before bed.** Someone who answers email on a bright tablet in bed and someone who reads by a low lamp send different messages to their body clock. You can pick one light in your evening to turn down this week and step outside for daylight with your first coffee. Then watch how you sleep for a few days, remembering that light is only one of several things that shape sleep. If poor sleep goes on for weeks, talk to a doctor.$$

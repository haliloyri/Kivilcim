A signal from the eye reaches the thalamus and splits in two. One short branch runs straight to the amygdala, while the long road leads on to the thinking brain. Daniel Goleman built a new idea of intelligence on the gap between those two speeds.

[[story]]
Before he wrote the book, Daniel Goleman was a science reporter at The New York Times, covering research on the brain and behaviour. In *Emotional Intelligence* he draws on Joseph LeDoux's work on the amygdala, the brain's emotional centre, and he treats emotion as a system that works alongside reason, not against it.

The mechanism he describes goes like this. A signal from the senses arrives first at the thalamus. From there one branch runs directly to the amygdala, while the main road continues to the neocortex, the thinking brain. Both routes start together, but one of them is much shorter.

That shortcut has a consequence. The amygdala can set off a reaction before the thinking brain has fully taken in what is happening. The body may already be getting ready while the mind has not yet named the event, which is why a person sometimes finds they have said or done something they later regret.

Goleman calls such a takeover an emotional hijacking. Anger or fear grabs the wheel for a moment, and the thinking brain only announces itself afterwards. He presents the short route as a fact about how brains are built, not as a flaw in any one person.

The book's second claim touches the argument about intelligence. Goleman reports that IQ accounts for at most about a fifth of what shapes success in life, and that the rest depends on other forces. Among them is the ability to handle one's own feelings and relationships.

He uses the figure to open a question: if two people with the same IQ end up in very different places, what explains the difference? His answer looks beyond solving problems, to staying steady under pressure and working well with others.

Putting the two ideas together, he builds the concept of emotional intelligence: knowing yourself, managing yourself, empathy and social skill. He offers these as skills that can be learned, not as a fixed talent.

[[lessons]]
$$**When a strong reaction arrives, give the thinking brain a moment before you speak.** A head chef about to shout at a server who wrote the order wrong might step away from the counter and drink a glass of water first. In that short gap he can notice that the reaction started before his thinking did, and that lets him choose the words he will say. Your anger is not suppressed this way, only held for a few seconds before it leaves your mouth. Next time you can make that pause a little earlier, and notice what it costs you.$$

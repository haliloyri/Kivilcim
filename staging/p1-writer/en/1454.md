Perspective, humility, humor, acceptance. Forgiveness, gratitude, compassion, generosity. Eight words, set out in two rows of four, are what two of the world's best-known religious leaders offered when asked what a joyful life stands on after a week of talking.

[[story]]
*The Book of Joy* (2016) records a week of conversation between the Dalai Lama and Archbishop Desmond Tutu, moderated by Douglas Abrams. Its third and last part is called the eight pillars of joy. The book sorts them into two groups.

They come after the obstacles. The book's second part walks through what gets in joy's way: fear, stress, anxiety, anger, frustration, sadness, grief, despair, loneliness, envy, illness and the fear of death. The pillars are the answer to that list, which is why the book does not present them as ornaments for a good mood.

Four of them belong to the mind: perspective, humility, humor and acceptance. Four belong to the heart: forgiveness, gratitude, compassion and generosity. One reviewer notes that the Dalai Lama tends to favor proactive mental training, while Tutu's advice leans toward responding once hurt has already happened. Between them, the pillars cover both the habits you build in advance and the ways you recover.

Perspective gets its clearest example in the Dalai Lama's own talk about exile. When he looks only at the difficulties in his own country, he worries. When he widens the view to include other people's suffering, his own suffering lessens.

##Joy, the book suggests, comes from not focusing too much on yourself.## Time's review of the book calls this the central takeaway, and the writers behind it treat compassion and generosity as the most decisive of the eight. Even the mind pillars point away from the self: perspective and humility both widen the view past your own worries.

The tone of the week matches that. The two men teased each other like old friends, and a summary describes the Dalai Lama poking Tutu's belly while Tutu called him mischievous. Tutu offers one line that sums up the spirit of the project. You are made for perfection, he says, but you are not yet perfect; you are a masterpiece in the making.

[[lessons]]
$$**Pick one pillar for the week instead of trying to hold all eight.** Someone who finds Sunday evenings heavy might choose gratitude, and end each day by telling a flatmate one thing that went well. The choice is small enough to keep and specific enough to notice, and it leaves seven pillars for another week. At the end of the week you will know whether that pillar changed how your days felt, and you can swap it for another from the mind or the heart.$$

[[story]]
Donella Meadows opens *Thinking in Systems* with a demonstration she used early on when teaching. She brings out a Slinky, the long, loose spring that can pour from hand to hand or walk itself down the stairs. She perches it on one upturned palm. With the fingers of her other hand she grasps it from the top, partway down its coils.

Then she pulls the bottom hand away. The lower end of the Slinky drops, bounces back up and yo-yos, hanging from her fingers. She asks the class what made it bounce like that.

"Your hand," they say. "You took away your hand."

So she picks up the box the Slinky came in and holds it exactly the same way, on a flat palm, held from above by the other hand. She pulls the lower hand away. The box just hangs there.

##The answer lies within the Slinky itself.## In Meadows's words, the hands only suppress or release behavior that is already latent in the structure of the spring. She calls this a central insight of systems theory, and she builds the rest of the book on it.

She defines a system as a set of things, people, cells or molecules, connected in such a way that they produce their own pattern of behavior over time. The system, she writes, to a large extent causes its own behavior. An outside event may unleash that behavior, but the same event applied to a different system is likely to produce a different result. With a Slinky this is easy to accept. With people, companies or economies, she admits, it can sound like heresy.

Her examples make the point uncomfortable. Political leaders don't cause recessions or booms, because ups and downs are built into the structure of the market economy. Competitors rarely cause a company to lose market share; the losing company creates its losses at least in part through its own policies. And the flu virus does not attack you. You set up the conditions for it to flourish within you.

[[lessons]]
$$**When a problem keeps coming back, look at how things are set up before blaming the latest trigger.** If the same trouble returns with different triggers each time, the thing producing it is probably the arrangement itself rather than any one person or event. A family that argues every Sunday evening might notice the fights always start when everyone tries to plan the week at once, and move the planning to a calmer moment. Looking at structure first lets you change the setting instead of hunting for a culprit, which makes the same problem less likely to return next week.$$

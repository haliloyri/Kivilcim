Three words. That is how many emotions, on average, people could name as they were actually feeling them when a research team surveyed about 7,000 of them over five years. The words were happiness, sadness and anger. A whole book was written to give the rest of the list a name.

[[story]]
Brené Brown's team ran those surveys, and Time's report of the research puts the average at three. Three, for everything a person feels in a day: the hot flush in a meeting, the flat evening, the stomach drop at a message. People had plenty of feelings. They had very few words for them.

Keep the scope in view. Three is an average of what people could identify as they were feeling it, so some named more and some fewer. It says nothing about what they could define later with a dictionary open. The gap is between feeling something and having the word ready in that moment.

Her book *Atlas of the Heart* (2021) is the answer, and it is built like a map. Its subtitle says what it is for: mapping meaningful connection and the language of human experience. It charts 87 emotions and experiences, grouped into 13 land masses, and each one is presented as a destination you can travel to and look around.
Look at two of the place names. "Places We Go When Things Are Uncertain or Too Much." "Places We Go When We Compare." They name situations, not single feelings, so a reader can begin with where they are standing and find the word nearby. In the comparison section the book sets admiration, envy, jealousy, schadenfreude and freudenfreude side by side, the way a road atlas puts neighbouring towns on one page.

The neighbours matter because close words are easy to blur. Envy and jealousy are the book's own pair. ##Envy is wanting something another person has, and jealousy is fearing the loss of a relationship you already have.## Anyone who says "I'm jealous" when they mean the first has the wrong word for what to do next.

What the map asks of a reader is a better word than the nearest of three. The book came out in November 2021 and later became a five-episode series on HBO Max.

[[lessons]]
$$**Look for a second, more exact word before you act on the first.** A cook who snaps "I'm angry" at the end of a bad shift has used the biggest word he had. If he stops and tries two or three others, one may fit better and point to a different next step, such as asking for help with the rota instead of banging a pan. You get a way to slow the first reaction down by a few seconds, and a sentence a colleague can actually answer. The habit costs a pencil and a minute, and it works on small irritations as well as large ones.$$

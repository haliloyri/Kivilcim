[[open]]
Seven puzzle pieces lie on a table beside a few magazines, and the experimenter walks out of the room. What the students do with the next few minutes is the whole experiment, and it was run on three separate days.

[[story]]
Daniel Pink's *Drive* (2009) builds part of its case against paying people for everything on a single study by the psychologist Edward Deci, published in 1971. The students in it already liked solving a puzzle made of Soma pieces, and Deci wanted to know what money would do to that liking.

The study ran for three days. One group was never paid. The other group worked unpaid on the first day, was paid on the second and went unpaid again on the third. In the middle of each session the experimenter left the room, and the students could do whatever they liked: keep working on the puzzle, or pick up the magazines on the table, Time and The New Yorker among them. Deci watched through a one-way window and measured how long each person kept playing with the puzzle once the room was empty.

On the second day the paid group spent more of the break on the puzzle than the others. ##On the third day, with the pay gone, they spent significantly less than the group that had never been paid.## In Pink's reading, a payment had changed what the puzzle was for.

Pink gathers other results that point the same way. In the candle problem, people offered money for a fast solution did much worse than people offered nothing. And children who were rewarded for drawing later refused to draw without a reward, while children who got none kept drawing for fun.

The puzzle study has critics. In 1996 Eisenberger and Cameron argued that the drop on the third day might be a reaction to having the reward taken away, and that free time is a weak measure compared with what people say about their own interest in a task.

Pink's practical line is that employers should pay enough to take money off the table. According to the book, higher pay improved performance only on basic mechanical tasks, and on tasks that call for thinking, decisions or creativity it led to lower performance.

[[lessons]]
$$**Look at what a reward turns the task into.** A teenager who loves drawing starts selling sketches to classmates for pocket money, and stops drawing on the day the buyers lose interest. What used to be a pleasure has become a job with a price, and the drawing ends with the payment. Before you attach a prize or a fee to something people already enjoy, ask whether the activity is still worth doing without it, because you may be changing what it means to them, and that is a question one short conversation can answer.$$

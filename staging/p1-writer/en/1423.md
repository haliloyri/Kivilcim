There is a walking practice with a loop and no finish line. You go up and down a short path, or round a circle, and when you reach the end you simply turn and go back. It sounds like a joke about going nowhere, and the author who describes it means every word.

[[story]]
In *Wherever You Go, There You Are* (1994), Jon Kabat-Zinn teaches walking meditation by taking away the one thing a walk usually has: a destination. You are not walking to get anywhere. You pace a path back and forth or circle a loop, and once the goal is gone, nothing is left to do except lift a foot and put it down.

Attention rests on the whole movement of the foot, or on its separate parts if you prefer, and it can be paired with the breath. When you notice yourself rushing, you slow down. Slowed down, a single step comes apart into heel, sole and the shift of weight from one leg to the other, and at the end of the path you turn and the same few metres begin again. That is the entire instruction, and it asks nothing about how well you walk or how far.

The book has no score for it. If you wonder whether you are doing it right, the advice is to leave that question unanswered and come back to the present moment, which in walking means the foot again.

Kabat-Zinn's point is that having no destination makes it easier to be where you are. On an ordinary walk the body is on the pavement while the mind has already sat down at the meeting it is heading for. The path takes that habit away, because there is no next stop for the mind to jump to.

##In the chapter "This Is It," meditation means recognizing where you already are, with nothing to fix first.##

Off the path, the practice continues. Kabat-Zinn argues that mindfulness can be built without formal meditation, in the shower or over a meal, by doing one thing at a time. Water running or a fork rising are the same practice in another place.

[[lessons]]
$$**Start from the place you are standing.** A person waiting in a bakery queue usually treats those minutes as lost and reaches for a phone, yet standing there and hearing your own breath is something you can actually do. When you stop treating where you are as a stop on the way to somewhere better, you notice what is available in it. The wait becomes a small stretch of time that belongs to you, and you can begin it right there, between the counter and the door, without preparing anything or waiting for a better moment.$$

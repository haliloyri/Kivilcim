[[story]]
The Greek poet Simonides, who lived in the fifth century B.C., stands at the centre of a story that Joshua Foer retells in *Moonwalking with Einstein* (2011). Simonides is at a banquet when a messenger calls him out. While he is outside, the roof falls in.

The guests inside are crushed beyond recognition. Relatives come, and nobody can say whose body is whose. Simonides can. ##He recalls where each guest had been sitting minutes before, and the bodies are identified by their seats.## He is walking back through a room that no longer exists, one chair at a time.

Tradition counts that night as the discovery of the memory palace. The method uses spatial memory, our grip on places. Pick a place you know well, set each thing you want to remember at a spot along a route through it, and recall the list later by walking that route in your mind. A route has an order, so the items come back in the order you left them. The house you grew up in or the walk to work will do, because you already know both by heart.
Whether it happened this way is another matter. Reviews of the book call the story possibly accurate and possibly mythological. The earliest surviving description of the technique is in a Latin textbook, the *Rhetorica ad Herennium*, from about 86 to 82 BC, roughly four centuries after Simonides. In between, the method was clearly in use.

Foer did not just report on it. He used the method in the year of training that took him from reporter to winner of the 2006 USA Memory Championship. A legend about a collapsed hall became a practical tool for a man who had to learn a long list in a short time.

[[lessons]]
$$**Park each thing you must remember in a place you know well, then walk past it.** A waiter learning a new menu pictures the first dish on the bottom step of the staircase at home, the next one a few steps up, and so on to the landing. On the shift he climbs those steps in his mind and reads the dishes off in order. You get a way to hold a list in order without writing it, and the staircase is something you already own, so there is nothing new to carry. If he forgets a dish, he knows which step to look at.$$

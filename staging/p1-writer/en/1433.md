[[story]]
In *Measure What Matters*, in a chapter titled "Stretch: The Google Chrome Story," John Doerr uses Google's Chrome browser to show what a stretch goal looks like in practice. Google's aspirational OKRs, he says, aim at roughly 60 to 70 percent attainment. He backs the idea with the goal-setting researcher Edwin Locke, who found that hard goals produce more than easy ones, and specific hard goals more than vague ones.

Chrome started from zero. In 2008, Sundar Pichai's key result for it was 20 million seven-day active users. Doerr writes that he thought there was no way they would get there. Pichai has a passage of his own in the chapter about pushing to the limit of the team's ability and beyond.

For 2009 the goal went up to 50 million, two and a half times the year before. The team ended the year at 38 million, about 12 million short. By Google's measure that is 76 percent, above the 60 to 70 percent range.

Then came the target for 2010. Pichai proposed 100 million and backed it with plans on advertising, faster code and distribution deals. Larry Page said it was not enough, since it covered only a small slice of the world's internet users. They settled on 111 million.

##Chrome reached 111 million seven-day active users in 2010.## That was close to three times the number it had ended 2009 with. The 2009 miss had not lowered the next target, and the team went on to beat a number that had looked out of reach.

In Doerr's account, then, a miss is read as data for setting the next number rather than as a verdict on the team.

[[lessons]]
$$**Set one target you might miss.** Targets you will certainly hit show how fast you are comfortable going, not how far you could go. A small shop owner who sets next month's order count slightly above the level she reaches easily may end the month under target and still above her best month. Reading a miss as a measurement lets you set the next number with evidence in hand instead of a bruised ego, and it keeps you asking for more than you already know you can do.$$

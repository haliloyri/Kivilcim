[[open]]
One airline looked at its competitors and decided that its real competition was a car on the highway. It dropped the meals and the seat choice, kept a short list of things drivers care about, and ended up with a line on a chart that looked like nobody else's.

[[story]]
W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne's *Blue Ocean Strategy* (2005) gives the reader a chart before it gives an answer. Along the bottom run the factors an industry competes on and invests in. Up the side runs how much buyers receive on each one. The authors call it the strategy canvas.

Every company draws a line across it. Lay the lines of rivals on top of each other, and the canvas shows how similar their strategies look to buyers. A bundle of near-identical lines is what a red ocean looks like from above.

Airlines show it well. The rivals invested across many factors at once. Southwest dropped or cut some of them, among them meals, seat choice and hub connectivity, and put its weight on three: friendly service, speed and frequent point-to-point departures, all at a low price.

By giving up hub connectivity, Southwest could offer frequent departures on the limited set of routes it flew. Frequent departures were a factor the other airlines had not pushed. Its line went high where theirs was flat and low where theirs was high.

Then the authors added a line for something that is not an airline. They drew car travel on the same chart as a point of comparison. Southwest attracted car drivers by making flying more like driving: fast, cheap and available when you want it. One summary of the book cites a tagline that fits the picture: the speed of the plane at the price of the car, whenever you need it.

The authors' point is about the chart itself. Once the lines were drawn, it was clear which of Southwest's factors the other airlines had never moved.

[[lessons]]
$$**Draw your offer next to your rivals' before you decide how to stand out.** A neighborhood café can list what the other cafés on its street offer, from seating to pastries to speed, and mark each one high or low. If its own marks follow the same shape as everyone else's, it is competing on the same terms as the street. Make the chart on a single sheet and find the one factor where your line could sit clearly apart, instead of guessing from memory.$$

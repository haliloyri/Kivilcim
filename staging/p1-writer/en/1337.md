A man sits with his arms crossed, in a room that happens to be cold. Is he defensive, or just chilly? Joe Navarro's warning is that you cannot answer until you know one other thing about him.

[[story]]
Navarro, in *What Every BODY Is Saying* (2008), starts with a caution. Reading body language is not memorizing a dictionary of signs. A rule like "crossed arms mean defensive" breaks down fast, because the person may be cold, or may simply find that posture comfortable.

His starting point is a person's baseline: how this individual looks when relaxed. How do they sit, how do they talk, what do their hands do? Without that, any reading of a gesture is a guess. If you spend a while with someone, you learn their relaxed self, and what matters is the departure from it.

##A gesture only means something against the background of that person's normal.##

An example makes it concrete. If someone always speaks quickly and slows down in a meeting, that is a signal. If someone already speaks slowly, the same pace tells you nothing. The same behavior carries two meanings in two people.

Stress does not look alike in everybody either. One person goes still when stressed, another speeds up. One looks at you more, another looks away. So Navarro suggests asking "what is a stress signal for this person?" instead of "what is a stress signal?"

The book is built around comfort and discomfort. A comfortable person tends to open up, a person who is uneasy closes up, but the yardstick is still that person's own normal. Navarro also distrusts any single signal. If the arms are crossed, the body leans away, the feet point at the door and the jaw is tight, that is a cluster, and a cluster says something.

The cost is time. You need to watch, listen and not hurry. The picture of the brain as split between feeling and reason is a shortcut too, and worth keeping in mind.

[[lessons]]
$$**Learn what normal looks like before you read a change.** A teammate who is always quiet and a teammate who suddenly goes quiet should not be read the same way. Something may have happened to the second one, while for the first it is simply how they are. If you get to know the everyday version first, you can tell whether what you see is really a difference. It also keeps you from pinning a label on someone because of a single gesture.$$

&&Think of someone close to you: what does their relaxed self look like, and what exactly changes when they are under strain?&&

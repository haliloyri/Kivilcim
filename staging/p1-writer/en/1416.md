For eight years a pastor had led his church well, and then three decisions in a short stretch cost him something he had not known he was spending. The decisions were not wrong in themselves. The way he made them was.

[[story]]
John Maxwell tells this one on himself in *The 21 Irrefutable Laws of Leadership* (1998), in the chapter on the Law of Solid Ground. For eight years he had been the senior pastor of Skyline Church, and the church had done well under him. People knew how he decided things. They knew his usual process.

Then he made three major decisions without it. He fired someone. He cancelled a service. He changed part of the Christmas show. Each one, on its own, might have been defended. Together they showed his people something they had not seen from him before.

Trust dropped. Maxwell had spent credit he did not realize he was spending, and the congregation noticed before he did. He apologized in public and asked their forgiveness. By his account, that is how he won their trust back.

The law behind it is plain: trust is the foundation of leadership. "Character makes trust possible," Maxwell writes. "And trust makes leadership possible." A leader who loses trust, he says, cannot keep influencing people.

He compares trust to the change in your pocket. Every good decision adds a coin, and every mistake takes one out. An honest apology can put a few coins back, which is what his own apology did at Skyline.

Character, in his telling, shows people three things: whether you are consistent, how much potential you have and whether you deserve respect. He names three traits to build it: integrity, which means honesty even when it hurts, authenticity, which means being yourself, and discipline, which means doing the right thing day after day.

Not every mistake costs the same. Mistakes of ability are easier to recover from than mistakes of character, such as lying about the status of a project. You can see how much trust you hold by how much people tell you, bad news included.

Maxwell also reaches for a larger case. The public once backed the war in Vietnam despite heavy casualties, he notes, but support eroded as it dragged on and leaders such as McNamara and Johnson misled the public. McNamara resigned, and Johnson did not run again.

[[lessons]]
$$**Tell people before you change how you decide.** A manager who always posts the weekly schedule on Friday and one week emails a new one on Monday has moved nothing important, yet the team feels it. People rely on your usual way of deciding, so skipping it spends something they were counting on, even when the new choice is a good one. Name the change out loud and say why before you act, ideally to the people it touches first, and if you slip anyway, a plain apology costs less than letting the silence stand.$$

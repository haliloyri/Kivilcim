A fireman arrives at a burning house. Thich Nhat Hanh says he should pour water on the blaze first and not waste time looking for whoever set the fire. When we are angry, he points out, we usually do the opposite.

[[story]]
In *Peace Is Every Step* (1991), Thich Nhat Hanh describes what most of us do when anger arrives. We turn toward the person: his rudeness, his dishonesty, his cruelty. The more we think about him, listen to him or look at him, the more the anger flares.

His image for the better order is the fireman. The man may really be dishonest or hateful, or only imagined or exaggerated that way, but Hanh says the root of the problem is the anger itself. So the practice is to come back to yourself.

Breathing in, you know that you are angry. Breathing out, you know that you must put all your energy into caring for your anger. While it lasts you avoid thinking about the other person and refrain from doing or saying anything, which spares you damage you might regret later.

Mindfulness, in this practice, is not a judge. He compares it to an older sister who looks after her younger sister in an affectionate and caring way. It does not suppress the anger or drive it out, and it stays beside it the way you stay beside someone who needs company.

He is cautious about the usual release. Some people go into a room, lock the door and punch a pillow, and he adds that expressing anger can mean rehearsing it and making it stronger. ##The pillow may help for a while, because you spend your energy and feel better once you are exhausted, but the roots of the anger are still there.## If the seeds are watered again, the anger is reborn and the pillow gets pounded again.

For the first minutes he suggests going outside, where the fresh air and green trees help. You walk and say to yourself that anger is here, that this feeling will pass, and that you are strong enough to take care of it. All your attention goes to the soles of your feet touching the earth, and you wait until you are calm enough to look straight at the anger. Until then, you simply walk.

After a while the anger subsides and you feel stronger. Only then, he says, can you begin to observe it directly and try to understand it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Handle the anger before you handle the person.** A shop assistant who has just been told off by a customer wants to explain himself at once, and every sentence he rehearses in his head makes his voice a little tighter, so the customer's tone gets answered with his own. If he steps into the stockroom, breathes and gives the anger his whole attention, he can decide afterwards whether the conversation needs to happen at all, and in what words. The pause excuses nothing, and it leaves you free to say what you mean once you have calmed down.$$

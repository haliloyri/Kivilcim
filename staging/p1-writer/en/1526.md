Binder paper, pencils and a pile of unopened bird books lay on the table. The boy behind them was ten, close to tears, and the report they were meant for was due the next day. He had been given three months.

[[story]]
The boy was Anne Lamott's brother, and the scene gave her book *Bird by Bird* (1994) its title. Three months earlier he had been assigned a report on birds. He had not started it, and now the whole job sat in front of him as one block, too large for him to make a single move.

The books he needed were within reach, and he had not opened one. Binder paper lay spread across the table beside a handful of pencils. He was close to tears.

Their father came over and sat beside him. He put an arm around the boy's shoulder and said:

> Bird by bird, buddy. Just take it bird by bird.

The report was about birds, so the sentence handed the job a unit of measure: not the whole report, only the next bird. Lamott tells this story to students who feel buried by a project.

In her advice on short assignments, which she calls the first useful concept she has about writing, she compares writing a whole novel at once to trying to scale a glacier. Her recommendation is to take a big job little by little, in short bites.

She backs it with a line from E. L. Doctorow. Writing a novel, he said, is like driving a car at night. ##You can see only as far as your headlights, but you can make the whole trip that way.## The road ahead can stay dark as long as the next stretch is lit.

On her own desk sits a one-inch picture frame, small on purpose. It reminds her that she only has to write down as much as she can see through it, and her example is a main character: she describes her once, the first time she appears, in one paragraph. Whatever falls outside the frame can wait for another day.

[[lessons]]
$$**Choose one piece you can finish today.** Someone who inherits a cluttered garage and tries to sort every shelf, box and tool in a single weekend usually ends it with the door still blocked and the same piles in the same corners. If they pick one shelf and clear only that, the job shrinks to something they can actually see the end of. Once that shelf is done, the next one comes into view, and the garage stops looking like one impossible task and starts looking like a row of small ones you can close one by one.$$

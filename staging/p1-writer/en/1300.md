A chimpanzee choosing answers at random would have beaten the people in one famous set of quiz results. The quiz was not about maths or history. It asked about the world as it is today, and the people who got it wrong made the same kind of mistake.

[[story]]
Hans Rosling and the Gapminder team asked people simple multiple-choice questions about the state of the world. Every question had three answers, A, B or C. A chimpanzee picking at random would therefore be right about a third of the time, and in *Factfulness* Rosling uses that chimpanzee as his yardstick. Two of the questions were part of a longer list.

One question was how many of the world's one-year-olds have been vaccinated against some disease: 20, 50 or 80 percent. The answer is 80 percent. On average, only about 13 percent of the people asked got it right. Close to nine in ten picked one of the two lower figures.

Another was about extreme poverty. Over the last twenty years, had the share of the world's population living in extreme poverty almost doubled, stayed about the same, or almost halved? It had almost halved. On average, about 7 percent chose that answer, so more than nine in ten missed it.

Set those next to the chimpanzee's 33 percent. People scored 13 percent on the vaccination question and 7 percent on the poverty one. These are the averages Rosling reports, and they vary by country and group.

##The chimpanzees would have beaten the people.##

The chimpanzee is there to show that the pattern was no accident of chance. The errors were not scattered across A, B and C the way a chimp's guesses would be. They leaned the same way, toward the gloomier answer. Rosling read this as a problem with the picture people carry in their heads, not with their intelligence. On both of these measures the world had been getting better, and most people had not noticed.

[[lessons]]
$$**Check which way your wrong guesses lean.** When mistakes keep pointing in one direction, the cause is usually a bias, and a bias can be corrected by looking up the figure. A shop owner who believes most customers are unhappy with her delivery times may be remembering the few angry emails far better than the many orders that arrived without a word. Counting the complaints against the orders gives her an actual figure to work with, and next time she can remind herself that her gut tends to lean gloomy and adjust for it before she reacts.$$

[[open]]
Susan Cain asked a student at Harvard Business School a simple question: was anyone on campus quieter than the rest? He thought about it and said he couldn't tell her. In a school that runs on talk, nobody seemed to need the answer.

[[story]]
Cain went to Harvard Business School for *Quiet* (2012), and she gave the place a nickname: the Spiritual Capital of Extroversion. Future leaders of business and government are trained there to act confidently and to decide with incomplete information. What she watched was a school that teaches that skill by making everyone talk.

In class, participation drives status and grades. Each incoming class runs the Subarctic Survival Situation, an exercise in which groups of students brainstorm how to survive a cold-weather crash with almost nothing to work with. Since participation is graded, staying silent in that exercise has a cost too.

Most students, as Cain describes it, come largely to build lasting personal networks. The days are group exercises. The evenings she calls an extreme sport, because the socializing does not stop when class ends.

##At a school built to train confident talkers, the student could not say whether anyone on campus was quieter.## As long as speaking up turned into status and grades, nobody had much reason to notice the person who stayed silent.

Cain does not stop at the campus. She argues that introverts tend to prefer working on their own, and that solitude can be a catalyst for new ideas. Group brainstorming, she adds, is less effective than most people assume. She calls the spread of group work the New Groupthink and says it should not dominate the school day.

The numbers she cites do not match the room. By the studies she uses, one third to one half of Americans are introverts. Their average IQ is the same as that of extroverts, and they tend to earn better grades in high school and college. Some financial executives told her that cautious, introverted leaders on Wall Street might have noticed warning signs before the economic meltdown, which is their view, not a measured result.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask for ideas in a form that does not reward speed.** A weekly planning call tends to follow whoever answers first, while the careful colleague works out a better plan an hour later and never says it. Offering a short written round before the discussion lets that plan reach the table. You hear what people think, not just how quickly they could say it, and the quick speaker loses nothing. Over a few weeks the same quiet colleague may start speaking up live, because the first words are already on the page.$$

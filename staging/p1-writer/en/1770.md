An ad in the Palo Alto Times offered fifteen dollars a day. Seventy-five men applied, and twenty-four ended up in a mock prison in a Stanford basement, where a coin toss decided who would carry the baton.

[[story]]
In *The Lucifer Effect*, Philip Zimbardo describes the experiment he ran in August 1971 in the basement of Jordan Hall at Stanford, funded by the US Office of Naval Research. The twenty-four men chosen were judged psychologically stable. Whether each became a guard or a prisoner was decided at random. Each cell, about seven feet by ten, was meant to hold three prisoners, and the study was planned for two weeks.

Guards were given khaki uniforms, wooden batons and mirrored sunglasses, and were told not to hurt anyone. On the first day the Palo Alto police arrested the prisoners at their homes without warning. At the mock jail they were fingerprinted, strip-searched and given numbers and smocks. The guards called them by number, not by name.

Around half past two on the second night the prisoners rebelled by pulling off their number tags and caps. The guards sprayed them with fire extinguishers and sent the leaders to solitary. By day three the abuse had grown: forced push-ups and a bucket for a toilet. Prisoner 8612, Douglas Korpi, left after about thirty-six hours, in distress.

On day four prisoner 819 broke down crying. On the fifth night Christina Maslach saw guards making prisoners wear bags over their heads and objected to Zimbardo. She made him see that he too had changed. He ended the study on day six.

Zimbardo reads this as the power of a situation over character, and in the second half of the book he looks for the same pattern at the Abu Ghraib prison in Iraq. Later critics, including Thibault Le Texier, argue that the guards were guided in how to behave and wanted to please the researchers, so the evidence is weaker than the story first suggested.

[[lessons]]
$$**Look at the role and rules people are given before you judge their character.** Zimbardo's guards were chosen by chance, and within days the uniform and the baton seemed to shape what they did. A shop supervisor who grows harsh may be working under a system that rewards speed and punishes any delay, and as long as that system stands, replacing the person changes little. Changing the rule first lets you test whether behavior follows, and it protects whoever takes the role next.$$

Dave Logan and his co-authors give stage four of workplace culture a rule of thumb: the bigger the foe, the more powerful the tribe. Teams at this stage feel strong, loyal and busy. The book also names a stage above it, and the force that drives that one is different.

[[story]]
At stage four the sentence changes from "I'm great" to "We're great." In *Tribal Leadership* (2008), Dave Logan, John King and Halee Fischer-Wright describe people who share values and knowledge and collaborate. What stage three kept to itself now goes on the table. Their relationships come in threes, each tied to the same values, where stage three had pairs. Leaders at this stage feel pulled by the group, and losing the tribe hurts a person's sense of self. The tribe itself is small, 20 to 150 people, and one that nears 150 splits in two.

Competition does not go away. It moves outside. The rival is another tribe, often a competing company, and the authors' rule says the bigger that foe, the more powerful the tribe. The group also has a way of dealing with people who do not fit. It rejects them, the way a body rejects a transplant that does not take.

Stage five says "Life is great." The mood there is innocent wonderment. People make things nobody has dreamed of, because it is possible. The book's example is the team that built the first Macintosh. Nothing in that description needs a rival. Gallup's write-up of the authors' work lists Genentech, Pixar and Apple among the places where it shows up, along with parts of IBM and General Electric.

##Stage five turns up about two percent of the time, and it does not last.## The teams that reach it often fall back to stage four to regroup before they try again. In the book's picture, stage four is the place a tribe returns to when it needs its footing back.

The practical advice for stage four is plain. Hold regular "oil changes", team conversations about what is working, what is not and how to fix it. And recruit people who share the tribe's values, so that fewer of them get rejected later. One summary lists the signs of a stage-four group moving up: its networks become far more varied, and its time starts to follow its values and its noble missions.

[[lessons]]
$$**Give a strong group something to make, not only someone to beat.** A kitchen crew bonded over beating the restaurant across the street will cook hard for as long as that restaurant stays open. The useful question for the crew is what it would put on the menu if the rival closed tomorrow. Asking it keeps the energy that a foe provides and points it at a dish, a service or a standard that the people in the room can own. You can ask it quietly, at the next team lunch, long before anything goes wrong.$$

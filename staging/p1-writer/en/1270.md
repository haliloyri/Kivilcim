On the screen, one part of a person's brain is blazing while another goes dark. The person in the scanner is only lying still, listening to a description of the worst thing that ever happened to them. The researchers watched the two regions part ways.

[[story]]
Bessel van der Kolk began his career as a psychiatrist at a Veterans Administration clinic in Boston. Many of his patients were Vietnam veterans, men who had left the war but kept reacting as if they were still in it. In *The Body Keeps the Score* he writes that people with trauma often respond to a reminder as though the event were happening now.

He wanted to see this, not only describe it. With colleagues, he put people with traumatic histories into a brain scanner and let them listen to an account of their own experience. Two regions stood out.

The amygdala, the part of the brain that sounds the alarm when danger appears, became highly active. When that alarm goes off the heart speeds up, the muscles tighten and the body gets ready to run or fight. At the same moment, Broca's area went quiet. It is one of the areas that help turn an experience into spoken words, and a person with damage there can know exactly what they mean and still fail to build the sentence. ##The alarm was loud while the speech area was silent.##

What the volunteers heard was only a description, yet the alarm system treated it as a present threat. Van der Kolk concludes that in such moments the body reacts first, and the person may be unable to put the experience into words. That may explain why someone with a trauma history says "I can't explain it." For him the scan shows the difference between remembering an event and reliving it. One is telling about the past, the other is falling back into it.

That is why he argues that talk-based treatment may not be enough by itself, and that approaches which speak to the body directly belong beside it. The book's title comes from that idea: the body goes on keeping the score.

[[lessons]]
$$**Start with the body, then the explanation.** In the middle of a strong reaction a person often cannot say why, because the speech area may have gone quiet. A friend who comes back shaking from a harsh phone call will not get far if you press "What happened? Tell me." Offering a chair, asking them to put their feet flat on the floor and breathe out slowly gives the words a chance to return. The same goes for you when your own body reacts before your head can explain.$$

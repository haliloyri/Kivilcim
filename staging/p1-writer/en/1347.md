Barry Schwartz now expects a pair of jeans to fit as though it had been made for him. Nothing about his body or his budget changed to produce that standard. What changed was the number of cuts on the shelf, and a cheap bottle of wine on the Oregon coast helped him see why.

[[story]]
In *The Paradox of Choice* (2004), Schwartz describes a vacation in a tiny seaside town on the Oregon coast. He went into the small local grocery store to buy what he needed for dinner. When he reached the wine, there were about a dozen options.

The bottle he chose wasn't very good. He was satisfied anyway, because he hadn't expected to find anything very good in a store like that. The wine met the expectation the shelf had set.

Then he moves the scene. Had he been shopping in a store that offered hundreds or even thousands of wines, his expectations would have been far higher. ##Walking out of that store with a bottle of the same quality, he writes, he would have been sorely disappointed.## The wine is identical in both versions; only the shelf behind it is different.

He applies the same logic to the jeans that open the book. When jeans came in one variety, he was satisfied with the fit, whatever it was. Faced with relaxed fit, easy fit, slim fit, tapered leg and boot cut, his standards rose. In his words, the proliferation of options seems to lead, inexorably, to the raising of expectations.

He sees it in health too. Americans live longer and better than ever, yet the medical historian Roy Porter points to unparalleled anxiety about health in this age of longevity. People expect to live longer still, and to do it without any loss of capacity, so the gains don't bring the satisfaction you might predict.

Schwartz takes the argument to a darker place. With limitless choice, he suggests, people can get better results and still feel worse, and when a result disappoints they often blame themselves. By some estimates depression was about ten times as likely in 2000 as in 1900. Among the Amish of Lancaster County, Pennsylvania, a close community with few life choices, the rate of depression is less than 20 percent of the national rate. He reads this as a story about expectations, and presents it as his interpretation rather than a proven cause.

His practical advice starts from the same point. How we judge an experience depends heavily on what we compared it with beforehand. To keep expectations in check he suggests considering fewer options, settling for good enough rather than the best, and leaving room for serendipity. The little grocery on the coast had done all of that for him without being asked.

[[lessons]]
$$**Notice what you expected before you judge what you got.** Satisfaction depends on the gap between a result and the expectation behind it, and a wide range of options quietly raises that expectation before you have tried anything. A person who orders from a short café menu tends to taste the sandwich, while someone choosing from a long one often spends the first bite thinking about the dishes they skipped. Narrowing the field on purpose gives an ordinary good result a fair chance to feel good, and it lets you trace a disappointment back to where it actually started.$$

A futsal ball is smaller and heavier than a soccer ball, and on a cramped indoor court it is almost always at somebody's feet. In São Paulo, an eleven-year-old called Brunio keeps losing it on one trick, a move called the elastico.

[[story]]
Daniel Coyle's *The Talent Code* (2009) grew out of a trip he began in December 2006, to nine small places where skill seemed to grow. A tennis court in Moscow. A vocal studio in Dallas. A baseball island in the Caribbean. And a soccer field in São Paulo, where Brunio could not get the elastico right.

Brunio fails, then slows the move down. He breaks it into parts and tries each part again until something clicks. Coyle saw the same rhythm everywhere he went: players moved fluently and fast, went slow and halting, then picked up speed again.

In Dallas, Jennie, twenty-four, was working on the chorus of "Running Out of Time". She stopped at every miss and went back to the measure that had gone wrong. On the sixth try she got it.

##Coyle calls this deep practice, and the halting parts are the point.## You struggle in targeted ways at the edge of what you can do, in a sweet spot where reach just exceeds grasp. Mistakes belong to it, because paying attention to the error is part of how a skill circuit learns to fire cleanly. His paradox is that the struggle makes you smarter.

The biology he points to is myelin, the fatty insulation around neurons. Like the insulation on a wire, more of it lets a signal travel faster and more precisely. It builds up when one specific circuit fires again and again, and it is living tissue, not fixed at birth. Hence his line that practice makes myelin, and myelin makes perfect.

Brazil's ball makes the point. The futsal ball is small and heavy and the court is tight. In a Liverpool University study that Coyle cites, players touched the ball about six times more per minute than players of outdoor soccer. Juninho did not play on a full-size grass pitch until he was fourteen. Dr. Emilio Miranda sums it up: "No time plus no space equals better skills."

Coyle's claim is a measured one. Talent looks predestined, he writes, but we have a good deal of control over which skills we develop. It is his reading of nine small places, and it should be held as that.

[[lessons]]
$$**Slow down where you stumble.** Skill gets built in the stretch that keeps stopping you, not in the part that already runs smoothly. A cook who keeps scorching the sauce at the same step gains little by remaking the whole recipe, but turning down the heat, repeating only that step and stopping the moment it goes wrong shows exactly what needs fixing. Next time you practise anything, note where you stall and start the session there, and save the parts that already flow for the end.$$

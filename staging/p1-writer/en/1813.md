[[story]]
The company 3M had an unusual rule. Employees could spend part of their working hours on projects they were curious about, without asking anyone's approval. On paper this was pure waste. The company paid its people and told them to play with whatever they liked.

The best-known example starts with a small failure. The chemist Spencer Silver was looking for a strong glue and ended up with an adhesive that could be peeled off. It was seen as a mistake and went unused for years.

Then another employee, Art Fry, entered the story. On Sundays he sang in a choir, and he was tired of the paper markers in his hymn book constantly falling out. One day the failed adhesive came to his mind. That is how the sticky note was born.

##Big ideas are often born not in a planned laboratory but where different fields unexpectedly meet.##

In *Range* (2019), David Epstein makes a point that fits this story: an invention is rarely the triumph of a lone genius. The invention was possible in an environment that let different people and fields cross. The book argues that those who make the most valuable inventions are often not specialists who gave their lives to a narrow field but broad people who worked in many fields and could connect one to another.

A new idea is rarely invented from nothing. It is born when two existing things are joined in a way nobody had put side by side before. This has a price: a broad person does not advance as fast on one subject as the deepest expert, and looks scattered at first glance. But the missing half of a problem often sits inside an unexpected curiosity.

According to a finding Epstein relays, the people who produce companies' most valuable inventions are generally those who worked across different fields, not narrow lifelong specialists. That is why 3M's free time was an investment. No manager can know ahead of time which curiosity will meet which problem. By giving employees permission to be curious, the company was storing up raw material for future inventions.

[[lessons]]
$$**Do not rule out a curiosity at once because it "does not help my work".** An accountant's interest in photography seems unrelated to the job. But one day, when he has to explain client reports visually, that curiosity can put the missing piece on the table. An interest that looks aimless today may, years later, be exactly the half of a problem you were looking for.$$

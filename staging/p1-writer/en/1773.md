[[story]]
In the 1960s the surgeon Joe Bogen cut the corpus callosum in patients with severe epilepsy, and Michael Gazzaniga then began showing pictures to one half of the brain at a time. One patient was shown a chicken claw by one hemisphere and a snow scene by the other. His left hand picked a shovel. The left hemisphere, the half that speaks, had never seen the snow, and it explained that a shovel is what you need to clean out the chicken shed.

Jonathan Haidt, in *The Happiness Hypothesis*, takes Gazzaniga's name for that explainer, the interpreter, and identifies it with the rider in his image of a rider on an elephant. It invents reasons without knowing the real causes. The elephant is the rest, the automatic and emotional part that has done the choosing. Haidt lists four divisions of the mind: mind against body, left brain against right, new brain against old, and controlled against automatic processing. The last one is the match for rider and elephant.

The image came to him on a horse. In 1991, on his first ride alone, in Great Smoky Mountains National Park, with eight other riders and a park ranger, the trail along a steep hillside turned sharply to the left. Haidt froze and did nothing, and his horse and the one beside it turned by themselves.

Haidt was not the first to picture a divided self. Buddha compared the mind to a wild elephant held by a trainer, and Plato to a charioteer holding the reins of two horses, one obedient and one unruly. Haidt pictures Freud's version as a driver struggling with a lustful horse while the driver's father lectures from the back seat.

In his version the rider can steer only while the elephant has no strong desire of its own. When the elephant wants something, the rider is no match, and willpower alone tends to fail the way a tired muscle does while the automatic side carries on effortlessly. So the rider has to work indirectly: change your surroundings so the tempting thing is not there, and coax or distract the elephant instead of fighting it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Rearrange the path instead of arguing with the elephant.** Reasons only speak to the rider, and willpower tires like a muscle, so one more reason is a weak lever. Someone who wants to stop eating sweets late at night is better off keeping none in the house than relying on a firm resolution at ten o'clock. Once you ask what the automatic side meets when it walks into the room, you can change that one thing and stop spending willpower on the same fight every evening, which leaves the rider with something easier to do.$$

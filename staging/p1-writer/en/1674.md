Every morning at seven, one of the most famous scientists in history took a short walk before he sat down to work. He did not wait for the mood to arrive. He had decided the order of his day long ago.

[[story]]
Cal Newport, in *Deep Work* (2016), argues that concentrated work rarely happens by accident. It gets a ritual: a fixed place, a set length of time and rules about what is allowed. His examples are people who built that structure around their best thinking, and the structure is plain enough to copy.

Charles Darwin kept a strict day while he was perfecting *On the Origin of Species*. His son Francis remembered that he rose promptly at seven for a short walk, then worked in his study from about eight until half past nine. The walk marked the start, and the study held the rest.

Carl Jung began building his Bollingen Tower in 1922. Newport's reading is that Jung went there to push his work forward, not to escape it. The tower was a place built for a single purpose.

Bill Gates took what he called Think Weeks twice a year. He isolated himself, often in a lakeside cottage, to read and think. In a 1995 Think Week he wrote his "Internet Tidal Wave" memo. Each of them chose a place and a block of time well before the thinking started.

##What the three share is a decision made ahead of time about where, when and under which rules the thinking happens.##

Newport spells the pattern out. Decide where you will work and for how long. Set rules for the session: no internet, say, or a target for how much you produce. Prepare what supports you, such as food and drink, or a walk before you begin. Every item removes a small negotiation that would otherwise happen at the worst moment.

He also names four ways to schedule it. Monastic strips shallow obligations away almost completely. Bimodal splits time into clear stretches of deep work and everything else. Rhythmic sets a regular habit at the same hour each day. Journalistic fits deep work into whatever gap appears. They differ in how much time they claim, and they agree that the schedule comes before the mood.

[[lessons]]
$$**Settle the place, the hour and the rules in advance.** A tailor who has to decide each morning whether to begin the hard alterations will often start with the easy ones. If the hard work always happens at the same table, right after the same cup of tea, the choice is already made when he sits down. You can choose one desk, one start time and one rule, such as a closed inbox, and let those three carry you into the work. Once the pattern is set, the routine does the deciding, and your will is saved for the work itself.$$

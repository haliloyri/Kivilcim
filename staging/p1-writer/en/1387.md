For thousands of years, people believed no human could run a mile in under four minutes. Then Roger Bannister did it. Within a year, Tony Robbins writes, 37 other runners had done the same, and his question is what changed in their heads, since the track and the distance had stayed where they were.

[[story]]
In *Awaken the Giant Within* (1991), Tony Robbins asks what a belief really is. His answer is plain: a feeling of certainty about something. Say "I'm intelligent" and you are saying "I feel certain I'm intelligent." That feeling, he writes, lets you reach resources you already have. Plenty of ideas cross your mind that you do not believe, and whether one is an idea or a belief comes down to how certain you feel when you say it.

His picture is a table. An idea is a tabletop with no legs, and it will not stand by itself. The experiences that back it up are the legs, and with enough of them the tabletop holds. The strongest legs come from personal experiences with a lot of feeling attached, painful or pleasurable, and the more of them there are, the stronger the belief. Without legs, it falls.

References do not have to come from your own life. They can come from other people, from books, tapes and films, even from imagination alone, and they do not have to be accurate. That cuts both ways, and Robbins says you could assemble the belief that people will take advantage of you or the belief that they are basically good, each with its own legs.

Then he turns to the mile. For thousands of years, he writes, people held the belief that no human could run a mile in under four minutes. In 1954 Roger Bannister broke that barrier. Robbins says he did it not by practice alone but by rehearsing the run in his mind again and again, with so much feeling that the rehearsals became vivid references.

##By Robbins's count, 37 other runners broke four minutes within a year of Bannister's run, and 300 more the year after.## He reads those numbers as references: Bannister's run gave the others legs strong enough to feel certain that they, too, could do the impossible. These are Robbins's own figures.

He draws the opposite case too. Because they have failed before, he says, many people keep saying "let's be realistic," and most of them are simply afraid of being disappointed again. A belief about what is possible, in his account, is only as solid as the experiences standing under it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Collect an example before you call something impossible.** A cook who says he could never run his own restaurant can list many reasons, each one a kitchen disaster he remembers, and together they hold his belief up firmly. A single conversation with someone who started from the same bench adds one experience in the other direction, and that is how a belief begins to shift. Look for the example closest to your own starting point, because a stranger's triumph says less to you than a neighbour's, and let that example count, then notice how the belief sounds when you say it again.$$

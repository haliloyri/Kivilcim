Thirteen days, one notepad on a desk, and a pen that kept drawing sailboats. The pen belonged to a US president, the missiles in Cuba could reach much of the East Coast, and the advisers around him wanted an answer in kind.

[[story]]
In October 1962 President Kennedy learned that the Soviet Union had put missiles in Cuba, close enough to reach much of the US East Coast. The advice came at once. His advisers told him that aggression should be met with aggression, and they pressed for a military response.

Ryan Holiday uses the thirteen days that followed in *Stillness Is the Key* as his picture of a still mind under the heaviest stakes. Kennedy had read *The Guns of August*, about leaders who rushed into a war they could not undo, and the Bay of Pigs invasion had already gone badly. Holiday sets the two episodes side by side as very different kinds of leadership, and both stood behind the choice in front of him.

So he slowed the room down. He asked his advisers to think past the first move, to what the other side would do next as well as what to do now. He told his generals that the bombing they proposed could have catastrophic consequences. Holiday quotes his line about time: "You want to use time as a tool, not as a couch." While the others talked, he drew on his notepad: sailboats, and key words.

Instead of ordering an attack, he demanded that the missiles be withdrawn and chose a naval blockade, and he called it a quarantine, because Holiday notes that "blockade" sounded too aggressive to the Soviet leaders he was thinking about. Holding back gave Khrushchev time to reconsider his options. Khrushchev backed down and the missiles were withdrawn.

Holiday stops short of presenting Kennedy as still in every part of his life. In an interview he pointed to Kennedy's personal conduct during those same days as the other side of the picture. The stillness belonged to the decision in front of him, and that was the part that counted for the missiles.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask what the other side will do next before you send the first reply.** A café owner who reads an angry online review of her place can write back sharply at once, in the first flush of annoyance, or first work out how the reviewer and other readers will react to that reply. The sharp answer is the one that arrives first, and it has counted the least of what follows. One extra question about the next step, asked before you type anything, buys you the time to choose a reply you will still stand behind tomorrow.$$

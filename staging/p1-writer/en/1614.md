A man worked as an executive at Walmart for ten years while the company was growing. He understood the business better than almost any outside investor could. In all that time he bought no shares. Stanley and Danko use him to ask what separates people who build wealth from those who only earn well.

[[story]]
In *The Millionaire Next Door* (1996), Thomas J. Stanley and William D. Danko sort people by how much net worth they hold compared with their income and age. Those far above the mark they call PAWs, Prodigious Accumulators of Wealth. Those far below it are UAWs, Under Accumulators.

The book also tells of Mr. Willis, a Walmart executive for ten years. The company grew the whole time, he knew it from the inside, and he bought none of its shares. Owning the shares was never the hard part.

The authors went looking for what the two groups do with their hours. ##PAWs spend nearly twice as many hours a month planning their investments as UAWs.## Among middle-income PAWs the figure is about 8.4 hours a month, which is roughly one working day sitting with the numbers.

The UAWs have a line ready. They will start investing once they earn about ten percent more, and when the raise comes, the plan moves to the next raise. The authors report that, raise or no raise, most of them never sit down and begin. The promise sits in the future where it costs nothing, while the time it would take sits in the present where it does.

So the difference the book points to is less about cleverness than about a standing appointment. The PAWs have a regular stretch of the month with their portfolio open in front of them. The UAWs have good intentions, a busy diary and, sometimes, an inside view of a company that they never turn into a decision.

A word of care. The book is a study of American households from the mid-1990s, and Nassim Taleb has pointed out that it looks only at people who succeeded. The hours comparison is a pattern, and it does not guarantee that more planning produces more money.

What stays useful is the picture of Mr. Willis: knowledge on one side, shares on the other, and a gap between them that only an act could close.

[[lessons]]
$$**Book a recurring hour for your own money.** A carpenter who knows his tools perfectly may still leave his invoices in a drawer for months, because they never feel urgent next to the next job. Putting a fixed slot in his week, with the paperwork on the table, turns knowing into doing, and he needs no new method or extra knowledge to keep it. You can notice which money question keeps getting pushed to a later raise or a calmer month, and give that question the first slot.$$

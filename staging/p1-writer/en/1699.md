Two small companies in Palo Alto were chasing the same customers and the same engineers. Each believed it could make sending money over the internet easy. In the end they merged under one roof, and the real fighting started after that.

[[story]]
Jimmy Soni's *The Founders* starts the PayPal story with two separate companies. In 1998 Peter Thiel, Max Levchin and others started Confinity. Its first product let people send money between PalmPilot devices, and the company then moved to payments by email.

In 1999, also in Palo Alto, Elon Musk and his co-founders started X.com, offering money services online. The two teams were after the same customers and the same engineers.

Both companies tried to grow by paying new users to sign up, and the bills for it kept climbing on both sides. The competition wore both of them down quickly. In Soni's account, the same rivalry also forced each team to come up with new solutions.

In March 2000 they stopped fighting and merged, and Musk became CEO of the combined company. The combined company kept the X.com name and became PayPal in 2001. Its focus became the PayPal product that was spreading among eBay sellers. Fraud soon turned into a new fight, and Levchin's engineers built tools to catch it.

The deal did not end the conflict. Hard arguments over management and technology went on. In September 2000, while Musk was travelling, the board voted to take the CEO job from him and give it to Thiel.

##Two teams that had been trying to take each other's customers now stood behind a single payments product.##

That product spread among eBay sellers. eBay had a payment service of its own, yet in 2002 it bought PayPal for about $1.5 billion. PayPal went public in February 2002, and eBay announced the purchase that summer. Two rivals that began by draining each other had built the value of one company. According to the book's subtitle, these entrepreneurs went on to shape Silicon Valley.

[[lessons]]
$$**Set the cost of the fight next to the cost of merging.** Two tutors in the same neighbourhood may keep cutting their fees to win each other's students. If one proposes renting a shared classroom, she should also raise who will set the timetable, because the hard part starts after the handshake. That way she ends the fatigue of competing and has already prepared for the arguments a merger brings.$$

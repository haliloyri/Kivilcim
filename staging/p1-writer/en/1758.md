No one knew what the odd, repeating stretches of DNA in bacteria were for. A few researchers followed them out of sheer curiosity. That curiosity ended up producing a tool that cuts DNA at a chosen spot.

[[story]]
Microbiologists noticed odd repeating DNA sequences in bacteria and other microbes. At first, they had no known purpose. At the start these sequences were a mystery, and a few researchers in labs tried to understand why the same repeats were there. None of them had any sign that this would become a tool.

Francisco Mojica in Spain studied microbes that lived in salt marshes, and he suspected the repeats served some purpose. He worked on the question for months, then years. He worked out that they were part of a defence system against viruses.

According to Walter Isaacson's *The Code Breaker*, bacteria keep small pieces of virus DNA and use them to recognise and cut the same virus when it comes back. Nature had developed a molecular pair of scissors billions of years ago. Around the same time, scientists at a yogurt company showed that bacteria learn to protect themselves with pieces of virus DNA, which supported Mojica's idea.

Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier met at a conference in Puerto Rico in 2011 and joined forces. In 2012 they showed that the bacterial enzyme Cas9, steered by an RNA guide, could be directed to cut a chosen spot in DNA. The cutting place could be picked by changing the guide, rather like finding one sentence in a book and removing it.

A bacterial defence had become a tool scientists could program. The two shared the 2020 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Cas9's cutting place could be selected by changing the RNA guide. Scientists could now pick a target letter of DNA and cut there. What had been a bacterial puzzle was now an editing tool. The research team that crossed from curiosity to application had begun with an unexplained pattern.

##It all grew from a question with no obvious use: why do bacteria do this?## The tool came out of curiosity.

"I made a mistake." "I need help." In a talk, Simon Sinek named these two short sentences as the test of a team. If people in the room feel unable to say them, he said, the leader has already failed.

[[story]]
Building trusting teams is the second of the five practices Simon Sinek sets out in *The Infinite Game* (2019). His definition is plain: a trusting team is one whose members feel safe expressing feelings, asking for help, discussing problems and admitting mistakes. Nothing in it concerns talent or targets.

In a 2019 talk to the US Army, tied to this practice, Sinek called psychological safety the foundation of trusting teams. He described it there as a biological and anthropological concept that is not up for debate, and said people must feel safe enough to perform at their best. Soldiers who cannot say "I made a mistake" or "I need help," he argued, point to a leader who has not created that safety. He added that admitting fear or doubt about one's own qualifications should be possible too. Trust, he said in that talk, is the root of friendships, relationships and trusting teams, because it lets people rely on each other with their frailty and their insecurity.

Where it is missing, summaries of the book describe the opposite. People hide mistakes and fake expertise instead of asking for help, so problems pile up unaddressed. Where it exists, people speak up, admit errors and raise concerns, and information moves faster through the team.

Sinek also gives a way to size up each member on two measures, performance and trust. Someone with high performance and low trust is the one to watch. Someone with low performance and high trust may be worth keeping if they are coachable.

##The leader sets the tone.## A leader listens to concerns and lets people raise issues without fear of repercussions. According to one reviewer, the chapters on this practice lean on Shell Oil and the Marine Corps Officer Candidate School for their cases.

[[lessons]]
$$**Make it cheap to say "I got this wrong."** A kitchen manager who thanks the cook for admitting a burnt batch will hear about the bad delivery from the next cook before it reaches a customer. Where mistakes get a cold reaction, people learn to hide them, and the manager sees the problem only after it has grown. Your first reaction to a mistake teaches everyone near you how early to tell you the next one.$$

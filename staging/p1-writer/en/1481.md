The book's first sentence is about having too much, not too little. A person with a phone in hand can reach more facts in a minute than they could read in a week, and still not know which one to trust. The author thinks that is where the real contest is fought.

[[story]]
Yuval Noah Harari opens *21 Lessons for the 21st Century* with a sentence about excess: in a world deluged by irrelevant information, clarity is power. Whoever can see what matters has the advantage. Everyone else is left scrolling, one more item at a time, with the next one already arriving.

He applies the same logic to censorship. In his account, censorship works not by blocking the flow of information but by flooding people with disinformation and distractions. A flood needs no locked door, and nobody has to hide a thing for it to go unseen.

Fake news is part of this picture, and Harari gives it a pair of numbers. When a thousand people believe a made-up story for one month, he writes, that is fake news. When a billion people believe it for a thousand years, that is a religion. Put the two side by side and what separates them is how many people hold the story and for how long.

He adds that stories shared by many people have long inspired them, often to good effect, so he treats fake news as something old rather than a product of the internet. What changed is the volume. A story that once needed a market square or a pulpit can now reach thousands of screens before anyone asks where it started.

His practical advice is short. If you want reliable information, be ready to pay for it. Pick a source that has a reason to be right, and treat the free feed as the place where the noise lives. Clarity, in his sense of the word, takes a choice made in advance about what you will let through, because the feed will not make it for you.

[[lessons]]
$$**Choose your sources before the feed chooses for you.** When far more information arrives than you can read, the useful skill is deciding what deserves your attention. A shop owner who gets a dozen tips about supplier prices every week might settle on two sources she has checked before and ignore the rest unless one of them confirms a tip. Doing that lets her spend her attention on a few claims she can test, and she will notice sooner when a confident message turns out to have no source behind it at all.$$

[[open]]
Seven ways to get paid fit on a single list, and the same product can sit on any of them. A coffee machine that needs capsules, for instance, could earn its money from the machine, from the capsules, or from both.

[[story]]
In the canvas from *Business Model Generation* (2010), Alexander Osterwalder and Yves Pigneur give getting paid its own box, called revenue streams. The box comes with a menu of seven. It lists selling an asset, charging a usage fee, taking a subscription, lending or renting, licensing, brokerage and advertising, and each one answers a different question about when and for what a customer hands over money. The box asks how income is earned from each customer segment, so a business serving two segments may need two answers.

Summaries of the canvas pair the options with companies. Two examples help. Netflix stands for subscription fees and UPS for usage fees, so a customer who pays for a shipment pays each time the service is used, while a subscriber pays every month for access.

A third route sits in the middle of the customer's routine. In the pattern called continuing purchases, the first item is cheap or even free and creates demand for the follow-up. Coffee machines need capsules, and printers need ink cartridges. The money arrives with each refill. One summary adds that the reverse also works, with a high-priced premium product and a low-priced complement.

The cost structure box sits opposite the revenue box and records which costs are fixed and which vary, and whether the business is driven mainly by cost or by value, and whether it gains from economies of scale and scope. Summaries give low-cost airlines as the cost-driven example and Rolex as the value-driven one.

The bottom row depends on the rest of the sheet. The guidance for the canvas says revenue streams and cost structure hold up only if the boxes above them are right, so a new way of charging usually forces a look at the customer, the offer and the channel as well. The same guidance suggests dating and versioning the canvas and redrawing it as evidence comes in.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask when your customer would most naturally pay.** A shop that repairs bicycles can charge for each repair, sell a monthly check-up plan or rent out a loan bike. Each option matches a different moment in the customer's week, and each one asks the shop to organise its work differently. If you list the ways your own work could be paid for and pick the moment that fits your customer's habits, you may find a better fit than the one you inherited. The change costs nothing to try on paper before the customer ever sees it.$$

Lab mice can be raised with no microbial exposure at all, which sounds like the cleanest and safest start an animal could have. The Sonnenburgs use them to argue that we have been picturing our gut bacteria the wrong way round.

[[story]]
Justin and Erica Sonnenburg, microbiology researchers at Stanford, describe those mice in *The Good Gut* (2015). Most people think of gut bacteria as trespassers that the body merely puts up with, so an animal that has none ought to be the healthiest one in the room. The book reports the opposite.

Germ-free mice, it says, develop severe airway responses similar to asthma when an allergen is present. Mice that carry a full microbiota are protected. The comparison is hard to wave away, because the second group received nothing except the microbes themselves.

The authors set a plainer observation beside it: children raised on farms have fewer allergies than children who were not. Neither finding proves how the immune system learns its limits, but both point the same way.

Another experiment concerns weight. Researchers took gut microbes from a pair of twins, one lean and one obese, and gave each set to mice. The mice that received the obese twin's microbes gained more body fat, while the lean twin's microbes left the mice lean. The book says they "got fat," yet the cited paper measured a rise of roughly ten percent in body fat compared with controls, so the effect was real and modest.

Antibiotics get a careful treatment. Early in life they can directly change the make-up of the microbiota, the Sonnenburgs write, and observational studies link childhood antibiotics to higher body weight. The authors add that these studies have not established antibiotics as a cause of immune-mediated disease.

Their larger worry sits on the plate. The book argues that Western diets supply too few of the fibers gut microbes can ferment, and that lower microbial diversity follows. The germ-free mouse is the extreme case of the same idea: an animal that never had a community to feed.

[[lessons]]
$$**Think of your gut as a community you feed.** Someone who finishes a course of antibiotics and goes straight back to white bread and takeaway has unsettled that community and then given it little to eat. Seeing digestion as an ecosystem makes you ask what each meal offers it, not only whether you are hungry. You can then change one regular meal for a week and notice how your body responds, and that small test tells you more about your own gut than any general rule would. $$

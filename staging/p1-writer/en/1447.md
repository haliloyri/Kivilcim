[[story]]
Seneca wrote *On the Shortness of Life* to a man he calls "my dearest Paulinus," and he opens by answering a complaint. People grumble that nature gave them too little time. His reply: "It is not that we have a short time to live, but that we waste much of it." Life is long enough and given in generous measure, he says, if all of it is well invested.

His comparison is money. Wealth, however modest, grows when a responsible guardian handles it, while vast wealth in the hands of a bad owner is scattered at once. Life stretches the same way for someone who knows how to manage it.

So where does it go? Seneca lists what keeps people busy: greed, drink, idleness, political ambition. Some spend their days chasing other people's wealth or complaining about their own. Others fill them with ledgers, bail and dinner parties that have themselves turned into business. Death takes them by surprise. Seneca accuses his readers of living as if they were going to live forever, and he says a man with white hair and wrinkles has not necessarily lived long, only existed long. Nothing is less characteristic of a preoccupied man than living, and learning how to live takes a whole lifetime.

People guard their property closely and let others trespass on their existence. They are careful with land and coins, yet wasteful with the one thing it is right to be stingy about. No one values time, Seneca says: everyone uses it lavishly, as if it cost nothing.

Then he turns to the reader. Go over your own life, he says, and count how few days turned out as you intended. His verdict: you are dying before your time.

[[lessons]]
$$**Count the hours that went where you meant them to go.** A week feels full whether or not it was spent on what mattered, so the feeling tells you little. A teacher who marks in her diary on Friday which hours went where she planned can see which demands keep taking the rest. With that pattern in front of her, she can protect one hour next week before anyone else asks for it. The count takes a few minutes, and it replaces a vague worry about time with a specific one she can act on.$$

[[open]]
A mother comes home from work with a headache and finds her daughter singing happily. She tells the girl to shut up, because her voice is ugly. The sentence takes a few seconds to say, and the girl may carry it far longer.

[[story]]
Don Miguel Ruiz builds the first agreement in *The Four Agreements* on a simple claim: the word is a force. It is the power we have to express and communicate, to think, and so to create the events in our lives. The mind, he says, is fertile ground, and words are the seeds we drop in it, whether of fear or of kindness. He calls it the most important agreement and the hardest to keep, and claims that this one alone can lift a person to what he calls heaven on earth. Elsewhere in the book he describes how children are raised much like a dog is trained, with punishment and reward: a good girl when she does what her parents want, a bad girl when she does not.

His example is the mother with the headache. The daughter, who had been singing, grows afraid to use her voice, develops a complex about how she sounds and may turn shy and withdrawn. Ruiz calls this black magic, and he adds that people use it all the time on the ones they love without realizing. The mother was probably just tired.

That is where the agreement gets its name. "Impeccable," Ruiz points out, comes from the Latin *pecatus*, which means sin, and the prefix turns it around: without sin. A sin, in his definition, is anything you do that goes against yourself, and you go against yourself when you judge or blame yourself. To be impeccable is the opposite. You take responsibility for your actions without judging or blaming yourself.

Ruiz compares gossip to a computer virus. Once it is in, you absorb somebody else's biased view of a person, pass it on, and the mind fills with the chaos of a thousand voices talking at once. The same goes for what we are told about ourselves. A child who hears that she is stupid or ugly tends to believe it, and breaking that spell takes someone who catches her attention and tells her it is not true.

[[lessons]]
$$**Build the sentence for the memory of the person hearing it.** The mother's single sentence stuck hard enough to change how the girl felt about using her voice. A team lead who sees a newcomer's messy report can skip a verdict like "this is a mess" and offer "if you split this section under two headings it reads much easier" instead. The correction still gets made, it leaves the newcomer's wish to keep writing untouched, and you have thought about how long your words will last in the other person.$$

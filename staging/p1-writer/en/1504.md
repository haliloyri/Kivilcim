[[open]]
A librarian is called over by two men who cannot agree about a window. One wants it open and the other wants it shut. Neither man is willing to budge, and each has a reason he has not said out loud. She never touches that window at all, and both men still end up satisfied.

[[story]]
Roger Fisher and William Ury open their case for looking past demands in *Getting to Yes* (1981) with this scene. One man wants fresh air, and the other doesn't want a draft. Each says "open" or "closed", and each is defending a position. The only deal that seems possible is the halfway one: open it a crack.

The librarian asks what each of them is after. One wants air, the other wants to avoid the draft, so she opens a window in the next room. Air comes in and there is no draft. Both men get what they actually wanted, and neither has to give up anything.

##When positions collide, the needs behind them often don't, and the answer can sit in a third window.## The authors' method is a simple question put to both sides: why do you want that? They add that when asked why, people often name a cause rather than a purpose, so the question has to be asked of yourself too.

The book's large example is Camp David in 1978. Israel wanted to keep part of the Sinai, and Egypt wanted all of it back. Side by side, the positions left no room. Once the reasons were on the table, they differed. Israel's concern was security, with no Egyptian forces near its border. Egypt's was sovereignty over its land. The two concerns did not collide the way the two demands had. The Sinai went back to Egypt on the condition that it stay demilitarized.

A reading-room window and a desert peninsula are far apart in scale, yet Fisher and Ury look at both the same way, beneath the demand that has been put on the table. Down there they usually find a short list of basic needs: security, economic well-being, belonging, recognition, and control over one's own life.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask what a demand is protecting.** When two colleagues want the same meeting room at the same hour, one may want quiet and the other may want the projector. If you ask each of them why, a different room or a different time might satisfy both, and nobody has to give way. You stop lining up the demands against each other and start looking at what each person needs, which usually leaves you more than one possible answer, and the conversation turns from who wins to what works.$$

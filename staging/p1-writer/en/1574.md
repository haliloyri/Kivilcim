[[open]]
A psychologist found the first edition of a children's book she loved. The date inside was one year after her father died. She had always remembered him reading it aloud to her, and she had been sure of it for as long as she could remember.

[[story]]
Carol Tavris uses a memory of her own in the memory chapter of *Mistakes Were Made (But Not by Me)*, written with Elliot Aronson. As a child she remembered her father reading her James Thurber's *The Wonderful O*. Years later she found her first edition and looked at the date: 1957, one year after he died. Her conclusion was plain: someone else gave her that book and someone else read it to her.

The chapter is titled "Memory, the Self-Justifying Historian," and it goes on to a study led by Daniel Offer. Teenage boys were interviewed about charged subjects: their home lives, sexuality, religion, their parents, how they were disciplined. More than three decades later the men were interviewed again, at forty-eight, and asked to recall what they had said about themselves as teenagers. Their ability to guess was no better than chance.

The errors leaned in one direction. Nearly half of the men remembered believing that sex in high school was acceptable. Only 15 percent had held that view at fourteen.

Couples show the same drift in a plainer setting. In surveys of spouses, the shares of the housework that each partner claims always add up to more than 100 percent.

Present feeling does some of the editing. Feeney and Cassidy found that teenagers who felt close to a parent six weeks later remembered past quarrels as less intense than they had reported at the time. In a 1958 study of attitudes to desegregation, Jones and Kohler found that people on each side remembered the plausible arguments for their own position and the weak ones for the other.

The authors boil the pattern down to one line: "If mistakes were made, memory helps us remember that they were made by someone else." Their point is that memory behaves like a historian with a stake in the outcome, rewriting without telling us.

[[lessons]]
$$**Check a vivid memory before you rely on it.** A memory can feel sharp and still have been rewritten, so the strength of the feeling tells you little about whether it is right. Someone who is sure a sibling started an old family argument can look for a message or a photo from that week before bringing it up at dinner. If the record agrees, the claim stands on more than a feeling, and if it does not, the conversation starts from curiosity instead of accusation. Either way the other person hears a question rather than a verdict.$$

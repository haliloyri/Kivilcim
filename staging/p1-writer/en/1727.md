[[story]]
At a large insurance company, the executives assumed that their experts, given the same file, would name prices close to one another. To test that, a noise audit was run: the same case summaries went to different people, each judging alone, and the answers were laid side by side. Daniel Kahneman, Olivier Sibony and Cass Sunstein describe the audit in *Noise*.

Forty-eight underwriters were given realistic summaries of risks and each set a premium. An underwriter is the insurance professional who prices a risk. Everyone looked at the same cases on their own, and each person's number was on record before anyone talked about it.

The executives expected a gap of about 10 percent between any two underwriters. The typical gap between two underwriters' premiums turned out to be 55 percent of their average premium. That was more than five times the expectation.

##The file was the same. The only thing that changed was who looked at it.##

A second experiment gave the same claim cases to claims adjusters and asked what payment the insurer should offer. The setup was the same here: the case held still, and the person looking at it was the variable.

A premium that is too high loses the customer, and one that is too low loses money on the policy. The authors stress that such errors in opposite directions do not cancel out. They add up as cost to the company, which is why two experts quoting the same file differently are a cost in both directions.

The authors call this scatter noise. Bias is a shared error, the same mistake pushing every judgment in one direction. Noise is the spread between people looking at the same case, each landing somewhere else. The executives had never seen it, because each expert's decision stayed on their own desk.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask two people separately about the same thing before you trust either number.** Say you ask two repair shops for a quote on the same fault and the figures come back far apart. Your first reaction is to suspect one of them of cheating, but both may be honest, and the gap may simply be how widely people price the same job. Getting a third quote then shows you less about which number is right and more about how wide the range really is, which is the thing you need to see before you decide anything.$$

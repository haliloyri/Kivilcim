After three days of playing Tetris for hours at a stretch, some players could not switch the game off. They saw blocks falling in their dreams. Others saw them while awake, in the shapes of buildings and the street. Shawn Achor reads that oddity as a warning about what our jobs train us to see.

[[story]]
In *The Happiness Advantage* (2010), Shawn Achor describes a study at Harvard Medical School in which 27 people played Tetris for several hours a day, three days in a row. Afterwards the reports came in. Some participants could not stop dreaming of shapes dropping from the sky. Others kept seeing the shapes everywhere during their waking hours.

##They had started to see the world as rows of Tetris blocks waiting to be fitted together.## Achor calls this a cognitive afterimage. A brain that has repeated a pattern for long enough keeps scanning for it after the game is over.

He then ties it to how attention works and compares it to an email spam filter. Scientists estimate, he writes, that we remember roughly one of every hundred or so pieces of information that reach us. The filter drops the rest. The catch is that the filter only lets through what it has been set to find. Achor gives the reader a small test: close your eyes, think of the colour red, then open them and look around the room. Red jumps out from everywhere.

Work often sets that filter in one direction. People get rewarded for noticing the problems that need solving, the stresses that need managing and the injustices that need righting. Sometimes that is exactly what the job needs. Achor's worry is getting stuck there: the better you get at scanning for the negative, the more of the positive you miss, until even a paradise can feel like hell.

His fix runs the same mechanism the other way. Every day, write down three good things from the last twenty-four hours. It takes about five minutes, and the items do not need to be profound, only specific. In the research he cites, people who did this for a single week were happier and less depressed at follow-ups one, three and six months later.

Writing the list forces the brain to go back over the day looking for something it would otherwise filter out, and like the Tetris players, it gets better at whatever search it practises.

[[lessons]]
$$**Give your attention a second search on purpose.** An accountant who spends all day hunting for errors in invoices can come home and look at the kitchen, the kids' homework and a partner's plans with the same eye. A full day of practice simply carries over into the evening, and nobody switched the filter back. A few minutes spent looking for what went right gives the filter a second job, so you see what is working as well as what is broken, and, like any search you practise, you get better at it the more often you run it.$$

A birthday cake sits at a school for Tibetan refugee children in northern India, topped with trick candles. The man it is for is turning eighty. The guest of honour beside him has flown a long way, and over the coming days the two of them will try to answer one question together.

[[story]]
*The Book of Joy* (2016) grew out of that visit. In April 2015, Archbishop Desmond Tutu traveled to the Dalai Lama's home in Dharamsala, India, to celebrate the Dalai Lama's eightieth birthday. The book they made from the week was meant as a gift for others, with Douglas Abrams moderating the conversations and writing them up.

Their question was posed in plain words: how do we find joy in the face of life's inevitable suffering? Both men had reasons to know the question well. The Dalai Lama, for one, has lived as a refugee for about five decades. Neither was asking it from a comfortable seat.

The cake was cut at the Tibetan Children's Village school in Dharamsala on April 23, 2015, and the photograph shows trick candles. That is the tone the week kept. The two men teased each other continually, and a summary describes the Dalai Lama poking Tutu's belly while Tutu called him mischievous. They shared their spiritual practices alongside the laughter.

Neither man dressed the occasion up. The publisher's description says the Archbishop has never claimed sainthood and the Dalai Lama considers himself a simple monk. Abrams, for his part, set the stories next to research from scientists and psychologists, and the book ends with daily joy practices that anchor the two men's own emotional and spiritual lives.

##What came out of it was a book with three parts: the nature of true joy, the obstacles to joy, and the eight pillars of joy.## The obstacles it covers run from fear, stress and anger to grief, illness and death. The pillars come in two groups, four of the mind and four of the heart, and each part is built from the conversations rather than from a lecture.

By the standard of conversations about joy, the setting is almost comic. Two elderly men with long histories of hardship, a week of talking, a cake with trick candles. The book does not hide the hardship, and it does not wait for it to pass before looking for joy.

[[lessons]]
$$**Take a hard question to someone and ask it out loud.** A woman unsure how to keep going through a difficult year could invite an older friend to coffee and put one plain question to her: how did you find any lightness when things were hard? Saying it aloud makes the question smaller and lets the other person answer from their own life. You may leave with a clearer picture of your own situation than you would have reached alone, and with someone who knows what you are carrying and whom you can call on the next hard day.$$

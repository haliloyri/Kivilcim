The mushroom you see in a forest is only a small, visible part of a living thing. The real body lies under the ground in fine threads. Those threads hold the forest soil together and do work that would amaze you.

[[story]]
The biologist Merlin Sheldrake describes in *Entangled Life* a kingdom of fungi that is neither plant nor animal. Genetically they are closer to animals than to plants. The mushroom we see on a plate is only a fruiting body.

Sheldrake portrays fungi as far more complex than most people realise. A fungus can be as old as a tree. Yet seeing it with the naked eye is often impossible.

Most of the organism is invisible. In soil or inside wood runs a web of fine threads, called mycelium. A mushroom is only the small tip of this web that comes up to the surface.

The cells of a fungus are often thread-shaped, spreading through soil or wood. The threads hold a huge surface for absorbing food. That is why the hidden part of a fungus is often far larger than the part that shows.

Many fungi partner with plant roots. They pass minerals such as phosphorus to the plant and receive sugars in return. According to Sheldrake, such partnerships mattered when plants first moved onto land.

Some fungi steer animals. One called Ophiocordyceps takes over ants and pushes them to act in the fungus's favour. In nature there are friendships and there are traps. Sheldrake is careful about popular claims of a "wood wide web".

The fungi's hidden work shaped the forest itself over time. A large part of life rests on their quiet work under the ground.

Fungi also sit at the other end of the kitchen: the mould in cheese, the yeast in bread, in beer. Most of us meet them every day without noticing.

##The visible part of a fungus is small, and what holds ecosystems together is the hidden threads beneath it.## A hidden structure was doing more than the visible one.

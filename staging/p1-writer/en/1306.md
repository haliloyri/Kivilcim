Dry grass and dead branches lay on the forest floor, and the crews had one rule for dealing with a fire: put it out, however small. The rule looked sensible, even obvious. Then came the question of what it left behind on the ground.

[[story]]
Nassim Nicholas Taleb's logic in *Antifragile* applies to forest fires, a case where protection backfires. In his account, the rule was to put out every blaze, small or large, quickly and completely, because fire looked like harm to a forest. Every small fire was stopped early, and each one stopped counted as a success, since the trees were still standing and nothing visible had gone wrong.

Yet those small fires had been doing a job. They burned off dry grass and dead material and let the ground renew itself, so that fuel never had the chance to pile up. Stopping them left the dry material exactly where it lay.

Year after year the grass and dead wood stacked up, and the next fire to reach it had far more to burn. The protection had made the forest riskier, and the forest looked perfectly healthy until the moment it burned.

##Take away the small stress, and the large one gets more room to grow.##

Taleb carries the pattern into other places. A child kept away from every difficulty grows up fragile. An economy that is never allowed to contract stores up a larger collapse for later. A system that never feels a small shock has no way of clearing itself, so the shock arrives all at once. Small, frequent disturbances, in his view, are how a natural system stays healthy, and their absence is the hidden risk.

Antifragile, as he uses the word, means gaining from small shocks, the way a person gains from falling down and getting back up, or from being stressed and then repaired. A forest that is allowed to burn a little belongs to that kind of system, and a forest that is never allowed to burn becomes a stack of fuel.

[[lessons]]
$$**Let small problems surface before they stack up.** A kitchen manager who quietly fixes every small mistake before the owner sees it may believe she is protecting her team. Meanwhile nobody learns what goes wrong, and the same slips keep gathering until one of them lands in the middle of a busy evening. If she lets the small ones show and talks them through, the team deals with them while they are still easy to deal with, and she can see which ones keep coming back. The slips cost little at that size, and every one of them becomes information she would otherwise never have.$$

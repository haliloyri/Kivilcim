A medical blogger writes that he can no longer read War and Peace. A writer who has lived among long books all his life says his own concentration slips after a page or two. Nicholas Carr thinks both are describing a change in how the brain works, and he tries to show why.

[[story]]
Carr's *The Shallows* (2010) grew out of his 2008 essay "Is Google Making Us Stupid?" It begins with a feeling. For some years, Carr writes, he has had the uncomfortable sense that someone or something has been tinkering with his brain. Where he once sank into long prose, his attention now drifts after a page or two.

He is not the only one. Carr quotes Bruce Friedman, a blogger on the use of computers in medicine, who says he has almost totally lost the ability to read and absorb a longer text. He puts it plainly: he cannot read *War and Peace* anymore.

Carr takes such reports seriously because of what he has read about plasticity. The brain adapts to what it is asked to do again and again. A skill that is exercised grows stronger, and one that is neglected fades. His examples include monkeys that learned to use tools, violinists, and London taxi drivers who build detailed mental maps of the city.

##The same plasticity that lets a taxi driver map a city lets the mind be reshaped by what it does all day.##

Then he turns to the page itself. Studies of hypertext, he says, found that deciding whether to click a link adds mental work that has nothing to do with reading. Carr's reading of the research is that linear text still does better for comprehension, retention and learning.

For memory he goes to a sea slug. In the early 1970s Eric Kandel showed with *Aplysia* that moving something from short-term to long-term memory physically changes the brain: the synapses grow new terminals. Kandel's condition is that the information be processed thoroughly and deeply, and Carr draws the short version: the key to consolidation is attention. The Web, he writes, is a handy supplement to memory. Used as a substitute, skipping that inner work, it risks emptying the mind of its riches.

The claim is Carr's argument, built from these pieces. It is not a measurement of any one reader.

[[lessons]]
$$**Choose which kind of reader you practise being.** A cook who tastes everything in a quick sip may slowly lose patience for a sauce that needs an hour at low heat. The brain strengthens what you repeat, so the way you read in an ordinary week is also training for the way you will read next month. You can use that by keeping one stretch of the day for reading or work that you hold in a single piece, and noticing how it goes.$$

Brené Brown had a filing error, and she says so. For years she had put resentment next to anger, as a close cousin. Then a conversation with an emotions researcher moved it across the map, to a neighbourhood she had not expected.

[[story]]
In *Atlas of the Heart* (2021), Brown writes that she had always thought resentment lived close to anger. It felt that way: the tight jaw, the replaying of a slight, the heat under the skin. The book maps 87 emotions and experiences, and she had assumed this one was easy to place.

One summary of the book says the idea changed after an interview with emotions researcher Marc Brackett. ##Resentment, Brown came to see, is not part of the anger family at all. It is a function of envy.## The conclusion is the book's own, and it is a flat contradiction of what she had assumed.

That is why the word shows up in her comparison section, beside envy, jealousy, admiration, schadenfreude and freudenfreude. Envy is wanting something another person has. Resentment, as the summaries read it, is what a person does with that wanting when it feels unfair. The anger on the surface is real, and under it sits a want that has not been admitted.

Summaries add a few details. One describes resentment as bitterness tied to perceived unfairness, with envy hidden beneath the frustration. Another notes that it often follows physical or emotional exhaustion, which fits: a tired person watches someone else get the rest they wanted. A third says it can point to a need nobody has spoken aloud, or to a balance that has tipped unfairly. In Time's summary, what people most often envy is looks, status and wealth, so the hidden want can be as plain as any of those, or as small as an afternoon.

A reclassification like this changes what you look for. If resentment is anger, you search for the line that was crossed. If it belongs to envy, you search for the thing someone else has that you would like to have.

[[lessons]]
$$**When you resent someone, ask what they have that you want.** A baker who stews over a colleague leaving at four o'clock can spend the whole week replaying the unfairness of it. If she asks what the colleague has that she wants, the answer may be an afternoon off, and that is something she can ask for. The question moves her from judging a person to naming a need, and it gives the next conversation with her manager something specific to say. If the answer turns out to be something nobody can give her, she at least knows what the week's irritation was made of.$$

&&Think of someone you resent right now: what do they have, or get, that you quietly want for yourself?&&

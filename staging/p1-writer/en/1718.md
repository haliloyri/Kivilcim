A legion stood on the bank of a small river. What mattered was not the water but the law behind it. Whatever Caesar did next would turn a political quarrel in Rome into a war with no way back.

[[story]]
In 49 BC Roman law forbade a commander to lead his army into Italy. Caesar was in command in Gaul, and a small river called the Rubicon marked the edge of the province he governed. On one side of it a man was a commander. On the other, if he brought soldiers, he was a rebel.

The tension had been building for months. According to the accounts, a consul named Marcellus had charged Pompey with defending the city after rumours that Caesar would invade Italy. In December 50 BC the Senate voted 370 to 22 that both Pompey and Caesar should give up their armies, yet Pompey and a consul rejected it.

At the start of January Caesar said he would resign if the others did. His representatives offered to give up Transalpine Gaul if he could keep two legions and stand for the consulship without being in Rome. Cato rejected those terms. Then the Senate demanded that Caesar give up his post or be judged an enemy of the state. Tribunes loyal to him vetoed, the veto was ignored, and they fled to his camp.

Caesar did not disband his army. In the scene Tom Holland's *Rubicon* tells, he came to the river with the 13th Legion. To cross was not an ordinary march but open rebellion against the state, and historians usually place it on the night of 10 or 11 January 49 BC.

He advanced. On the far side he took the city of Ariminum without resistance. Word reached Rome a few days later, and civil war had begun.

##Crossing one river turned a political dispute into a war that could not be undone.## The river itself was small, but an army arriving on the other bank had changed what the border meant. The Republic's political balance shifted and never returned to what it had been. Holland takes his title from that threshold.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before an irreversible step, name what you will not be able to take back.** A shop owner sending a formal written warning to a tenant may see it as a small piece of paperwork, yet it can change the relationship for good. Saying out loud what cannot be undone before the letter goes out turns a hurried decision into a calm one. The decision may stay the same, but you make it knowingly, with the worst outcome in view, and it becomes harder to say "if only" later.$$

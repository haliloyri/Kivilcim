Nobody posted a sign, changed a price or said a word to the people eating. In a hospital cafeteria in Boston, the only change was what sat in the refrigerators beside the cash registers and in a few baskets around the room. Three months later the drinks going through the tills looked different.

[[story]]
In *Atomic Habits* (2018), James Clear tells the story of Anne Thorndike, a primary care physician at Massachusetts General Hospital, and a six-month study she ran in the hospital cafeteria. Until then, the refrigerators right next to the cash registers held only soda.

Thorndike's team added water to those refrigerators. They also set baskets of bottled water next to the food stations around the room. Soda stayed where it had always been, in the main refrigerators. The only difference was that water now sat within reach at every place people picked up a drink.

Nobody was told about any of it. ##Over the next three months, soda sales fell by 11.4 percent and sales of bottled water rose by 25.8 percent.## Similar changes to where the food was placed produced similar results.

Clear links this to an older idea. In 1936 the psychologist Kurt Lewin wrote a simple equation: behaviour is a function of the person in their environment. Clear's other example comes from a Dutch suburb during the energy crisis of the 1970s. The houses were nearly identical except for where the electric meter sat. In some it was in the basement, in others in the main hallway upstairs. The households that walked past their meter every day used less energy.

His conclusion, in the chapter titled "Motivation Is Overrated; Environment Often Matters More," is that people often choose products because of where they are, not what they are. Cookies on the kitchen counter get eaten even when nobody is hungry. A guitar in the closet or vitamins at the back of the pantry make the habit easy to skip. So his suggestions are about location: keep pill bottles next to the bathroom faucet, and fill a few water bottles in the morning and leave them around the house.

A few details complete the picture. In the published study, the cafeteria had first introduced traffic-light colour labels, and the water figures come from the later placement phase. A follow-up found that the shift toward healthier purchases held for 24 months.

[[lessons]]
$$**Put the healthy option where your hand already goes.** Someone who snacks in front of the TV every evening, with crisps in the cupboard by the sofa and fruit in the bottom drawer of the fridge, is letting the shelves make the decision. Moving the fruit bowl to the coffee table and the crisps to a high cupboard is a one-time job, and it spares you the same argument with yourself every night. Once the setting is changed, the healthier choice starts happening without a fresh round of willpower each time, or a new promise to yourself.$$

[[open]]
Most of us carry a map of the world with two colours on it. One side is rich, the other is poor, and there is a wide gap between them. A set of four numbers puts most people somewhere the map has no colour for.

[[story]]
Hans Rosling calls it the gap instinct: the urge to cut the world into two groups with a chasm between them. The rich West sits on one side and the poor rest on the other. In *Factfulness*, he swaps that picture for four income levels, measured in dollars per person per day.

The lowest, level 1, is anyone living on less than $2 a day, and about 1 billion people are there. Level 2 runs from $2 to $8 a day, with about 3 billion people. The step above, from $8 to $32 a day, holds about 2 billion. At the top, past $32 a day, stand about 1 billion.

Add the four groups up and you reach roughly 7 billion people. Now look at where they stand. About 5 billion of them live on levels 2 and 3, the two middle steps, which is roughly seven people in ten. Neither end of the scale has more than 1 billion. Said out loud, the four counts run 1 billion, 3 billion, 2 billion and 1 billion.

Fold that back into the two-camp picture and something has to give. The 1 billion on level 1 and the 3 billion on level 2 would end up in the same group, though they sit on different steps of the ladder. The 2 billion on level 3 would be forced onto one side or the other.

Rosling's four levels put a dollar line at each step: $2, $8 and $32. That line places a person on a step, where a label such as "developing" says almost nothing about which one. The whole world fits on that ladder, and the largest groups are in the middle.

[[lessons]]
$$**Look for the middle before you pick sides.** A two-camp picture hides the group in between, and that group is often the largest one. A café owner who sorts her customers into regulars and tourists may find that most of the people in the room are neither, such as office workers who come in once or twice a week. Once she names the middle group, she can ask what it wants, which she could never have learned by treating everyone as one of two types, and she can plan her opening hours around the people who actually fill the room.$$

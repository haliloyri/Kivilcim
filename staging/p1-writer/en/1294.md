An ad runs on a search results page for a product that nobody has manufactured yet. All it does is send curious people to a basic web page. Somebody counts how many of them click, and how many go on to order, before a single unit is made.

[[story]]
Tim Ferriss built his own business, BrainQUICKEN, around a supplement sold online, with outside companies doing the manufacturing and the handling of orders. In *The 4-Hour Workweek*, he describes how he decided whether a product deserved that kind of setup before spending on it.

The usual order of things runs the other way. A founder drafts a plan, raises money, builds the product, fills a warehouse and opens for business. Only then does the first real customer show up and say yes or no.

Ferriss turns it around. First he puts up a basic web page about the product, with a way for a person to order. Then he buys small search ads that lead to that page, so strangers who are already looking for something like it see the offer.

The ads and the page are the whole experiment. Nothing sits in a warehouse yet, and the manufacturer and the order handling, the costly parts of the business, all come after it. What the test produces is a pair of numbers: how many people clicked the ad, and how many went further and tried to order.

##A number from strangers who typed a search is worth more than a plan written alone.##

If the numbers justify it, he goes ahead and has the product made. If they do not, the idea is dropped or changed, and the loss is a small ad bill instead of a garage of unsold stock.

That is the quiet point of the method. A guess about what people want stays a guess until someone is asked to click, to order, or to turn away, and a plan written at a desk cannot ask them. The test makes that moment happen early, while changing course is still cheap.

[[lessons]]
$$**Let a small real action decide a big commitment.** A home baker wondering about buying a second oven could post a pre-order list for next week's loaves and see how many neighbours put their names down. If the list fills up, the oven is a reasoned purchase, and if it stays nearly empty, she has saved herself an expensive mistake. You swap an opinion about what customers might do for what a few of them actually did, and you do it while changing course is still cheap, which is the moment when being wrong costs the least.$$

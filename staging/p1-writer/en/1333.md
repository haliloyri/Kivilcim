[[story]]
Prisoner 4859 had arrived at Auschwitz on purpose. Witold Pilecki, a Polish officer, was swept up in a Warsaw street roundup of about 2,000 men on 19 September 1940, and on 21-22 September he reached the camp with 1,705 other prisoners under the name Tomasz Serafinski.

Mark Manson opens Everything Is F*cked with him. He reads Pilecki as heroism, the ability to conjure hope where none exists, and uses him to ask where hope comes from.

Inside, Pilecki built an underground group, the ZOW, organized in secret cells of five. It aimed to lift inmates' morale, bring in news from outside, hand out extra food and clothing, build intelligence networks and train detachments to seize the camp if a relief attack came. His homemade radio transmitter took seven months to finish and, until it was dismantled in the autumn of 1942, sent out figures on arrivals and deaths. He had been sending secret reports since October 1940, and they became a principal source of intelligence on Auschwitz for the Western Allies.

The camp Gestapo was killing many ZOW members, and Pilecki wanted to convince the Home Army's leaders that a rescue was possible. On the night of 26-27 April 1943 he escaped with two comrades and was wounded by gunfire on the way, and that August he wrote a report. The Home Army command judged that a rescue attack would fail, citing a lack of transport, supplies and shelter for the freed prisoners. After the war, the communist-run Polish authorities tried him in 1948 and executed him on 25 May of that year.

Manson defines hope as the belief that you can make progress toward something valuable. He says it rests on a sense of control, a belief in the value of something, and community. One line credited to the book calls hope the only thing any of us willingly dies for, and Pilecki's path fits it.

The book still ends on a turn. Manson asks the reader to act without hope, to stop hoping for better and to be better.

[[lessons]]
$$**Tie hope to a task within reach.** Hope tends to hold when it is attached to something you can still move, even if the larger situation stays out of your hands. Someone on a failing team who knows they cannot save the whole project can pick one fixable process, such as the handover notes, and move it forward a little every day. After a few weeks of that, they have turned a large expectation into a small job, and they can see how much has actually changed and how far they have come. That gives hope something solid to rest on while the big problem stays open.$$

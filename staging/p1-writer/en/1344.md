"Observe your thoughts." Dan Harris read that advice from Eckhart Tolle, recognised himself in the problem it described, and then hit a wall. Nobody told him how. It took him a while, and a practice he had once mocked, to find the part of the instruction that was missing.

[[story]]
Peter Jennings had handed Dan Harris the faith beat at ABC News, though Harris was a lifelong agnostic. The assignment led him to Eckhart Tolle's books and then to an interview with Tolle himself. Tolle's claim was that much of our suffering comes from identifying with the stream of thoughts in our heads, and in that description Harris saw his own competitiveness and defensiveness.

##What he couldn't find was a method; being told to observe your thoughts doesn't explain how to do it.##

The thing he was trying to observe became the centre of *10% Happier*. Harris calls it "the voice in my head": the narrator that comments on everything, usually unkindly, hopping from branch to branch like a monkey. He treats it as the universal human problem, not a personal quirk. His preferred title for the book was "The Voice in My Head Is an Asshole." The publisher said no, so the voice moved to the subtitle: "How I Tamed the Voice in My Head, Reduced Stress Without Losing My Edge, and Found Self-Help That Actually Works."

Meditation supplied the missing how. What it gave him, he says, was "the ability to respond rather than react", a short gap between the voice's urging and what he actually did. For strong emotions he found a four-step tool practical enough to repeat: RAIN. Recognize what is happening. Allow it to be there. Investigate how it feels in the body. Non-identification, meaning don't take it as who you are.

The voice didn't go quiet. In his account it was still active, still commenting on meetings and interviews and everything in between, but no longer entirely in charge of what came next.

That turned out to have its own trap, and he walked straight into it. For a while after he took up meditation, Harris became too passive at work, mistaking calm for not caring. His boss, Ben Sherwood, pushed back, and Harris had to learn that equanimity and ambition could sit side by side. The subtitle's "without losing my edge" was not decoration. Taming the voice was never meant to switch off the person who wanted things.

[[lessons]]
$$**Name the voice's comment and check it in your body before you believe it.** When the inner narrator produces a harsh sentence about you, labelling it as a comment and noticing where it sits physically puts a small, real distance between you and it. An engineer who fumbles a point in a meeting can, instead of replaying "I always do this" all evening, think "there's the voice again", notice the tight chest, and go back to dinner. Doing this keeps the voice from running your evening without having to win an argument with it first.$$

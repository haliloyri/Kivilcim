England's best table tennis player walks into the biggest tournament of his career and plays far below his own level. Years later he can still say exactly how it felt: humiliated.

[[story]]
That is how Matthew Syed describes the 2000 Sydney Olympics in *Bounce* (2010). He had risen to become England's top table tennis player and a two-time Olympian. Now he was at his most important tournament and he played well below his best. He wanted to know what had happened to a skill he had spent years building.

His explanation rests on two systems in the brain, the explicit and the implicit. A novice learning a task monitors each step consciously while the brain builds the framework for it. Once the skill is learned, it sits in the implicit system and runs by itself.

Under pressure, control of a familiar task shifts back to the explicit system, which cannot do many things at once. The expert starts to struggle with what he had already mastered. Syed describes the explicit system pulling a skilled player back toward the step-by-step play of a beginner.

##The ordinary example is a full glass of wine carried across an expensive carpet.## You slow down, watch every step, and a walk you have made ten thousand times becomes awkward.

The wider theme of the book is that the mind shapes what the body does. One summary recounts severely injured soldiers who were injected with saline and whose pain eased while they believed it was morphine.

Syed's own habit, by the same summary, is to put a big competition in perspective by thinking about his relationships, his health and his family, which lowers the pressure.

One reviewer noted that Syed seems to conclude choking just happens and nothing can be done about it, and argued that this sounds as biologically determinist as the talent myths Syed rejects. The explanation says why experts choke, and says little about how to avoid it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Keep your attention off the steps your body already knows.** A confident driver who suddenly starts naming every pedal and mirror during a test hesitates and stalls, though nothing about the driving has changed. The skill was running by itself until she began to watch it. When the moment matters, aim your attention at the outcome or at the person in front of you, and let the practised hands do the rest. Before a talk, a test or a match, decide on that target in advance so you are not looking for it when your pulse is up.$$

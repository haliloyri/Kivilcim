A people grew rich on the oil beneath their land, and the shares of that income could be inherited but never sold. The deaths began inside the gap that rule left, one family member after another.

[[story]]
The Osage had been moved onto rocky land in Oklahoma. They bought it in the 1870s and held the underground rights in common. When oil was found beneath it, their wealth grew until, in the 1920s, they were among the richest people per head in the world. David Grann's *Killers of the Flower Moon* tells what followed.

The income was shared out through headrights, tied to individuals. A headright could be inherited but not sold, so it passed by marriage and by death. Many Osage also had white guardians, approved by a court, who managed their money, and guardians often took a share of it.

In 1921 Anna Brown was found shot in a ravine. Her mother, Lizzie, died soon after. In 1923 the house of Anna's sister Rita and her husband Bill Smith was blown up. Another sister, Mollie Burkhart, watched her family die one by one. Her husband, Ernest Burkhart, was the nephew of the rancher William Hale.

Hale was known across the county as a friend of the Osage, a big man in the hills. The family at the centre of the deaths was tied to him by marriage. It took years for anyone to see that.

Local lawmen, doctors and lawyers often covered the deaths. The new federal Bureau of Investigation took the case and sent Tom White. Because local officers could not be trusted, agents went in under cover. Hale and others were convicted.

Tom White, who ran the investigation, came to the job from the newly formed bureau. Local law enforcement could not be trusted, so agents entered the county without revealing who they were.

##Grann concludes that far more people were killed than the trials ever showed.## A people's money had been arranged so that others gained from their deaths, and the crime grew inside that arrangement.

[[lessons]]
$$**When someone else controls a person's money or rights, look at who gains if that person is gone.** A student society might let one member hold the cash box with nobody else seeing the accounts, so neither a mistake nor bad faith would ever show. A joint signature, or a plain statement looked at every three months, moves trust from the person to the arrangement. That makes it easier for the treasurer and for those whose money it is.$$

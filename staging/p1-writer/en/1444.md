A large company replaces about half its customers within five years, about half its employees in four and a half, and about half its investors in under a year. One business book treats those departures as a gauge of how the company is doing, and not a small one.

[[story]]
Frederick Reichheld, a director at Bain & Company, built *The Loyalty Effect* (1996) on a count most companies leave off the front page: who leaves. The figures in Publishers Weekly's review are blunt. A major company replaces about half its customers in five years, half its employees in four and a half, and half its investors in under a year.

A reader summary of the book sharpens the picture. A typical company loses 10 to 30 percent of its customers each year, employee turnover of 15 to 25 percent a year is common, and about 40 percent of US employees stay with an employer for two years or less. At those rates, the summary says, disloyalty cuts corporate performance by 25 to 50 percent or more. These are the book's mid-1990s figures, and the sources here could not check them independently.

Reichheld calls customer defection one of the most revealing measures of business failure, and the goal he sets is zero defections. The count shows where the company stopped giving people a reason to stay.

The core of the argument is a cause-and-effect link between employee loyalty and customer loyalty. Disloyal employees rarely build lasting relationships with customers, because those take time. Long-term employees add value through training, efficiency, picking the right customers, keeping them and bringing in referrals.

His answer is what the review calls loyalty-based management: actively holding on to customers and aiming at those likely to stay. State Farm and Toyota's Lexus are among the companies the book offers as proof that it improves the bottom line. The catalog description adds MBNA, John Deere and Leo Burnett to that list. The book's jacket gives a slightly shorter figure for employees: half of them gone in four years rather than four and a half.

The reviewer even calls loyalty an old-fashioned concept that looks obvious yet had been overlooked amid rapid turnover in business.

[[lessons]]
$$**Count the departures too.** Most of us keep track of who arrives, the new customer, the new follower, the new hire, and treat the ones who stop showing up as background noise. A bakery owner who notices every new face at the counter but never writes down which regulars stopped coming has no way of seeing a pattern. A short list of who has not been back lately, and a plain question to a few of them, turns a vague sense of drift into something you can answer. Doing this once a month keeps the list short and the questions easy.$$

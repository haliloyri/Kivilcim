[[story]]
Ben Horowitz and Marc Andreessen founded Loudcloud in 1999, near the peak of the dotcom bubble. Two months later they had raised $21 million, and investors urged Horowitz to run the company as though capital were free. Within six months it had hired almost 200 people and spent $5 million on a new office. Horowitz tells the story in *The Hard Thing About Hard Things*.

In June 2000 it closed a $120 million round at a $700 million pre-money valuation, and by the end of the year it had 477 employees. For the third quarter it had forecast $100 million in booked sales and finished with $37 million in contracts. Customers paid monthly, so booked sales overstated the cash coming in.

The IPO filing came in September 2000. It showed $1.94 million in revenue over the previous six months, forecast $75 million for the coming year, and warned that without the offering the money would run out in about three weeks. The press called the company another unprofitable dotcom. The roadshow opened at $10 a share; within three weeks comparable companies lost about half their value, and the price was cut to $6. The offering raised $162.5 million.

At the first earnings call, Loudcloud cut its revenue guidance from $75 million to $55 million, and the stock fell from $6 to $2. On 26 September 2001 its competitor Exodus went bankrupt, a year after being valued at $50 billion. In early 2002 Loudcloud's largest customer, paying $1 million a month and about a fifth of its revenue, went bankrupt too.

Horowitz had planned to buy Opsware, the software that managed Loudcloud's servers, and build the company on it, because software needs less capital than a cloud. He gave a team of ten the job of making Opsware run on any server platform and kept the plan from the rest of the company, so it would not distract from the core business.

Now he chose to leave the cloud business. IBM and EDS were the leading bidders, and EDS won: it paid $63.5 million in cash, took on the liabilities and expenses, and agreed to license Opsware for $20 million a year. Loudcloud kept the software and renamed itself Opsware. No management book told Horowitz this was the right path. In 2007 HP bought Opsware for about $1.6 billion.

[[lessons]]
$$**Pick the least bad option early and say it aloud.** A restaurant owner who keeps postponing a choice between two bad options pays the cost of both at once, in tired staff and wasted money. When she announces which one she has picked and the date it starts, her staff can plan around it, and she can see within weeks whether it works. Naming the least bad choice, instead of waiting for a clean answer, turns an argument into a plan with a date and leaves room to correct it.$$

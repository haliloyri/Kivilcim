Twelve chapters, twelve causes, and the phone is only one of them. A book that sets out to explain why people cannot focus ends up at exhaustion, interrupted workdays, lost daydreaming and pollution.

[[story]]
Johann Hari divides *Stolen Focus* (2022) into twelve chapters, and each one takes up a different deeper cause of the attention crisis. A review says the book draws on over 250 interviews with experts in neuroscience, psychology, education and technology. The phone is on the list, but it is not the whole list.

Begin with the body. Physical and mental exhaustion is one of the causes he names, and it sits next to the smartphone as an equal and not as an afterthought.

Then comes the way work arrives. Hari describes multitasking as ineffective and switching between tasks as something that wears the brain down. Phones add constant interruption on top of that. An always-on work culture, he argues, brings stress and lowers productivity, because being reachable at all times leaves no long stretch in which to stay with one thing.

##The cause that sounds backwards is the decline of mind-wandering.## The book describes mind-wandering as essential for attention to work properly, so a mind that is never allowed to drift is not necessarily a better-focused one. His own stay in a Provincetown beach house fits that picture: when he let his mind wander there, he reports, new ideas came.

Pollution is on the list too. It sits far from any screen, which is part of his point that the crisis has many roots.

The book also travels to odd corners. It features veterinarians who diagnose dogs with ADHD, a favela in Rio where everyone lost attention in an unusual way, and an office in New Zealand that found a technique to restore its workers' attention.

Put together, the chapters make an argument by counting. If twelve separate things pull at attention, telling people to put the phone down answers only one of them.

[[lessons]]
$$**Look at the whole day when focus slips.** A bookkeeper who blames her phone for her errors may find they cluster on the days she slept badly and was interrupted every few minutes. The phone is easy to see, so it takes the blame, while the poor sleep and the interruptions go unnoticed. Listing a few causes beside the phone lets you pick the one you can change first.$$

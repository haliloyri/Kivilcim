[[open]]
In lean times a cell stops building and starts breaking down its own parts for reuse. Two enzymes decide when, and Sinclair's advice about what to put on your plate is built around them.

[[story]]
David Sinclair introduces the two in *Lifespan* (2019). The first is TOR, the growth enzyme. When food is plentiful it keeps cells growing. When times are bad it limits growth and has the cell recycle its components, a process called autophagy. Worn parts are taken apart, and their pieces go into new ones.

The second is AMPK. It reacts to low energy, and when it fires it switches on glucose and fatty-acid metabolism. Together the two sit inside what Sinclair calls the survival circuit, one saying build, the other saying make do.

In a modern kitchen the build signal is rarely quiet. Meat at lunch and again at dinner keeps, by Sinclair's logic, the growth switch pressed down all day, and the recycling never gets its turn. His diet advice targets mTOR. Branched-chain amino acids, found mostly in animal protein, activate it, so eating fewer of them lowers the signal, and he recommends less animal protein and more vegetables. Exercise and the drug metformin push AMPK instead.

The drugs get their own part of the story. Rapamycin blocks mTOR and extended mouse lifespan by 9 to 14 percent, at the price of a weaker immune response. Metformin, which activates AMPK, is described as mimicking some effects of caloric restriction. Both act as shortcuts into a mechanism that famine used to trigger.

Not everyone agrees with the diet side. One reviewer disputes Sinclair's stance on red meat while accepting the warning about overstimulating mTOR. Others ask whether cutting protein costs muscle, and that question stays open.

A cell is not a person either. A switch that works in a dish or a mouse may not behave the same way in a whole human body, and the rapamycin figure comes from mice.

[[lessons]]
$$**Build a pause into any system that only pushes forward.** A carpenter who takes orders without a break sharpens his tools only when one snaps, and by then the job in front of him is late. Small faults pile up where no maintenance slot exists. Leave one stretch of the week with nothing new added, and use it to look at what needs care before it breaks.$$

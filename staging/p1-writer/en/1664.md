Not long after a couple move in together, one partner's voice turns harsh over something small. Asked what is wrong, she says "Nothing", while everything in her face says the opposite. The reaction is far larger than its cause, and one author has a name for what sits behind it.

[[story]]
Eckhart Tolle builds a whole chapter of *A New Earth* around that moment. In his account, any negative emotion that is not fully faced and let go leaves a remnant of pain behind. The remnants join into an energy field that lives in the cells of the body, an accumulation of old emotional pain, and he calls it the pain-body.

It has two stages. One is dormant, the other active. Dormant usually lasts a few weeks, sometimes days or months, and rarely years. When it is hungry it wakes, and Tolle says it can use the most insignificant event as a trigger: something somebody says or does, or even a thought.

Alone, it feeds on your thoughts. Suddenly your thinking turns negative. With someone else around, preferably a partner or a close family member, it pushes their buttons so it can feed on the drama. Tolle describes it as a semiautonomous energy form with a primitive intelligence, not unlike a cunning animal, and he calls it an addiction to unhappiness.

##You don't just marry your wife or husband, he writes, you also marry her or his pain-body.## Not long after moving in together, he says, a partner's voice may turn harsh. She accuses or shouts, most likely over a relatively trivial matter, or goes withdrawn. "What's wrong?" you ask. "Nothing is wrong," she says, while the hostile energy she gives off says everything is. Tolle says it is the pain-body speaking through her, not her real face.

People with dense pain-bodies blow insignificant matters up out of all proportion, and they cannot tell the difference between an event and their reaction to it. One of his examples is road rage: a driver loses his temper because the car in front is going too slowly.

Freedom begins, Tolle writes, with realising that you have a pain-body, and with being present enough to notice it as a heavy influx of negative emotion at the moment it wakes. Once it is recognised, it can no longer pretend to be you. It does not usually dissolve at once, but when the link to your thinking is cut, it begins to lose energy.

[[lessons]]
$$**When a reaction outgrows its trigger, look for the older feeling.** Someone who snaps at a friend for answering a message late may see, once calm, that the snap was about something that had been building in them for weeks, a tired stretch at work or an old worry that the message happened to touch. Seeing that, they can talk about what is actually weighing on them and leave the late answer aside. They gain a breath of room before the next quarrel, and they recognise the same wave a little sooner when it returns, which makes it easier to pause instead of answer.$$

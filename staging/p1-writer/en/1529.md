Every working day the same few people meet at the same time in the same place, and the meeting has a hard stop: under fifteen minutes. Nothing about it is meant to be impressive. It is meant to be short.

[[story]]
*Scrum* (2014), by Jeff Sutherland with J.J. Sutherland, treats the daily scrum, often called the stand-up, as one of the method's regular events. The rules are few. The meeting lasts under 15 minutes and happens at the same time and in the same place every day, so nobody has to ask when or where.

Its job is narrow. It exists to announce progress toward the sprint goal and any obstacles hindering it, without detailed discussion. Each person answers the same three questions: what did you do yesterday, what will you do today, and what is in your way. Those questions are standard in Scrum teaching.

##Anything that needs more than a sentence waits until the meeting is over.## The stand-up is where a problem gets named. It is not where the problem gets solved, and that is the rule that keeps it under fifteen minutes.

The meeting sits inside a larger rhythm. A sprint normally lasts between one week and one month, and two weeks is the most common length. At the end the team shows working increments to the people who asked for them, and a retrospective picks one or two improvements for the next round.

The team also keeps a burndown chart on public display, with the days remaining along one axis and the work remaining along the other, so everyone looks at the same picture. According to a summary of the book, a team should be no larger than nine people. At that size a daily round of three answers each can still fit inside the limit.

The design leaves little room for a long report. A person who has been stuck on something since yesterday has to say so in one breath, in front of everyone who could help, and the rest of the team hears it at the same moment.

[[lessons]]
$$**Name the snag early and take the details elsewhere.** A plumber who says on Monday morning that one fitting is missing gives the shop time to order it that day, while one who stays quiet finds out on Wednesday with the pipe already open. Saying what is in your way costs one sentence and does not require you to know the fix. Then the people who can help know the same morning, and the detail can be sorted out in a separate short talk.$$

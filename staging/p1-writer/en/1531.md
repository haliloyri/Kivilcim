[[open]]
A team of about thirty researchers examined 35,000 sales calls in more than twenty countries. What the data showed did not match what most sales training was teaching, and the group waited seven years before it published. Inside that result sits a practical question: how should a sales call end?

[[story]]
Neil Rackham founded the Huthwaite Research Group in 1974 and, with support from Xerox and IBM, raised about a million dollars for a study of selling. *SPIN Selling* (1988) rests on roughly twelve years of that work. The study ran long.

Rackham's findings fit poorly with the training of the day, and several publishers turned the manuscript down at first. Part of the friction sits in a distinction the book makes between small and large sales. Habits that help on a cheap, one-visit purchase can do damage on a big one.

Closing is the clearest case. The book defines it as any seller behavior that implies or invites commitment, so that the buyer's very next statement accepts or declines. On a small, low-priced sale a pressing close can work. On a large one, according to accounts of the research, aggressive closing tends to annoy the buyer and can be ineffective or even harmful.

For large sales Rackham counts success differently, by what the buyer commits to. An Advance is a step that moves the sale forward, an action the buyer actually agrees to. A call that ends with no agreed next step is a Continuation, and he counts it as a failure. Top sellers plan a range of small, achievable Advances before each call.

A request for a proposal does not count as an Advance on its own. Unless the buyer also agrees to some follow-up action, the call is still a Continuation.

The book splits a sale into four stages: Opening, Investigating, Demonstrating Capability and Obtaining Commitment. In a large, complex sale, Investigating matters most. Closing belongs to the last stage. In the calls that went well, summaries say, the buyer did most of the talking. Even the opening follows the size split: a benefit statement up front works better in small sales than in large ones.

Inc. ranked the book first on its 2013 list of the best how-to-sell books, saying it turned selling from an art into a science. Summaries also report that the first group of about a thousand salespeople trained in the method sold roughly 17 percent more than control groups from the same companies. It is a secondhand figure, and the sources do not say who measured it, so it reads best as a claim rather than proof.

[[lessons]]
$$**Aim for a next step you both agree to.** A conversation can end in a yes, a no, or a step that keeps things moving, and in big decisions the last one is usually the easiest to earn. Someone pitching a repair job to a restaurant owner might skip the push for a signature and agree on a day to bring a written estimate. The owner has then chosen the next move, and you leave with a concrete reason to talk again instead of a guess about how it went. Agreeing on that step out loud also tells you whether the interest was real.$$

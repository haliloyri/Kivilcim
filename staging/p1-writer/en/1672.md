Two people sit at their desks for the same eight hours. One of them finishes a piece of work that took real thought, and the other finishes a pile of messages. Cal Newport has a short formula for why the afternoon looks so different for them.

[[story]]
In *Deep Work* (2016), Cal Newport splits professional effort into two kinds. The first he calls deep work: activities done in a state of distraction-free concentration that push your mind to its limit. They produce new value, they improve your skill, and they are hard for anyone else to copy.

The second kind is shallow work. Newport describes it as logistical tasks that do not demand much thought and are often done while distracted: answering email, sitting in a status meeting, filling out a form. These tasks create little new value in the world, and someone else could do them about as well.

Both kinds fill a day. Both feel like work, and the inbox gives the quick satisfaction of something cleared. Yet the two do not trade one for one, and Newport puts the difference into a single line. ##High-quality work produced equals time spent multiplied by intensity of focus.##

Take the line apart. If the focus is low, the hours barely count, and an afternoon with a phone buzzing beside the keyboard can be long and thin. If the focus is high, a shorter stretch can carry far more.

Newport adds a ceiling. Experts, he says, max out at around four hours of deep work a day, and beginners manage far less. That makes the hours precious, because nobody has eight of them. The question for a working day is therefore less how to fit more in than how to keep the few that exist from being spent on the inbox.

The formula does not tell you which tasks deserve the focus. It only says that sitting at a desk is not the same as working there. A calendar full of meetings can look busy and still leave no hour at all for the work Newport means.

[[lessons]]
$$**Count focused hours, not desk hours.** A bookkeeper can spend a whole day at her desk and still have only a short stretch in which she actually reconciled the difficult account, because the phone and the inbox kept pulling her away. If she picks the hardest task and gives it the first clean block of the morning, she will see how much one protected stretch gets done. You can ask of any day how much of it had your full attention, and plan the next one around that answer. Over a week, the pattern shows whether your hours or your focus is the thing running short.$$

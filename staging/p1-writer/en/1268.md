Wake at seven in the morning, stay up through the day, go out with friends that night and drink nothing at all. By the time you drive home at two, a sleep scientist says, your attention on the road matches that of a legally drunk driver.

[[story]]
The scenario comes from Matthew Walker's *Why We Sleep*, built on a study from Australia. Researchers took two groups of healthy adults. One group drank to the legal driving limit of 0.08 percent blood alcohol, the other stayed awake for a single night, and both took an attention test that counted lapses. After nineteen hours awake, the sleep-deprived group was as impaired as the drunk group. The decline had started at fifteen hours.

Walker then turns to the people we trust most when we are vulnerable. Many medical schools required residents to work thirty hours in one go, often twice a week, with twelve-hour shifts in between. Residents on a thirty-hour shift, he writes, commit 36 percent more serious medical errors than those working sixteen hours or less: a wrong drug dose, a surgical implement left inside a patient. After such a shift they make 460 percent more diagnostic mistakes in intensive care.

The danger runs both ways. Exhausted residents are 73 percent more likely to stab themselves with a needle or cut themselves with a scalpel. Driving home, their crash risk rises by 168 percent. Walker asks the obvious question. Would you accept treatment from a doctor who took a few swigs from a hip flask of whiskey in front of you?

##The finding Walker considers most harmful is that tired people cannot tell how impaired they are.## In David Dinges's study, ten nights of six hours' sleep left people performing as badly as after twenty-four hours with no sleep at all. Asked how impaired they felt, participants consistently underestimated it. Walker compares them to someone at a bar who has had far too many drinks, picks up the car keys and confidently says, "I'm fine to drive home."

The book also shows what changes when the hours change. In several American pilot studies, residents limited to sixteen-hour shifts with an eight-hour rest before the next one made over 20 percent fewer serious medical errors. Walker's point is less about willpower than about the clock, since no number of years on the job, he argues, trains a doctor out of the need for sleep.

[[lessons]]
$$**When you are short of sleep, judge your fitness by the hours you have been awake.** Sleep loss lowers attention and also dulls your ability to notice the drop, which makes "I feel fine" the least reliable signal you have. A parent who has been up all night with a feverish child can hand the car keys to their partner for the pharmacy run instead of trusting that feeling, even when it is very convincing. Tying the decision to the clock lets you move risky tasks to the hours before tiredness becomes invisible to you.$$

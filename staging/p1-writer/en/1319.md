Two lists lie side by side on the table. Each one holds every household job one partner does, written down so nothing is left out. One list stops. The other seems to go on forever. The man looking at them had always believed he did his fair share.

[[story]]
Nir Eyal tells this on himself in *Indistractable*, in the middle of an argument about calendars. He starts with the to-do list. It's the usual way to plan: write down everything you want to do and hope you find time for it. Anyone who keeps one, he says, knows how tasks get pushed from one day to the next, and the next.

He suggests starting from values instead, and sorting your time into three life domains: you, your relationships and your work. Then you make time for each. He calls it timeboxing, which simply means deciding what you'll do and when you'll do it. The goal is a calendar with no white space, and success is measured only by whether you did what you planned. As he puts it, you can't call something a distraction unless you know what it's distracting you from.

His own test of this happened at home. He and his wife, Julie, kept bickering about why certain things weren't getting done around the house. Equality was one of his values in the marriage, and he thought he lived it. He was wrong. By his own admission he was one of the husbands who don't do their share of the housework, and that he was often oblivious to the tasks Julie handled.

If she wanted help, why didn't she just ask? Over time he came to see that working out how he could help was itself work, and she already had a dozen things on her mind. He didn't know what to do, so he'd stand there confused or slink off to do something else. Too many evenings followed that script, ending in late dinners, hurt feelings and sometimes tears.

Then, on one of their scheduled date days, they sat down and each listed every household task they performed, making sure nothing was left out. ##Comparing Julie's seemingly endless list with his own was, he writes, a wake-up call.## They agreed to split the jobs, and, most important, they timeboxed each one on their schedules, leaving no doubt about when it would get done.

He uses the same method with his daughter. One afternoon they wrote over a hundred things to do together in town on little strips of paper and dropped them into a "fun jar." Every Friday afternoon they pull one out. He and Julie also schedule a date twice a month.

[[lessons]]
$$**Give the things you value a time slot, not just a spot on a list.** A task without a time tends to slide to tomorrow, however important it is and however high it sits on the page. Two flatmates who keep arguing about the cleaning every other week could put "bathroom, Saturday 10:00" in a shared calendar, and the argument moves from who noticed the mess to whether the slot happened. When something does get skipped, they can calmly talk about moving the time, and the conversation stays about the plan rather than about whose fault it was.$$

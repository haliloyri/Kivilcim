A neurologist takes off his patient's left shoe, scratches the sole of the foot with a key, and steps out of the room to fetch his ophthalmoscope. A minute later, the man has still not put the shoe back on, and he is looking at everything in the room except the shoe.

[[story]]
Oliver Sacks tells it in *The Man Who Mistook His Wife for a Hat*. Dr. P., a singer and then a teacher at the local School of Music, had come to him after an ophthalmologist found nothing wrong with his eyes but trouble in the visual parts of his brain. Sacks was checking his reflexes when he took off the left shoe and left him to put it back on.

Finally Dr. P.'s gaze settled on his foot. "That is my shoe, yes?" he asked. Sacks said no, that was his foot, and pointed out the shoe, and Dr. P. answered, "Ah! I thought that was my foot."

His eyesight itself was good enough to spot a pin on the floor. Sacks opened a *National Geographic* at its cover, an unbroken expanse of Sahara dunes, and asked what he saw. Dr. P. described a river, a little guest-house with a terrace on the water, people dining and coloured parasols here and there. None of it was on the page.

Sacks must have looked aghast, but Dr. P. seemed to think he had done rather well. He decided the examination was over and looked round for his hat. He reached out, took hold of his wife's head and tried to lift it off and put it on, and she looked as if she was used to such things.

A few days later, at their apartment, Sacks held up a glove. Dr. P. turned it over as he had turned over the geometrical solids and said, "A continuous surface infolded on itself. It appears to have five outpouchings, if this is the word." A container of some sort, he guessed, perhaps a change-purse for coins of five sizes. ##He could describe the glove exactly and never once saw a glove.##

At the table there were coffee and little cakes, and Dr. P. ate hungrily, humming, until a loud knock at the door stopped him. He sat frozen, no longer seeing the table as a table, until the smell of the coffee his wife poured brought him back. In the kitchen she told Sacks that he dressed the same way: she put out his usual clothes in the usual places and he put them on singing to himself. "He can't do anything unless he makes it a song."

Asked what was wrong, Sacks said he could not tell him, but he could say what was right: Dr. P. was a wonderful musician, and music was his life. He prescribed a life that consisted entirely of music. Sacks never saw him again, and writes that Dr. P. lived and taught music to the last days of his life.

[[lessons]]
$$**When one route fails, put the task on one that still works.** Dr. P. could not recognise a glove, yet he dressed and ate without trouble as long as a song carried him through it. Someone who loses the thread of a long written plan might follow it easily once a colleague talks it through aloud. Treating the second route as a real way of doing the work, and not as a poor substitute, keeps you moving while the first one stays blocked. The next time a routine stalls, you can ask which of your other routes is still open before you decide the task is beyond you.$$

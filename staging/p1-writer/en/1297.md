[[open]]
A Silicon Valley executive says yes to every request his bosses and colleagues bring him. His calendar fills, his best work keeps slipping, and his performance drops until he starts thinking seriously about quitting. Then a mentor gives him one rule he can check against every new request.

[[story]]
Greg McKeown tells this story in *Essentialism*, about an executive he calls Sam Elliot. Sam said yes to everything, large or small. He went from meeting to meeting and joined the long e-mail debates that followed them. He felt most of his time was going to work that did not move anything forward. His performance slipped, and he seriously considered leaving to become a consultant.

A mentor gave him blunt advice. ##Do only the things you would do as a consultant, and nothing else.##

Sam began to say no. At first he weighed each request by whether he had the time and resources. Later he asked a harder question: was this the most important thing he could be doing at that moment? He stopped going to many meetings, stayed out of most e-mail arguments, and put his effort into a few high-value projects. His work got better and so did his satisfaction. His performance ratings went up and he received a large bonus.

McKeown uses a piece of word history to explain why this is hard. According to him, "priority" entered English in the 1400s and meant the very first thing. It stayed singular for about five hundred years. Only in the 1900s, he writes, did people start talking about "priorities," as if several things could be first at once. Dictionaries confirm the fifteenth-century origin, while the date of the plural is McKeown's own claim.

He also describes a paradox of success. A person with a clear purpose does well, and success turns that person into the one everyone comes to. More options and requests arrive, each needing time and energy. Effort spreads thin, and the clarity that produced the success fades. Success, in McKeown's account, can undo itself. McKeown borrows Jim Collins's phrase for the trap, "the undisciplined pursuit of more."

Sam had walked straight into that paradox. Being good at his job had brought the flood of requests. He never left. The way out was the consultant's filter, applied inside the job he already had, one request at a time.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before accepting a request, ask whether it is the most important thing you could do with that time.** When you know your one priority, every new request has something to be measured against, and it becomes clear what can wait. A teacher invited onto a third committee can remember that her main aim this term is to prepare a new course, and turn the invitation down politely. Asking this one question regularly, especially when you feel flattered to be asked, helps you notice when yeses given in good faith are quietly pushing your best work aside, before your performance tells you.$$

A corporation, one businessman liked to say, is not a building or a factory full of people. It is a file folder with a few legal documents in it, sitting in some attorney's office. He believed that folder decided who paid the most tax, and in what order.

[[story]]
In *Rich Dad Poor Dad*, Robert Kiyosaki starts his lesson on taxes in a classroom. His teacher told the story of Robin Hood as a romantic hero who robbed the rich to help the poor. The man the book calls rich dad, the father of Robert's best friend, called Robin Hood a crook. Robert's own father, a highly educated government official, saw things the other way, and the boy grew up between the two.

Rich dad's history of tax was short. Taxes used to be temporary, raised to pay for wars, against Napoleon in Britain and for the Civil War in America. England made income tax permanent in 1874, the United States in 1913 with the 16th Amendment. In rich dad's telling, both were sold as taxes on the rich only. As government's appetite grew, they trickled down to the middle class and the poor, the very people who had voted for them.

The rich, he said, went another way. Corporations had become popular in the days of sailing ships. Investors put money into a corporation to finance one voyage to the New World. If the ship was lost, the crew lost their lives and the investors lost only what they had put into that trip.

##The heart of the chapter is an order of events: employees earn, get taxed, and try to live on what is left, while a corporation earns, spends everything it can, and is taxed on whatever remains.##

Kiyosaki lists what he means. Car payments, insurance, repairs, health-club memberships, even vacations recast as "board meetings in Hawaii," all paid with pre-tax dollars, all legal in his view. Meanwhile, he writes, the average American works from January to May just to cover taxes.

That needs an editorial note. Critics call this advice oversimplified at best, and running personal holidays or gym fees through a company can break tax law. Rules also vary a great deal by country.

The scene readers remember is smaller. When young Robert repeated his father's advice to work his way up the corporate ladder, rich dad only chuckled. "Why not own the ladder?" He paid good tax accountants and attorneys, because that cost him less than paying the government. The biggest bully, he told the boys, was not the boss but the tax man.

[[lessons]]
$$**Check the order in which money leaves your hands.** What you keep depends on how much you earn and also on which rules touch that money first. A freelance designer who keeps the receipts for the laptop and software she really uses for work, and asks an accountant what counts, can end the year with a different bill from a friend on the same income who never asked. Looking at the order lets you find the legal options that apply to you, and it gives you a reason to check with a professional before copying a bold example from a book.$$

More than a century ago, a Danish physiologist named Christian Bohr described something odd about blood. Hemoglobin lets go of its oxygen more easily when there is more carbon dioxide around it. For anyone who thinks of CO2 as pure waste, that is the detail that does not fit.

[[story]]
James Nestor's *Breath* (2020) spends a chapter called "Less" on the consequence. It is easy to assume that a deeper breath brings in more oxygen and that more oxygen is better. Nestor's argument leans on Bohr's finding to question the second half of that sentence.

The mechanism is simple to state. Carbon dioxide in the blood makes the blood slightly more acidic, and hemoglobin responds by releasing more of the oxygen it carries. When carbon dioxide falls, the blood turns slightly less acidic and hemoglobin holds on to its oxygen more tightly. The oxygen is in the blood, but it stays on the carrier.

##So breathing more can leave less oxygen available to the tissues that need it.## Someone who breathes off a lot of CO2, with big or fast breaths, can end up with hemoglobin that is reluctant to let go. A summary of the book puts the point bluntly: carbon dioxide is essential for respiration, not just a waste product.

The design has a neat logic. Working cells make carbon dioxide and acid, so the tissues that are busiest are also the ones where hemoglobin lets its oxygen go most readily. In the lungs, where CO2 is breathed out, the same molecule picks the oxygen back up. Bohr, incidentally, was the father of the physicist Niels Bohr.

That is the reasoning behind the book's advice to breathe less and more slowly. Nestor describes about 5.5 breaths a minute as the pace to aim for, which leaves carbon dioxide at a healthier level. A second figure from the same summary says that during normal breathing the lungs absorb only about a quarter of the oxygen they take in, so there is slack to work with and no need to gulp more.

The claim is worth holding with some care. The Bohr effect itself is textbook physiology. How far it explains the benefits of slow breathing is Nestor's argument, and the details here rest on summaries of the chapter rather than on its text.

[[lessons]]
$$**Give slow breathing a fair try before gulping air.** After a sprint, you gasp for air, and the gasping feels like what the body wants. After a minute of small, slow breaths in a quiet room, you may notice your thoughts racing a little less. The difference suggests that a bigger breath is not always the useful one. You can try a slower rhythm before reaching for a deep, gulping sigh, and notice which of the two leaves you steadier a few minutes later.$$

I took a risk, I got hurt, how do I get back up? Some version of that email reached a researcher's team every week for two years. Her answer has three stages, and the messiest one sits in the middle.

[[story]]
In the two years after *Daring Greatly*, emails reached Brené Brown's team every week. Readers had taken a risk, been hurt, and wanted to know how to get back up. Brown says she has collected this kind of data throughout her work, and she describes herself as a grounded theory researcher. "I've collected that data all along," she says, "and what I've learned about surviving hurt has saved me again and again." *Rising Strong* answers in three stages: the reckoning, the rumble and the revolution.

The reckoning is walking into our story. All it takes, Brown says, is recognizing that we are feeling something, and the exact name of the emotion can wait. The opposite of recognizing is denying. The body reacts to emotion before the conscious mind does, and it often pushes us to pull away. After the recognizing comes curiosity, and Brown's examples sound like ordinary sentences: why am I being so hard on everyone around me today, and what is setting me off?

The rumble is owning our story. It begins with turning up curiosity, and it is the messiest stage. We get honest about the stories we tell ourselves, test them, and separate what is true from what is only protecting us. She ties curiosity to a 2014 study in *Neuron*, which found that brain chemistry changes when we become curious, helping us learn and remember. Brown describes thoughts, actions and feelings as three legs of one stool, so positive thinking alone does not change behavior while the feelings stay negative. Owning our story and loving ourselves through that process, she writes, is the bravest thing we'll ever do.

The revolution is writing a new ending and changing how we engage with the world. ##The rising strong process is nowhere near as powerful as the rising strong practice##, Brown writes. The uprising starts when the first reaction is curiosity instead of reflex, asking what is going on with me before reacting. Those are small steps, and once they become a daily way of engaging, the change reaches the people around us.

[[lessons]]
$$**Name the feeling before you question it.** Brown's sequence starts with noticing that you feel something, because an unnamed feeling steers the story quietly and nobody else can see it. Someone who notices on the way out of a meeting that they shut the door a little too hard can tell themselves that something set them off. Only then does asking why become useful, and the question stops turning into a defense. Taking the stages in order lets each one do its own work, and afterwards you decide which story deserves testing and which can wait for another day.$$

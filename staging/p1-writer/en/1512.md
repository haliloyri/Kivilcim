At bedtime a Stoic does not empty the mind. The work runs the other way: go through the day, moment by moment, and see how each one went. Even a lapse gets a hearing, and a conditional pardon, before the lights go out.

[[story]]
In *On Anger*, Seneca describes his own evenings: once the lamp has been taken away and his wife has fallen silent, he goes through his whole day. He replays what he said and what he did, and he says that he conceals nothing from himself and omits nothing. William Irvine builds on this practice in *A Guide to the Good Life* (2008), where he calls it a bedtime meditation derived from Seneca.

The method has a lineage. In Seneca's own account in *On Anger*, Sextius used to question his mind at day's end: what bad habit of yours have you cured today, what vice have you restrained, in what way are you better? Irvine's version asks you to review the day's events, how you responded to them, and how a Stoic should have responded. Seneca's stated goal was to reduce his vices and mistakes each day.

Seneca does not dread his faults, because he can forgive a lapse on the condition that it never happens again. He names two of his own. He had argued too combatively in one dispute, so he resolves to stop arguing with people who are unwilling to learn. He had rebuked someone too bluntly and hurt him instead of correcting him, so in future he should weigh whether his words are true and also whether the listener can bear them.

A reviewer's notes give sample questions for Irvine's version. What failing did I resist? Where did I improve? Did I let others upset me? Did I give in to temptation? Did I let go of worry about something beyond my control? Was I careful to internalize my goals? The list sorts the day into what is under complete control, under no control and under some but not complete control.

Meditation here is active reflection on the day, with the mind busy. Irvine finds some forms of Buddhist meditation time-consuming and physically and mentally challenging, while the Stoic review is small enough to do in bed. It also works as a reminder, since techniques such as negative visualization are easy to forget when life is going well.

In an interview, Irvine put the aim plainly. He wants progress and says nobody will ever achieve Stoic perfection.

[[lessons]]
$$**Review the day looking for one adjustment.** A shop owner who snapped at the delivery driver can recall that moment in the evening and look for one thing to change, rather than a verdict on herself, since a verdict gives her nothing to do tomorrow. She might decide that tomorrow she will greet him first and ask about the route. The day stops being a trial and becomes a small correction she can make before breakfast, and she goes to sleep with a plan instead of a grudge.$$

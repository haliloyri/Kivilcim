[[open]]
A teenager is prescribed an antidepressant and told his depression is a shortage of serotonin. The first tablet is 20 milligrams. Over the next few years the dose climbs to 60, and the boy grows into a man who starts asking what was missing from the explanation.

[[story]]
Johann Hari's own prescription opens the story of *Lost Connections* (2018). As a teenager he was prescribed paroxetine, the drug sold as Paxil. A review in the British Journal of General Practice reports what his doctor told him, that depression came from a chemical imbalance in the brain, depleted serotonin.

He kept taking it. The same review says the dose rose from 20 milligrams to 60 over a period of a few years.

The book is his case that the explanation was too small. Hari argues that depression is far from simply a chemical imbalance, though chemistry may be a small part of it. He cites Irving Kirsch's meta-analysis, which he describes as showing a mood improvement of only 1.8 points on the Hamilton scale.

Against that, he sets a list. ##The book names nine causes of depression.## Seven are connections that can be lost: meaningful work, other people, meaningful values, childhood trauma, status and respect, the natural world, and a hopeful or secure future. Two are biological, genes and brain changes. Only two of the nine, in other words, live inside the skull.

The book does not stop at the list. One summary describes a doctor's practice in East London that became a hub for volunteer groups, where patients were offered one of over a hundred ways to reconnect, such as pottery, exercise or gardening, and a group of them turned an urban plot into a garden. The remedy it points to is joining something, in addition to taking something.

Take it as Hari's argument, not as settled science. Critics, among them the psychiatrist Dean Burnett, say he cherry-picked his evidence and overstated how new his findings were, and a reviewer notes that he is a writer, not a scientist. The summaries do not tell anyone to stop a treatment, and neither does this story.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask what a person is cut off from, alongside how they feel.** A flatmate who seems low since he moved cities may have lost the people he used to see on Thursdays and the routines that came with them. Asking about those losses gives you something concrete to talk about, which "how are you feeling" often does not, and the answer may be as ordinary as a Thursday evening that no longer exists. The question sits beside professional care and does not stand in for it, and it lets you notice a missing piece before the weeks have piled up.$$

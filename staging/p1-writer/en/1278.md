"Our educational system is broken." "America is exceptional." "There is no God." These are the answers one investor hears most often when he asks job candidates his favorite question, and he counts every one of them as a miss. The candidates may even be right, but they are saying what many others already say.

[[story]]
The question sits near the start of Peter Thiel's *Zero to One* (2014), and he says he asks it whenever he interviews someone for a job: "What important truth do very few people agree with you on?"

On paper it looks like a five-second question. Thiel explains why it is so hard to answer well. It is intellectually hard because everything we learn in school is, by definition, already agreed upon. It is psychologically hard because the person answering has to say out loud something they know will be unpopular. Brilliant thinking is rare, he writes, but courage is in even shorter supply than genius.

That is why those three common answers fail. The first two might be true, but plenty of people already agree with them. The third simply picks a side in an old and familiar argument. None of them names a truth that only a few people see.

A good answer, Thiel says, fits a single sentence pattern: "Most people believe in x, but the truth is the opposite of x." He offers his own. Most people think globalization will define the world's future. He thinks technology matters more, because spreading today's ways of creating wealth across the whole planet, without new technology, would not be sustainable.

Later in the book the interview question turns into a business idea. ##Every great company, Thiel argues, is built around a secret that is hidden from the outside.## Some secrets are about nature. Others are about people: things they don't know about themselves, or don't talk about. His examples were sitting in plain view. Airbnb saw spare rooms, and Uber saw empty car seats, as supply nobody was using.

So why do so few people go looking? Thiel names four trends. Incrementalism: school teaches us to advance one small, approved step at a time. Risk aversion: a secret hasn't been vetted by anyone yet, so believing it means risking being wrong in public. Complacency: the people with the most freedom to explore new ideas tend to believe in secrets the least. And flatness, the sense that in a global talent pool someone smarter must already have found whatever you just noticed.

[[lessons]]
$$**Run your bold idea through the "most people believe x" sentence.** If you can't finish the sentence honestly, or if half the people you know would nod along, what you have is a common complaint rather than an insight. A nurse who thinks her ward's shift handover is badly designed may write the sentence and realize that every colleague in the break room already says the same thing. Writing it out on paper separates the beliefs you share with the crowd from the few you actually hold alone, and those few are the ones worth testing.$$

[[open]]
"Looks great, let me know when it launches!" A founder can walk out of a meeting with those words and feel it went well. Rob Fitzpatrick puts a second sentence next to it, "Can I buy the prototype?", and says only one of the two tells you anything.

[[story]]
*The Mom Test* (2013) has a chapter on what a customer meeting must produce. It is not a pleasant feeling or a stack of kind words. Fitzpatrick calls the two things he wants commitment and advancement, and a meeting counts as a success only if it delivers one of them.

Commitment means the customer gives up something they value. He lists three currencies. Time: they agree to a next meeting with a goal, give feedback on wireframes, or start a trial. Reputation: they introduce you to a decision maker or a teammate, or put their name to a public testimonial. Money: a letter of intent, a pre-order, a deposit.

Advancement means the conversation moves to the next real step of the sales process, not another round of friendly talk. The two work as a pair. A commitment that goes nowhere is a gesture, and a step forward that costs the customer nothing is a courtesy.

##"If you don't know what happens next after a product or sales meeting, the meeting was pointless."##

Seen this way, the two sentences from the opening belong to different kinds of meetings. "Looks great, let me know when it launches!" asks the customer for nothing and leaves nothing behind. "I would definitely buy that" is the same, a hopeful mood with no date attached. The good endings Fitzpatrick offers are practical ones: "When can we start the trial?" and "Can I buy the prototype?"

Money is not required early on. In the first meetings a commitment of time or an introduction can do the job. One good ending quoted from the book is "There are a couple people I can introduce you to...", which has the customer spend some of their own reputation and hands you a place to go next. The failed ending is made of compliments and fluff only: the customer leaves happy, you leave happy, and nothing has moved.

He also gives a rule of thumb for weighing what you hear: the more they are giving up, the more seriously you can take what they are saying. A person who lends an hour of their calendar has said something small. A person who offers an introduction to their boss has said more, and a person who hands over a deposit has said the most.

[[lessons]]
$$**Judge a conversation by what the other person gave up and what happens next.** A cousin who simply loves your catering idea and cheers loudly has given you a feeling. A cousin who books a tasting date, brings a friend and asks for a price has given you time, reputation and a step forward. When you compare the two, you can see which of your conversations were warm and which were useful, and you can ask for one small next step at the end of the next one, before the glow has faded.$$

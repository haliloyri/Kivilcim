Over Portland, Oregon, a United Airlines jet kept circling while its crew worked on a landing gear problem, and the fuel was dropping the whole time. The flight engineer had mentioned it, and the plane kept going around.

[[story]]
On 28 December 1978 a United Airlines DC-8 was coming in to Portland when the crew lowered the landing gear and did not get a clear signal that it was locked. They held a circling pattern near the airport to sort it out. Matthew Syed uses that flight in *Black Box Thinking* (2015) to show how aviation deals with failure.

The crew's attention stayed on the gear, circle after circle. Meanwhile the fuel dropped. The flight engineer had said it was getting low, but the captain was still working on the gear problem, so two pieces of information sat in the same cockpit and one of them crowded out the other.

The plane ran out of fuel and came down in a wooded suburb, short of the airport. Ten people died, and the plane lay in a wooded suburb of a city it had spent so long circling. It soon became clear that investigators would have plenty to work with, because the aircraft carried recorders. A black box is really two devices, one for the plane's data and one for the voices in the cockpit, and between them they let investigators reconstruct what the crew had been watching.

They found that the crew had lost track of time and fuel, and that some of them had not pressed the captain hard enough. The findings went well beyond one company's files and were shared across the industry, and training changed: crews learned to speak up and captains learned to listen.

Syed sets this beside medicine, where, in his account, errors are less often investigated and shared. After a crash, aviation asks what happened and how to stop it next time before it asks who is to blame. That habit is what the book means by black box thinking.

[[lessons]]
$$**Write mistakes down where others can read them.** In a restaurant kitchen, a mislabelled allergy order that gets quietly corrected tends to come back the following week with a different cook. If the same slip goes into a shared log, every cook learns the new check. A record like the Portland recordings turns a mistake from one person's embarrassment into something the whole team knows, so the same slip does not have to happen to each of them in turn. Someone who writes the slip down early also finds it easier to describe calmly, because by then the story has a clear beginning, a cause and a fix.$$

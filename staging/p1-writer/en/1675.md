"No thanks, I'm trying to quit." "No thanks, I'm not a smoker." Two people hold the same cigarette offer and give nearly the same answer. According to James Clear, the difference between those two sentences is worth more than it sounds.

[[story]]
In *Atomic Habits*, published in 2018, Clear sets the two answers side by side. Both people are being offered a cigarette, and both say no. Judged by what they do, it is a tie, since neither of them smokes. Clear says the tie hides something, because the sentences come from two different pictures of the self.

The first person still sees himself as a smoker who is attempting to behave differently. Each refusal is a small fight against what he takes to be his real inclination. For the second person, smoking is no longer something he does, so there is nothing left to resist.

##One person is fighting a habit, and the other has stepped outside it.##

To explain why this matters, Clear describes three layers of change. The outer layer is outcomes, meaning what you get, such as the weight lost or the book finished. The middle layer is processes, meaning what you do day to day. The innermost layer is identity, meaning what you believe about yourself. Most people begin at the outside, choose a target and push toward it. Clear argues that change which lasts begins at the center, with identity.

How do you change an identity? Telling yourself "I'm that person now" does not do it. Clear's answer is that every action you take is a vote for the type of person you want to become. One vote does not decide the election. A single workout does not make you an athlete, and a single skipped cigarette does not make you a non-smoker. The votes accumulate, though, and what you need is a majority, not a unanimous result.

That is why he proposes two steps. First decide who you want to be, then prove it to yourself with small wins. The small wins matter because they are the votes.

Clear also points to the origin of the word. The English word for identity comes from Latin roots meaning "being" and "repeatedly," so identity is, literally, repeated beingness. You became a smoker the same way, by doing it over and over until it felt like you.

[[lessons]]
$$**Describe your goal as who you are.** Someone who says "I'm trying to save money" fights a small battle every month, while someone who says "I'm a person who sets aside part of each paycheck" is simply carrying on with something. The behavior can be identical, but the second speaker no longer treats it as an exception to his normal self. If you listen to how you phrase your own goals for a few days, you will notice where you are still negotiating with an older version of yourself, and you can decide whether to change the sentence.$$

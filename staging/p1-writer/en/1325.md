[[open]]
Pinned above a writing desk is a crude cartoon of the shark from Jaws, with a thick X drawn through it. It looks like a joke a child might make. It is actually homework, assigned by a teacher of blocked artists to anyone who keeps hearing a certain voice.

[[story]]
In *The Artist's Way*, Julia Cameron gives that voice a name: the Censor. She describes it as a nasty internal and eternal critic, the voice that follows every sentence you write and every line you draw and whispers that it falls short. She places it in the logical side of the brain, the one that likes order and judgment.

Her advice is to turn it into a character, instead of trying to silence it. ##"Think of your Censor as a cartoon serpent, slithering around your creative Eden, hissing vile things to keep you off guard."##

If a serpent doesn't work for you, she suggests another cartoon figure, perhaps the shark from Jaws. You draw it, put an X through it, and post it wherever you write or work. Making the Censor into the nasty, clever little character that it is, she says, begins to pry loose some of its power over you.

Behind the drawing is a plain claim. Cameron reminds her readers that the Censor's negative opinion is not the truth. The voice says something; saying it doesn't make it so. The crossed-out shark keeps that distinction in front of you every day you sit down to work.

The rest of her toolkit points the same way. Morning pages, three handwritten pages first thing every morning, are partly a way of getting past the Censor. You spill out of bed and onto the page before it's fully awake, and since there's no wrong way to write them, its opinion has nothing to hold on to. Beyond the reach of its babble, she writes, we find our own quiet center.

In the first week of her course, Cameron also asks readers to look backward. They list the historic "monsters" who damaged their creative self-worth, and the "champions" who encouraged it. The list shows that some of the sentences we hear in our own heads were first said by someone else.

A cartoon shark with an X through it is a small thing to pin to a wall. But once the critical voice has a cartoon face, it gets harder to mistake it for your own.

[[lessons]]
$$**Give the critical voice its own shape.** Once your inner critic has a name or a face, you start to hear it as one voice among several, rather than as the final word on your work. A keen home cook who hears "this will be bland" before every new recipe could name that voice after a fussy relative and taste the dish anyway, as an experiment. The voice may keep talking, but you are the one who decides whether the attempt goes ahead, and fewer of your projects get abandoned before they have even begun.$$

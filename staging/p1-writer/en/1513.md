[[story]]
In the 1980s Seagate's engineers built a 3.5-inch disk drive and carried it to the customers who bought most of the company's products. Those customers weren't interested. They wanted more storage in the drives they already used, and the small one held less.

Clayton Christensen laid out the pattern in *The Innovator's Dilemma*, after studying the disk drive industry as it shrank from one drive size to the next. Drives came in 14-inch, 8-inch, 5.25-inch and 3.5-inch generations. Each time a smaller size arrived, the companies that led the old size mostly did not lead the new one, and newcomers took their place.

The odd part is how the old leaders behaved. They were well run. They asked their best customers what they needed, they put money behind what those customers asked for, and they pushed capacity higher every year. Christensen's argument is that these habits were the reason they lost.

A smaller drive did worse on the one measure the big customers cared about. Mainframe and minicomputer buyers wanted capacity, and a drive that held less was no use to them. So the leaders judged the small drive a poor product, and by their customers' standards it was.

Elsewhere, buyers wanted something different. Makers of desktop computers, and later of portable ones, cared about size and weight and could live with less capacity. Newcomers sold to them.

##Once those buyers existed, the small drives kept improving.## Over the years their capacity climbed until it covered what the original customers needed, and those customers moved to the smaller, cheaper drives. The old leaders, who had spent years perfecting the large ones, were now holding the wrong product.

Christensen reads this as a flaw in the logic of listening, not in the people doing it. A company that follows its best customers looks in the direction those customers already face, and the buyers who matter next are often standing somewhere else.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask who is not buying yet.** A product is usually judged by the people already using it, and they tend to ask for more of what they have. A repair shop that fixes only what its regulars bring in will hear no complaints from the people who gave up on repairs and now throw things away. Spend one conversation this month with someone outside your usual customers or colleagues, and write down what they use instead of you and why. That answer is often the earliest sign of where your own regulars will go later.$$

The Hadza of Tanzania live on tubers, baobab fruit and honey, and a Stanford microbiologist has set what that does inside their gut against what a city diet does inside ours. The main number in the comparison is an estimate.

[[story]]
The Hadza are a population of just over a thousand people in Tanzania's Rift Valley, fewer than two hundred of whom still follow the traditional way of life. Their diet centers on meat, berries, baobab fruit, tubers and honey. In *The Good Gut* (2015), Justin and Erica Sonnenburg say the Hadza eat 100 to 150 grams of fiber a day. That is an estimate, not a measurement.

Justin Sonnenburg, who works at Stanford, has put that figure beside ours. Speaking of the industrialized world in 2017, he said, "We average 15 grams per day." The book also cites a comparison in which adults in the United States had the least diverse gut microbiota, set against two nonindustrial populations in South America and Africa.

Why would fiber matter so much to bacteria? The book describes microbes fermenting it into short-chain fatty acids, and it cites a study of people aged 76 to 95 in which more fiber went with more of those acids. One summary of the book adds that when fiber is scarce, gut bacteria turn to feeding on the mucus lining of the gut and weaken its barrier.

Later work from the Sonnenburg lab followed the Hadza for about a year. Researchers collected 350 stool samples from 188 people. Some bacterial species fell in the wet season, when honey supplied much of the calories, and came back in the dry season, when tubers peaked. Samples from the same season a year apart were nearly identical.

In mice, the same lab found that a loss can be permanent. Animals moved from a high-fiber to a low-fiber diet saw about 60 percent of their bacterial species fall in number, and after four generations on low fiber some species did not return when fiber did. Researchers have not shown that this loss harms health, and mice are not people.

[[lessons]]
$$**Feed your gut a variety of fibers.** Someone who eats the same bowl of cereal and the same sandwich every day offers the same few microbes the same few meals. Beans one day, an unfamiliar vegetable another, and whole grain instead of white rice give more kinds of bacteria something to eat, since each kind lives on slightly different fibers. You can add one fiber-rich food you rarely eat to this week's shopping list and notice how your body feels after a week.$$

The fuel gauge was going down in a cockpit where two of the crew knew it. The captain's attention was on a landing gear problem. They had tried to tell him about the fuel, and their voices had not carried far enough, and the subject dropped.

[[story]]
On 28 December 1978, United Airlines Flight 173, a DC-8, was approaching Portland, Oregon, when the crew ran into a problem with the landing gear. The indicator that should have shown the gear locked did not confirm it. They held in the air and worked on the problem, circling for a long time away from the runway.

While they did, the plane ran out of fuel. It came down in a suburban neighbourhood near Portland, There were 189 people aboard. Ten died, and the rest survived.

Afterwards the cockpit voice recording, the black box, showed what had been said inside. The first officer and the flight engineer had tried to alert the captain to the fuel situation, but out of deference to him they had not pressed the point.

##Someone knew about the fuel, and there was no way to make the captain hear it.##

The US National Transportation Safety Board, which investigated, summed up the cause this way: the captain had failed to monitor the fuel, and the others had not communicated their concern effectively.

After the crash, crew resource management, or CRM, took shape in aviation. The idea is simple: crews learn to speak up, and captains learn to listen. Today it is a standard part of airline crew training. In 1979 NASA held a workshop on resource management on the flight deck, and in 1981 United Airlines began a comprehensive programme.

In *Black Box Thinking*, Matthew Syed presents aviation as an industry that learns from its failures. The difference, as he tells it, is that accidents are investigated independently and the findings are shared across the whole industry. The black box that gives the book its title stands for that approach. This crash is one of the best-known examples of that learning.

[[lessons]]
$$**Do not hint at a concern, say it plainly.** If you see a risk in a plan your boss likes and settle for an indirect remark such as "we might also want to look at a few things", what you said may not be heard. On Flight 173 the warning was tried but not pressed. When you name the risk in one sentence, with the number or the fact attached, the other person is far more likely to take it seriously.$$

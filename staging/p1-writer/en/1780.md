[[open]]
Netflix's expense policy is five words long, and its vacation policy is shorter still: there is none. Behind both is a company that once cut about a third of its people and expected the rest to slump. They did not.

[[story]]
After the dot-com crash dried up venture capital, Reed Hastings laid off about a third of Netflix's staff, including some who were doing adequate work. He expected morale to collapse. In *No Rules Rules*, written with Erin Meyer, he reports that the people who stayed were energetic and produced high-quality work. He concluded that removing less-than-stellar workers raised talent density.

Rules came out next. An employee questioned the vacation policy, and Hastings removed it entirely in 2003: Netflix neither allots vacation days nor tracks them. Managers were asked to give context, such as no vacation within two weeks of a deadline and two weeks' notice for a five-day vacation. Leaders took long vacations openly so others would feel free to do the same. Employees did not take much more time off than under the old policy.

The expense policy came down to five words: act in Netflix's best interest. It replaced an earlier wording, spend company money as if it were your own, which had made people too frugal. The book's example is economy class on a flight, unless an employee must present at a conference early the next morning after a redeye, when business class is justified. There are safeguards. Managers regularly sample employees' expenses, employees know they could be checked at any time, and when someone is fired for overspending, managers tell the staff.

Hastings says this freedom works only where talent density is high and feedback is candid. Managers apply a keeper test: if this person said they were leaving for a similar job at a peer company, would I fight to keep them? If the answer is no, Netflix lets the person go with a generous severance, reported at about four to nine months. It pays each person at the top of the market for their role, with no bonuses. The book treats staying silent when you have useful feedback as disloyal to the company.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask what a rule is covering for.** A café owner who requires her signature on every refund after one bad week can ask whether the real gap was one clerk or missing guidance on what counts as a fair refund. If it was guidance, a two-line note replaces the signature and customers stop waiting at the till. Checking the reason before keeping a rule lets you remove the control while keeping the thing it was meant to protect. It also stops a rule from outliving the bad week that created it.$$

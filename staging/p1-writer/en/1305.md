"Nobody knows anything," the screenwriter William Goldman said about which films would sell. In a book about rare, unpredictable events, that line sits close to the story of a well-fed turkey that would have been better off with the same attitude.

[[story]]
In *The Black Swan*, Nassim Nicholas Taleb borrows a problem from the philosopher Bertrand Russell, who told it with a chicken. Taleb uses a turkey. The turkey is fed every day by the same hands. Each feeding firms up its belief that the general rule of life is to be fed by friendly humans looking out for its best interests.

On the afternoon of the Wednesday before Thanksgiving, something unexpected happens to the turkey. ##Its feeling of safety was at its highest at the very moment the risk was greatest.## The chart in the book is titled "One thousand and one days of history." A thousand days of data told the turkey nothing about day one thousand and one. Worse, its experience had taught it the wrong lesson.

Taleb's answer to the turkey is the barbell strategy he used as a trader. Instead of putting money into "medium risk" investments, he suggests placing about 85 to 90 percent in extremely safe instruments such as Treasury bills, and the remaining 10 to 15 percent in extremely speculative bets.

The logic is about the floor. No Black Swan can push you below the safe part, while the small speculative part stays open to large surprises. The average is still medium risk. What changes is that the unexpected can now help you more than it hurts you.

He sorts whole activities the same way. In film, some kinds of publishing, scientific research and venture capital, you can lose small and win big, because any given book or film might take off for reasons nobody predicted. In banking, lending or catastrophe insurance, surprises mostly hurt. You lend money, and the best case is that it comes back. If the borrower becomes very rich, you do not get an extra dividend.

That is where Goldman comes in. He knew he could not predict how any single film would do. He also knew that the rare unexpected hit would pay him enormously, so the uncertainty worked in his favor.

Taleb's summary for all of this is one line: invest in preparedness, not in prediction.

[[lessons]]
$$**Make the losses you cannot survive very safe, and keep your bold bets small and cheap.** Caution belongs where a bad surprise could finish you, and boldness belongs where a surprise can only help. An illustrator who covers her rent with a steady part-time job and spends a few evenings a week on her own picture books loses little when a book flops and gains a lot if one unexpectedly takes off. Splitting things this way helps you spot the places where a long calm stretch has quietly made you depend on a single support.$$

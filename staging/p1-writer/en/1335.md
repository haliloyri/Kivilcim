[[story]]
In a newspaper interview about his book Everything Is F*cked, Mark Manson told about this woman, and the example comes from that interview rather than from the book's pages. She was in her late twenties and had dated ten men without one lasting result. Manson put a question to her. Would she rather date twenty men she felt nothing for, or meet two she connected with?

She chose the two. He told her to cut 80 per cent of the men she normally considered and to set her standards by values. He added that the difficulty and the fear of doing that cut are part of what makes the commitment worth having.

The idea behind it sits in a line credited to the book: self-limitation is the only ethical form of freedom. Reading notes on the book describe a contrast between fake freedom and real freedom. Fake freedom means more stuff and more options, and it keeps people on a hedonic treadmill. Real freedom involves limits and asks what you are willing to give up.

In the interview he pushes the point further. Freedom, he says, comes from eliminating options rather than adding more, and abundance leaves people in distraction, envy and FOMO. He defines real freedom as the ability to choose your commitments and what you give up.

He calls limiting yourself a key challenge for the next ten to fifteen years and compares it to an information diet. Follow fewer people, he says, and hold higher standards for whom you trust and whom you listen to. A few good sources beat many mixed ones.

Awareness comes first, and he warns it will be uncomfortable. After that comes the cut itself, and the question he suggests asking is simple: what is not serving me?

Manson says he wrote the book after the 2016 US election, when he felt the internet's honeymoon was over. At his talks, he says, dating questions have come back, and he calls them all problems of fake freedom. These are the author's own comments, made around the book, and they are worth reading as that.

[[lessons]]
$$**Choose what to give up.** Keeping every option open tends to scatter attention, while a shorter list gives you something to commit to and see through. A freelancer who says yes to every request and burns out might let go of most clients and focus on a few, which leaves room to do deeper work for those few and to get some of the lost time back. That makes it easier to see why the tiredness came and which work is actually worth the effort. After a few months the freelancer can compare income and energy and decide whether the shorter list stays.$$

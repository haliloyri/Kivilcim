Two sociologists collected roughly 500 million tweets, written by about 2.4 million people in 84 countries over two years. They were not interested in what people tweeted about. They wanted to know how the mood of the words shifted with the hour, and the answer came out the same almost everywhere.

[[story]]
Daniel H. Pink opens *When: The Scientific Secrets of Perfect Timing* with that study, by Scott Golder and Michael Macy of Cornell. Positive language, the kind that showed people feeling active, engaged and hopeful, rose in the morning. It fell in the afternoon. In the evening it climbed again. The pattern held across continents and time zones. On weekends the morning peak arrived about two hours later, as you'd expect from people sleeping in, yet the afternoon slide still came. Work alone could not explain it.

Pink names the three phases peak, trough and recovery. Most people, larks and the in-between group he calls "third birds," move through the day in that order. Night owls run it roughly in reverse, starting their day in something closer to recovery. For most people, Pink writes, analytic sharpness peaks in the late morning or around noon, and the slide that follows is the trough.

The trough shows up well beyond social media. Pink cites an analysis of more than 26,000 earnings calls from about 2,100 American companies. Calls held in the afternoon were more negative, irritable and combative than calls held in the morning. The researchers linked that tone to weaker share-price reactions, so the hour of a call was costing companies money.

##Then comes the twist: different kinds of work fit different points on the curve.## For analytic tasks that need focus and logic, most people do best at their morning peak. For insight tasks, the puzzles that need a sudden "aha," the result flips. Larks and third birds solve them better in the late afternoon or early evening, when they are not at their sharpest. Pink calls this the inspiration paradox.

His explanation is simple. When we are a little groggy, the mind's guard is down, stray thoughts get in, and unexpected connections become easier to make. The same looseness that hurts careful analysis helps a creative leap. That is why Pink keeps pairing two questions. What are we doing, and at which hour of our own curve are we trying to do it?

[[lessons]]
$$**Match the kind of task to the hour.** Work that needs checking, calculation and steady attention tends to go best during your sharpest hours, while open-ended idea work often goes better when you are slightly tired and your mind wanders more. A freelance designer can review invoices and contracts first thing in the morning and leave new logo sketches for the early evening. Splitting an ordinary working day that way puts each kind of thinking where it has the best chance, instead of forcing every task into whatever slot in the calendar happens to be free.$$

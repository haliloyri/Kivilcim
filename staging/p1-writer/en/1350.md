The normal in-service schedule was simple: four short lessons in microsurgery, all in one day. For half of a group of surgical residents, the lessons were spread out instead, a week apart. A month later the difference showed up on the operating table, in the pulsing aorta of a live rat.

[[story]]
Peter C. Brown, Henry L. Roediger III and Mark A. McDaniel use this study in *Make It Stick* (2014) as their vivid example of spaced practice. Thirty-eight surgical residents took four short lessons on reattaching tiny blood vessels. Each lesson mixed some instruction with some practice.

Half of the residents did all four lessons in a single day, which was how the hospital normally ran this kind of training. The other half did the same four lessons with a week between each one.

A month after the last session, everyone was tested. The residents who had spaced their lessons outperformed the others on every measure: how long the surgery took, how many hand movements they needed, and how often they succeeded in reattaching the severed, pulsating aortas of live rats. ##Among those who had learned in a single day, 16 percent damaged the rats' vessels beyond repair and could not complete the surgery.## According to the authors' notes, the results led the medical school to rethink its habit of cramming a technique into one intensive session.

The authors explain the gap through consolidation. For new learning to last, its memory traces have to be strengthened, given meaning and connected to what you already know, a process that unfolds over hours and may take several days. Rapid back-to-back practice leans on short-term memory. When you come back after a little forgetting, the extra effort it takes to retrieve the skill triggers consolidation again and makes the memory stronger.

Their answer on the length of the gap is practical: long enough that practice doesn't become mindless repetition, and long enough that a little forgetting has set in. Not so long that retrieving it turns into learning it all over again.

The catch is how spacing feels. Because some forgetting has happened, you have to work harder to recall, and it doesn't feel like you're on top of things. The authors describe studies in which people did better with spaced practice yet still believed they had learned more from massed practice. Cramming, they say, has been likened to binge-and-purge eating: a lot goes in, and most of it comes right back out.

The book offers a homely version of the method. The German scientist Sebastian Leitner arranged flashcards in four boxes. Cards you often get wrong sit in the first box and get practised most often, cards you know well get practised less, and any card you miss goes back to box one.

[[lessons]]
$$**Let a skill fade a little before you practise it again.** The effort of pulling something back from memory is what fixes it in place, and that effort only appears once some time has passed. Someone learning a new chord change on the guitar who spreads the practice over several days will fumble the first attempt each time, yet a month later their fingers find the change more reliably than if they had drilled it all in one evening. Treat that first clumsy try as a sign the method is working, and plan the gaps between sessions as carefully as the sessions themselves.$$

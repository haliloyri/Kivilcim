At its peak Kodak employed more than 145,000 people directly. Instagram, a company built on digital photographs, had 15. Two economists put those numbers side by side to show something that growth figures alone hide, and the pair of firms is only the start.

[[story]]
In *The Second Machine Age* (2014), Erik Brynjolfsson and Andrew McAfee compare two photography companies. The book continues their earlier *Race Against the Machine* and was a New York Times bestseller, shortlisted for the Financial Times Best Business Book of the Year Award. Kodak employed over 145,000 people directly, a third of them at its headquarters in Rochester, New York, and supported thousands more through dealers and distributors. Instagram, built on digital photo data, had 15 employees.

The authors use the pair to argue that the digital economy can create value without creating many jobs. The reason lies in the nature of digital goods, which, as they describe them, can be copied at close to zero cost, perfectly and instantly. A second copy of a photo, a song or a program takes almost nothing from anyone, not even a shelf, so a very small team can serve a very large audience. The authors point to a newer case: Forbes.com earnings previews that algorithms generate without any human involvement.

These two ideas carry the middle chapters of the book. Their word for the upside is bounty: the growing number, diversity and quality of goods available at falling prices. Much of it is free to the user, and the authors say GDP is inadequate for measuring it. In practice, something people value but do not pay for barely shows up in the figure.

The downside is what they call spread: growing differences in wealth, income and mobility between people. They name three drivers. Technology favours skilled workers, who gain the most, income from capital rises relative to income from labour, and superstar effects let the best performer in a market take a disproportionate share.

Their prescription is to maximise bounty while limiting spread. The Washington Post, reviewing the book at the time, found the policy proposals reasonable but thought they came straight from talking points that tech executives had long been peddling, a reminder to read the prescription with care.

The two photography companies show both sides in one pair: value created at one end, and a far smaller payroll at the other, and the question of who shares in it left open.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask what one more customer costs before you judge a plan.** A solo app maker and a print shop both land a big new batch of customers, and the print shop has to hire for it while the app maker barely notices. Before you judge a business idea or a career move, ask how much work one more customer adds, hour by hour. That shows you where the value will pool, and it tells you what has to be arranged for the people who do not share in it, if you want the plan to hold up.$$

A father and his daughter have already folded a paper airplane out of a page of their activity book. The next question is one they both have to answer: "If you could have any superpower, what would it be?" Years later he still can't tell you what she said.

[[story]]
Nir Eyal opens *Indistractable* with that afternoon. His daughter is their only child. The two of them were working through an activity book designed to bring dads and daughters closer: first naming each other's favorite things, then building a paper airplane from one of the pages, then the superpower question.

He was physically in the room. His mind was somewhere else. "Daddy," she asked, "what would your superpower be?" He grunted. "Just a second. I just need to respond to this one thing." His eyes stayed on the screen, his fingers tapping at something that could definitely have waited. She went quiet. By the time he looked up, she was gone.

He admits it wasn't a one-off. The same scene had played out countless times. After he found her and apologized, he decided technology was the problem and went to extremes to remove it.

He swapped his smartphone for an old-school flip phone, and soon missed GPS, the addresses saved in his calendar and the audiobooks he listened to on walks. To stop wasting time on news sites, he subscribed to a print newspaper; a few weeks later there was a neat stack of unread papers beside him while he watched the news on TV. To write without interruptions, he bought a 1990s word processor with no internet connection. Every time he sat down at it, his eyes drifted to the bookshelf, and soon he was flipping through books that had nothing to do with his work.

##"I'd just replaced one distraction with another."## Removing online technology hadn't worked, and that failure is where the book's argument begins: distraction starts from within. Most of the time, Eyal argues, what we are escaping isn't the device's pull but some discomfort of our own.

He borrows four steps from the psychologist Jonathan Bricker. Look for the discomfort that comes right before the distraction. Write the trigger down, with the time, what you were doing and how you felt. Explore that sensation with curiosity instead of contempt. Be extra careful in the in-between moments when you switch from one thing to the next.

His own weak spot is the urge to google something in the middle of writing. It's easy to call that research, he says, when deep down he knows it's usually a way out of difficult work. So he uses a ten-minute rule. Giving in is allowed, but only later. He waits ten minutes, riding the urge without obeying it, and by then the urge has usually passed.

[[lessons]]
$$**Find the feeling before you blame the device.** You can swap the phone for a flip phone or the laptop for an offline machine, but the wish to escape a hard moment stays with you and finds a new exit. A student who deletes a game while studying might notice that she now tidies her desk whenever an essay gets difficult, and start jotting down the moment and the feeling when it happens. Once she can see the feeling, she is dealing with the real problem rather than chasing distractions one app at a time.$$

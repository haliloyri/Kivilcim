On the calendar on his wall, next to the date of his next workout, a crisp hundred-dollar bill is taped flat. A ninety-nine-cent lighter sits nearby. Every morning he has a choice between two kinds of burning, and he has never once struck the lighter.

[[story]]
Nir Eyal describes this setup in *Indistractable*, and the problem behind it was almost embarrassing. His apartment complex had a fully equipped gym, free for residents. He couldn't blame traffic or membership fees. Even a long walk would have been better than nothing. Yet somehow he kept finding reasons to skip his workouts.

So he made what he calls a price pact with himself. First he set aside the time in his schedule. Then he taped the hundred-dollar bill to the calendar next to his upcoming workout and bought the lighter. Each day he could either burn the calories by exercising or burn the bill. Unless he was certifiably sick, those were the only two options he allowed.

Whenever petty excuses came up, the bill on the wall reminded him of the deal. He expects the reader's objection: that's too extreme, you can't burn money like that. That, he says, is exactly the point. ##He reports using this "burn or burn" technique for over three years, gaining twelve pounds of muscle, without ever burning the hundred dollars.## It's his own account of his own habit, not a controlled result.

Eyal calls tools like this precommitments and traces them to the Odyssey. Ulysses wants to hear the Sirens' song without steering his ship onto their shore, so he has his crew tie him to the mast. The decision is made in a calm moment, before the test arrives.

The book offers another kind, the effort pact. Inventors David Krippendorf and Ryan Tseng wanted to stop their own late-night snacking, so they built kSafe, formerly Kitchen Safe: a plastic container with a timer lock in the lid. Put the cookies in, set the timer, and the lid stays shut until it runs out. You could smash it with a hammer or run out to buy more cookies, but the extra effort makes both less likely.

Eyal attaches a warning to all of this. Pacts come last, after dealing with internal triggers, making time for what matters and cutting back external triggers. He also pushes back on a popular idea: that willpower is a limited tank drained by every decision. He reports that the theory has been seriously challenged, and that in a study by Carol Dweck's team, signs of depletion appeared only in people who believed willpower runs out.

[[lessons]]
$$**Make the decision once, while you're calm, and set a cost for breaking it.** When you're tired, almost every plan opens up for renegotiation, but a decision with a price already attached leaves very little to argue about. A writer who keeps skipping her early-morning hour could tell a friend she'll buy them a coffee for every morning she misses, and write the deal somewhere they can both see it. On a sleepy morning she then picks between two options she chose in advance, and spends her energy on the writing instead of on the excuses.$$

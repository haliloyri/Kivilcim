A salesperson can walk through a product's speed, price and efficiency and still be answering questions the buyer never asked. According to a summary of one sales book, the buyer is busy with two worries instead: losing money, and making a mistake.

[[story]]
Brian Tracy's *The Psychology of Selling* (2006) rests its picture of the customer on one claim, as a summary of the book puts it: all buying decisions are emotional rather than rational. The person who appears to be weighing specifications is, in this view, answering to something else. The book opens with a count: the top 20 percent of salespeople earn about 80 percent of the profession's money, and Tracy puts the gap down to a small, steady edge in skill, like a horse that wins by a nose and collects ten times the prize of second place.

Tracy's list gives a seller five wants to listen for and two worries to ease. He lists what pulls a buyer toward a purchase: money and security, personal growth, love and companionship, health, and status and prestige. None of them appears on a spec sheet.

Two fears pull the other way. The first is losing money, overspending on something the buyer judged badly. The second is making a mistake, buying something they won't want, don't need, or that won't work as expected. Both are fears of a regret that has not happened yet.

That is why Tracy builds a presentation around what a product does for the buyer rather than what it is. His format is show, tell, ask, and he tailors the pitch to six types of buyer.

Research outside Tracy's book suggests the claim is bolder than it needs to be. Neuroscientist Antonio Damasio described a patient, Elliot, in *Descartes' Error* (1994). After surgery to remove a tumor, Elliot's intellect was intact but his emotions had gone flat, and everyday decisions began to fail. According to summaries of the case, he lost his job, was drawn into ill-advised money-making schemes and ended up bankrupt. Damasio's explanation is that emotions, felt as bodily states, act as a fast guide for decisions.

That supports Tracy's point that feeling is part of choosing. It does not show that reasoning plays no part, which is what the word "all" would need. A seller who treats the buyer as a person with hopes and worries is on firm ground, and facts still have a job to do.

[[lessons]]
$$**Find the mistake they are afraid of.** Behind a hesitant "let me think about it" there is often the fear of one specific mistake, and a person rarely says which one unprompted. Someone choosing a laptop for a small shop may be circling the spec comparison when what worries them is paying for features they will never use. Asking what would count as a mistake lets you speak to that worry directly, and the facts you add afterward land on something the person actually cares about, instead of answering a question the person never asked you.$$

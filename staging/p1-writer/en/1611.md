The index and the people who owned funds in it were not paid the same. Across twenty-five years the S&P 500 averaged 12.5 percent a year, and the average fund investor came away with 7.3. The gap did not come from a single bad year.

[[story]]
John Bogle gives the comparison a chapter of its own in *The Little Book of Common Sense Investing* (2007), and he calls it "The Grand Illusion." The span is the 25 years from 1980 to 2005. In it the S&P 500 averaged 12.5 percent a year, while the average fund investor earned 7.3.

Put those two rates on $10,000 and the difference becomes visible. Taken as steady yearly rates, 12.5 percent turns the money into about $190,000 after the 25 years. At 7.3 percent it is about $58,000. That is arithmetic on the two averages, not a figure from the book, but it shows how much sits inside a gap of 5.2 points.

Where does the difference go? Costs account for only a part of it.

Most of the rest comes down to what investors do. Bogle points to timing and fund selection as the main cause of the gap. The pattern is easy to picture: an investor sees a fund have a strong stretch and buys it. Later the fund has a weak stretch, and the same investor sells.

Each move feels reasonable at the time, because it follows the latest result. Added up over twenty-five years, the moves leave the investor with 7.3 percent while the index they could have simply held made 12.5.

The title points at what readers are told and what they receive. The quoted return of the market is one number. The return people actually earn, with their purchases and sales in it, is another.

A separate survey points the same way. Dalbar looked at the 20 years ending in December 2010 and found stock fund investors earning 3.83 percent a year against 9.14 for the S&P 500. That survey is not Bogle's, and it covers a different stretch, but the gap has the same shape.

[[lessons]]
$$**Your own buying and selling is part of your return.** A shop owner who reorders only what sold best last week can end up with full shelves just as customers move on. The product she carries matters, but so does the habit of chasing the latest result. Before you move money, write down what changed in your plan, apart from a recent price, and let that sentence decide whether you act. If you cannot finish it, wait a day and look again.$$

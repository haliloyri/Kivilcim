The first dates were good, and so was the first month. A few weeks later the same person's voice began to grate, and habits that had gone unnoticed started to look like proof of a mismatch that nobody could have seen at the start.

[[story]]
Amir Levine and Rachel Heller describe this pattern in *Attached*. It begins well: early dates feel exciting and close, and the chemistry is there. Then the partner moves closer, and the mood in the relationship begins to change.

If the person has an avoidant style, this is the point where he or she starts noticing flaws. It might be the partner's voice, or a habit, or one of the small things that do not quite fit, and soon the thought arrives that the two are not really compatible. Small things start to loom, and each one seems to confirm the last.

Levine calls these deactivating strategies, automatic moves that push closeness away. His list includes dwelling on a partner's small imperfections and wondering whether someone else would be a better match. ##Deactivating strategies run as a reflex, without a plan.## Because they run on their own, the avoidant person may take them for sound judgement about the partner, when they say more about what closeness sets off.

It hurts the avoidant person as well. He or she wants closeness and feels pushed to retreat from it at the same time. The partner sees good evenings followed by a sudden chill, with no reason given, and may try harder to get close, which sets the reflex off again. Often neither of them can say what happened.

Levine's way out begins with recognition. Seeing the style, and telling yourself that this is your own pattern and not a verdict on the partner, is the first step toward changing it. Once the reflex has a name, the next flaw that appears can be treated as a signal to pause and not as a conclusion.

[[lessons]]
$$**Check an irritation against the calendar before you act on it.** A woman who finds herself annoyed by her new partner's laugh right after a warm weekend can write the irritation down and ask whether it would have bothered her a few weeks earlier. If it would not, she has learned something about her own reflex and not only about him. That gives her room to wait a day before deciding anything, and to notice whether the feeling fades or stays. If it stays, she will at least know it has lasted past the first rush of closeness, and she can raise it with him calmly.$$

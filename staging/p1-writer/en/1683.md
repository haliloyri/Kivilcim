Some leaders listen beautifully and still make a mess of the plan. Others see the whole market and misread the person across the table. Daniel Goleman traces such gaps to attention itself, which he says points in three different directions.

[[story]]
In *Focus*, Goleman begins with other focus, the attention that smooths the connections between a person and the people around them. Empathy lives here, and he splits it into three kinds. Cognitive empathy means understanding what another person thinks without feeling it yourself. Emotional empathy means feeling what they feel. Empathic concern is the compassion that moves you to help. Social awareness belongs here as well: reading body language and adjusting your behavior to the nonverbal cues in the room.

Inner focus points the opposite way, at oneself. It attunes a person to intuitions and guiding values. Goleman folds self-awareness into it: living by your values and purpose, following your gut, noticing where your own attention goes, and perceiving accurately how others see you. Willpower belongs here too. Resisting instant gratification for a long-term goal can be done by shifting focus away from the thing you want, by focusing on something else, or by keeping the longer goal in mind.

Outer focus lets us navigate the larger world. Goleman calls the skill systems awareness, and it takes practice. You detect the patterns of a system, map them, and move back and forth between the details and the big picture. He argues that large-scale problems such as climate change need this kind of attention, together with a long view.

For a leader, he says the three have to work together. Inner focus helps a leader assess strengths and weaknesses and manage the impact on others. Other focus helps with listening and sensing the energy of a team. Outer focus helps decide what to concentrate on and what to ignore. Goleman also holds that attention can be strengthened, which makes each of the three something a person can work on.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask which direction you skipped before you decide.** Under pressure, attention tends to run one way, and the information the other directions would have given never reaches the table. A cafe owner choosing whether to open a second branch might study only the profit figures, and so miss both her own hesitation and what her staff are thinking. A short look at yourself, at the people affected and at the wider picture before you commit shows which piece of information is still missing.$$

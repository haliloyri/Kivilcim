Five pairs of opposites with an awkward acronym decide what a picture should say, and the first thing they ask is not what the idea is but what the picture is for. Each pair asks you to lean toward one side.

[[story]]
Dan Roam's *The Back of the Napkin* (2008) holds a small toolbox for deciding what a picture should say. One part is a set of five filters with an awkward name: SQVID. Each filter is a pair of opposites, and you lean toward one side or the other.

First comes simple versus elaborate, which settles how much detail the picture carries. Next is quality versus quantity, which covers how the idea is expressed. Then vision versus execution sets the level at which you present it, and individual attributes versus comparison sets how you position it. Last is delta, meaning change, versus status quo, which decides what you emphasize.

There are two ways to run them. You can go through the pairs one by one to focus the idea itself, or you can weight each pair toward what matters most to the audience, which Roam calls audience focusing.

A second set sits beside the filters, and the whole toolbox is counted 3-4-5-6: three tools, four steps, five filters, six questions. The questions, who or what, how much, where, when, how and why, each have a matching picture: a portrait, a chart, a map, a timeline, a flowchart and a multiple-variable plot. Roam writes that for each of the six ways of seeing there is one corresponding way of showing. In his sequence the six questions belong to seeing, the five pairs to imagining, and the picture itself to showing, which has three steps: choose a framework, develop the idea, draw it and share it.

The book tries the tools on a case study, SAX Inc., an accounting software company whose sales are stagnating as competitors gain ground. In a review in the Globe and Mail, a training manager at a new company uses the same six questions on the firm's many training programs: who gets trained, where the lessons happen and when they occur.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask which side your audience needs before you draw.** A cafe owner bringing a one-page plan to her landlord can first work out whether the landlord wants a single change or a comparison with last year. She then draws only that side and leaves the rest for the conversation. The page stays uncluttered, and the picture serves the person reading it rather than the person who made it. Asking the question takes a minute and spares both of them a slide nobody needed.$$

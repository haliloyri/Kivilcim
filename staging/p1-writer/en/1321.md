[[open]]
A three-year-old wants to see the photograph now. The right answer fits in one sentence, and her father knows it. What he did next was leave that sentence unsaid and treat her question as a problem worth working on.

[[story]]
In 1943, on a family vacation in Santa Fe, Edwin Land took a photograph of his daughter. She was three years old, and she asked why she could not see the picture right away.

An expert answer was ready: that is how the camera works. ##Land did not leave it there.## Land later said he set about solving the puzzle she had set him. In Warren Berger's account he went for a walk, and within an hour he reached the "what if" of instant photography. Berger frames it as a question: what if you could have a darkroom inside a camera?

The hour was the short part. The practical details took far longer, and in Land's own words the work ran from 1943 to 1973. The first Polaroid camera went on sale on 26 November 1948, five years after the question. Land's company later became one of the "Nifty Fifty" growth stocks of the 1960s.

Berger puts the scene at the head of a three-question sequence: why, what if, how. The daughter's question was the why. Land's walk produced the what if, and the years that followed were the how. He sets these open questions against closed ones such as how many, how much and how fast, which keep the status quo in place. Why, he says, is the question companies ask least.

Polaroid itself filed for bankruptcy in 2001, a casualty of photography's digital shift, and Berger presents that as a warning about leaning on closed questions. He adds that schools and business cultures tend to reward rote answers over questions. The book's title comes from a line by the poet E.E. Cummings.

Land is not the only case. Berger traces other breakthroughs to a question too. In the Gatorade story, a college football coach asked why his players weren't urinating more, which led to the realization that sweating players were not replenishing fluids well enough. Berger also traces Netflix and Pixar back to founding questions. Hal Gregersen's research, which he cites, found that highly innovative leaders, among them Steve Jobs and Jeff Bezos, question everything.

[[lessons]]
$$**Hold the question before you give the answer.** When Land's daughter asked why she could not see the photo, a ready explanation existed and the question could have ended there. A shoemaker whose customer asks why a repair takes so long can say that this is how the shop runs, or can keep the question beside the work for a few days and look for the real reason, which is the part a stock answer would have skipped. Spending a short time with the question before answering often turns it into a list of things that could change, which a ready answer never produces.$$

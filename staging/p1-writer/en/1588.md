Doctors treat cancer, then bone loss, then failing eyesight, each as its own case. David Sinclair says they keep playing whack-a-mole with one underlying cause, and he gives that cause a name.

[[story]]
In *Lifespan* (2019), David Sinclair argues that aging should be classed as a disease. He calls it the common factor behind conditions from cancer to osteoporosis. The disease-by-disease approach, in his telling, has stretched lifespan without stretching the healthy years, and leaving aging off the disease list sends research money to the wrong places.

His explanation is the Information Theory of Aging. Cells carry two kinds of information. DNA is digital: copied reliably and intact enough that a cell can be reset to a young state. The epigenome is analog. Methyl groups sit on DNA and block certain genes, and that pattern drifts in a predictable way with age. Sinclair says the loss of this epigenetic information is the root cause of aging.

Reviews trace the idea back to yeast. As a young scientist in Leonard Guarente's lab, Sinclair saw that when DNA breaks, the Sir2 protein leaves its post at the mating-type genes. The cell has to drop one job to fix another, and Sinclair read in that a clue about how information gets lost.

The book treats reset as a prospect. Partial reprogramming with Yamanaka factors could in principle wipe the drifted methylation pattern, but nothing in 2019 had reached a person.

After the book, in December 2020, a *Nature* paper from his lab put three factors, Oct4, Sox2 and Klf4, into the retinal ganglion cells of mice. Youthful methylation patterns returned, and vision improved in a glaucoma model and in aged mice. Mice only.

Not everyone is convinced. Charles Brenner wrote a letter to *Cell*, titled "The information theory of aging has not been tested," about a 2023 paper from Sinclair's lab. A reviewer on a pro-longevity site adds that the theory leaves out factors such as advanced glycation end products.

[[lessons]]
$$**Look for what several problems share before you fix each one.** A baker whose bread, rolls and cakes all come out dry could rewrite three recipes, yet the oven thermostat may be the culprit. Setting the different faces of one trouble side by side lets you see whether one cause sits under all of them. Then you can ask that question before you patch a third time, and you spend your effort on the single fix that might help everywhere.$$

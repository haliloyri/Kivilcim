One kind of question showed up more often in the calls that failed: questions about the buyer's current setup. According to accounts of the research, the more of them a seller asked, the lower the chances of success. Diligence, it turned out, had a cost.

[[story]]
Neil Rackham's team spent about twelve years on the research behind *SPIN Selling* (1988), with roughly thirty researchers in more than twenty countries. Across 35,000 sales calls, what the sellers asked sorted into four kinds. Situation, Problem, Implication, Need-payoff. The name is the list.

Situation questions gather facts about the buyer's circumstances. Asking many of them went with lower success, and senior buyers in particular dislike being quizzed on facts. The successful sellers did their homework before the call, so they needed fewer of these. A seller who walks in with the facts already in hand can spend the call on the buyer's problems.

Problem questions explore the buyer's difficulties and dissatisfactions. They bring out what Rackham calls implied needs, weak signals such as a general worry that the buyer has not yet turned into a request. Experienced sellers asked more of these and asked them earlier. One source adds that raising several problems is safer than fixating on one, because a single focus can invite objections about the weak spots of the offer.

Implication questions follow a problem to its effects and ask how serious they are. Summaries single these out as the most powerful, because they raise the buyer's motivation to change, and skill with them does not improve just from experience. Top sellers brought in their solution late, once the effects of the problem had been talked through.

Need-payoff questions ask the buyer to say what a solution would be worth. That turns an implied need into an explicit one, a specific want the buyer has stated aloud. Benefits the buyer states carry more weight than the seller's, and they sound less pushy. Only then does a benefit, in Rackham's sense, exist: a statement of how the offer meets a need the buyer has already voiced.

In the successful calls, the buyer did most of the talking. One summary adds that open questions, a favorite of sales trainers, showed no link to success. Huthwaite describes SPIN as a flexible framework rather than a fixed sequence, so the four kinds work as a map, not a script.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask what the answer would be worth to them.** People weigh the value they state themselves more heavily than the value someone else tells them. A teacher who wants a parent to try a new homework routine can ask what would change at home if the evenings stopped turning into a fight, and then let the parent answer. The reasons the parent gives are theirs, so the teacher can build the plan on them instead of on a pitch. It also gives you a way to check your own pitch: if the other person cannot say why it matters to them, you have probably been doing the explaining for them.$$

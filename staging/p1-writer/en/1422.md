Set a timer for five minutes. For that long you may cry, complain or scream, whichever the bad news calls for, and nobody should tell you to calm down. When it rings, you say three words out loud and the time is up.

[[story]]
That is the Five-Minute Rule in Hal Elrod's *The Miracle Equation* (2019), and it belongs to the part of the book about emotional pain. When something bad happens, you do not wave it off and you do not stay in it. You give it a fixed window and a clock, and the clock is the part that does the work: the feeling is allowed, and it has an end that you can watch approach.

Inside the window nothing is off limits, not even the loudest option on the list. Elrod's own list is crying, complaining and screaming, and the point is that you are allowed to do whichever one you need. The feeling gets its full five minutes before anything else is asked of you.

##When the timer ends, you say "can't change it" out loud and move on.## The sentence is short on purpose. It is spoken, not thought, so it works as a marker that the window has closed. Nothing in the rule asks you to pretend the event was fine.

Behind the rule sits a claim about where pain comes from, and it is the reason the rule is shaped this way. Elrod says suffering is the direct result of resisting our reality. Emotional pain, in his view, rarely has to do with what actually happens to us and more to do with how we respond to it. The event itself is over once it has happened, and what keeps hurting is the resisting.

That is why the closing words matter more than the five minutes. "Can't change it" is an act of accepting a fact, and Elrod is careful to say what acceptance is and is not. He separates peace from happiness. Peace means accepting what you cannot change, and it does not require you to be happy about it.

So the rule asks for two things that people often treat as opposites. It lets you be as upset as you like for a limited time, and it asks you to stop fighting the facts once that time is over. Elrod puts the second step in a sentence short enough to say aloud, and lets the clock do the work of ending the first.

[[lessons]]
$$**Give a bad moment a fixed length and an ending line.** A cook whose dish comes back from the table with a complaint can spend the rest of the shift replaying it, or she can step into the back, let herself be angry for a few minutes and then say the words that close it. The time limit lets her feel the sting without handing it the whole evening. And once the sentence is said, the next order on the rail becomes the only thing in front of her, and the dish that came back no longer sits in her hands.$$

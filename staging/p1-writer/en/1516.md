Your mom will almost certainly tell you your business idea is good, because she wants to support you. That yes comes with love attached and almost no information inside. A whole book is named after her, and it asks what you should say to people instead.

[[story]]
Rob Fitzpatrick's *The Mom Test* (2013) starts from that yes. A mother hears your idea and answers out of loyalty, so she makes the perfect example of someone who will mislead you without meaning to. His claim is wider than mothers, though: almost anyone can hand you a useless answer, out of kindness, or because they do not know their own preferences, or because nothing is at stake for them.

So the book moves the work onto you. It gives three rules. Talk about their life instead of your idea. Ask about specific things that happened in the past, not generalities or opinions about the future. And talk less, listen more. Fitzpatrick sums up the split in one line: they own the problem, you own the solution.

Then he takes three questions founders love and shows what is wrong with each. "Do you think my idea for a product that does X is any good?" asks for a verdict, which is only an opinion. "Would you buy a product that did X?" asks about a future the person will never have to test. "How much would you pay for a product that did X?" invites a guess.

His replacements are dull in the best way. Ask how they handle X today and which products they use. Find out whether X is a real pain or something they mostly ignore. Ask how much time and money it costs them now, and whether they have ever paid for a fix. Another line he offers is "Talk me through the last time X happened."

Fitzpatrick's list of good questions goes on. "Why do you bother with X?" "What else have you tried for X?" "Who else should I talk to?" Each one points at what people have already done, not at your idea. A warning follows from that: if they haven't looked for ways of solving the problem already, they're not going to look for, or buy, yours.

##Once you start talking about your idea, they stop talking about their problems.##

Pitching feels like progress. The moment you do it, the conversation is about your idea again, and a polite person will answer the idea instead of describing their own week. That is why the first rule keeps your idea out of the room.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask about last time, not about your idea.** A woman planning a dog-walking service learns more by asking neighbours how they covered the dog during their last holiday than by asking whether they would use her service. The answer names a real week, a real cost and a real workaround, which a polite person cannot invent just to please her, and none of it depends on how much the neighbour likes her. With a past event on the table she can see how much the problem actually hurts, and she can drop a weak idea early without feeling let down by a friend.$$

"Think not so much of what thou hast not as of what thou hast." The sentence comes with an instruction attached. Pick out the best of what you have, and consider how eagerly you would go looking for it if it were gone. A Roman emperor set himself that exercise, and then added a warning.

[[story]]
Marcus Aurelius wrote the note in Book Seven of the private notebook we now read as *Meditations*. The warning follows in the same breath. While enjoying these things, he tells himself, take care not to grow so used to them that you overvalue them, so as to be disturbed if you should ever lose them. He wants to see what he has more clearly and to cling to it less, both at once. The note does not say which things he had in mind. He leaves the list for himself to fill in.

Book Eleven pushes the same thought somewhere harder. Marcus copies a saying from Epictetus: when a man kisses his child, he should whisper to himself, "To-morrow perchance thou wilt die." The objection follows at once, that these are words of bad omen. Epictetus answers that no word is ill-omened if it describes a work of nature. ##Otherwise it would be ill-omened to speak of the ears of corn being reaped.##

The note just before it is blunter still. To look for a fig in winter, Marcus writes, is a madman's act, and so is looking for one's child when that is no longer allowed. The one right after it turns to the vineyard. The unripe grape, the ripe bunch, the dried grape: all are changes, he writes, into something which does not yet exist.

Read side by side, these notes keep the kiss and the whisper together. The thought of loss arrives during the embrace and does not take its place.

Book Two applies the same measure to his own life. Since it is possible that he may depart from life this very moment, he tells himself to regulate every act and thought accordingly. In the same note he adds that leaving the company of men, if there are gods, is nothing to be afraid of, because the gods will not involve him in evil.

Today this kind of exercise is often given a Latin name. That name does not appear in Marcus's notebook, which he wrote in Greek. What appears instead is more concrete: a child's cheek, a fig tree in winter, ears of corn ready for the reaping, and a short list of good things worth noticing before they are gone.

[[lessons]]
$$**Picture something you have as if it were gone.** Things and people we see every day slowly turn invisible, and we often notice them only after we lose them. Someone who grumbles every morning about a creaky old bicycle can pause for a moment and imagine getting to work without it, and the same bicycle starts to look different. That brief thought lets you see the value of what you already have without rushing out for something new. It also prepares you a little for the day you might lose it, so the loss does not knock you over completely.$$

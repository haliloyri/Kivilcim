Fingers tingle, the head goes light, and the person doing it is told this is normal. That is the opening minute of a breathing drill whose best-known trial was small, and which has been promised more than that trial showed.

[[story]]
The basic breathing exercise in *The Wim Hof Method* (2020) takes about twenty minutes, ideally before breakfast on an empty stomach. You sit or lie down, never near water and never standing in the shower, because the book warns it can make you faint. Breath holds are not to be forced.

You take 30 to 40 deep breaths, filling the belly, then the chest, then the head, and letting each one out without force. Tingling or lightheadedness is described as normal, as carbon dioxide in the blood falls.

After the last exhale you hold your breath as long as is comfortable. When you feel the urge to breathe, you inhale fully, hold that breath for 10 to 15 seconds and let it go. Then you go through the cycle again, three or four rounds in all. The holds tend to get longer, and a hold timed before and after a round shows the difference.

##In 2014 a trial at Radboud University put the method under controlled conditions.## Twenty-four healthy volunteers were split 12 and 12: one group was trained in meditation, breathing and cold exposure, the other was not. Everyone then received a dose of E. coli endotoxin. The trained group reported fewer flu-like symptoms, had higher levels of an anti-inflammatory marker and lower levels of inflammatory ones.

The claims around it ran ahead of the evidence. It was one small trial. A 2024 meta-analysis of eight studies of the method, with 15 to 48 people each, rated their quality very low. Hof told the media that research showed his body could neutralize bacteria faster, which the study did not conclude.

The risk is real too. This kind of breathing can cause fainting, and in water that can be fatal: practitioners have drowned.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask how many people were tested before you copy a health method.** A friend swears by a supplement and offers to share it. Before you start, ask how many people tried it, whether anyone was compared with people who did not take it, and who ran the test. Counting heads and controls takes a minute, and it tells you whether you are hearing a result or a recommendation. The same question works on a diet, a gadget or a morning routine you meet online, and the answer is often a few friends and no comparison group at all.$$

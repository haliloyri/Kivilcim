For one academic year, 216 students let researchers log everything they did online, anonymously. At the end, the logs were laid next to one other piece of information. The difference that stood out wasn't in games or videos, though those showed up too. It was in the least exciting place on anyone's screen: the email inbox.

[[story]]
Nir Eyal uses this study in *Hooked: How to Build Habit-Forming Products* to show how feelings drive our clicks. It took place in 2011 at the Missouri University of Science and Technology. Two hundred and sixteen undergraduates volunteered to have their internet activity tracked anonymously for the academic year, and the researchers measured how often they went online and what they did there.

At the end of the year, the team compared that data with records of which students had visited university health services for symptoms of depression. No names were attached at any point; the tracking stayed anonymous from start to finish.

One of the authors, Sriram Chellappan, described what they found. Participants with depressive symptoms "tended to engage in very high email usage." They also watched more video, played more games and chatted more. In Eyal's summary, people with symptoms of depression used the internet more.

Why would that be? Eyal offers an answer and labels it a hypothesis. People with depression may feel negative emotions more often than others, and turn to technology for relief. The study shows the two things travel together. It doesn't show that email causes low mood, or that it cures it.

For Eyal the pattern fits something larger. Negative emotions, he argues, are among the most powerful internal triggers. Boredom, loneliness, frustration, confusion and indecision each bring a small itch, and the itch prompts an almost instant and often mindless action. Bored people reach for dramatic headlines. Stressed people look for calm on Pinterest. Lonely people find instant company on Facebook and Twitter, and uncertainty sends us to Google. Positive feelings can work the same way; wanting entertainment is a way of escaping boredom.

##Email he calls perhaps the mother of all habit-forming technology, because checking it tells us whether someone needs us.## Sometimes that confirms we matter. Sometimes, he writes, it simply confirms we exist.

Once a product is tied to a feeling, Eyal says, people come back without any prompt. That tie doesn't form overnight. It takes weeks or months of frequent use before the feeling and the product are linked in memory.

[[lessons]]
$$**Catch the feeling behind the repeated check.** A lot of the checking we do in an ordinary day answers a passing feeling rather than any real need for new information. Someone who realizes, after a week of paying attention, that they refresh their inbox mostly on evenings when they feel left out can pick up the phone and call a friend instead, which deals with the feeling directly. Knowing which feeling sends you to the screen does not ban the screen, but it turns an automatic move into a choice you can make on purpose.$$

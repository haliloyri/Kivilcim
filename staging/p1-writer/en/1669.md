Nobody bites into a raw potato. Thich Nhat Hanh takes that plain kitchen fact and uses it to explain what to do with anger. The answer has less to do with the potato than with the fire under it.

[[story]]
The Vietnamese monk Thich Nhat Hanh compares anger to a potato in his book *Anger* (2001). A raw potato cannot be eaten. It goes on the fire first, and only then does it become something that belongs on a plate. Anger, in his picture, is not something to swallow the moment it arrives, and not something to hand to anyone else.

There is nothing wrong with a potato. It is simply not ready. He looks at anger the same way: not as an enemy to be destroyed but as something that needs preparing, and that view changes what you do with it from the first minute.

The fire is mindfulness. When anger comes up, he says to begin mindful breathing right away: air in, air out, with the anger held in your attention while it cooks. He adds that simply embracing your anger and breathing in and out is enough. It needs no preparation and can be done in the chair where you are sitting.

Then comes the mouth. His second instruction is to try not to say or do anything while you are angry. The sentence that was ready to leave is the raw potato itself, and set in front of the other person it feeds neither of you.

He uses a second image as well: anger as garbage. Garbage becomes compost, and compost becomes a flower. The two pictures end in the same place. Anger left untouched stays raw, and anger that is looked after turns into something else.

The book's subtitle, *Wisdom for Cooling the Flames*, is about putting out the fires of anger. In the potato picture the fire is a different one: mindfulness, which cooks rather than burns. The two fires sit side by side in the same book, one that scorches and one that slowly makes the potato ready for the plate.

He also treats anger as a signal that something needs taking care of, and so cooking it does not mean pushing it out of sight. It means giving it the attention it was asking for.

[[lessons]]
$$**Let anger cook before you serve it.** A customer who is annoyed by a mix-up at a repair counter can take two slow breaths before answering instead of snapping at once. The sentence that follows the pause is usually shorter and clearer than the one that was ready. That gives you a say in what leaves your mouth, even on a bad day, and it lets you notice what the anger was actually about. Over a week of small irritations, that habit saves you several sentences you would have wanted back.$$

A paycheck arrives on the 1st. By the 7th, a savings account, a Roth IRA and the monthly bills have all been paid, and the person the money belongs to has not made a single decision since payday. Every step was set up once, ahead of time.

[[story]]
Ramit Sethi, author of *I Will Teach You to Be Rich* (2009), sums up his approach to automation in a flat statement: it is not about willpower. His reasoning is that people who mean to save still end up doing nothing, because the daily decisions wear them down. Automation takes those decisions away.

He points to a 401(k) study in which making enrollment opt-out instead of opt-in raised contribution rates from under 40 percent to nearly 100 percent. That finding concerns retirement enrollment, and no system promises the same jump everywhere. But it explains why he builds a flow instead of a pep talk.

Step one is linking accounts. The 401(k) connects to checking through direct deposit, and checking connects to a Roth IRA, a savings account and a credit card. Step two is asking companies to move their bill dates to payday, so that everything runs on one schedule.

Then comes the monthly rhythm, with payday on the 1st in his example. The 401(k) portion is withheld, and take-home pay lands in checking, where he suggests keeping a $500 buffer at first. On the 5th a transfer goes to savings, sized by his conscious spending plan, typically 5 to 10 percent of income. A second goes to the Roth IRA, roughly 10 percent of take-home pay. On the 7th, autopay handles the recurring bills.

Sethi prefers paying those bills by credit card and then paying the card in full from checking, with an email notification so he can review the statement before the payment is pulled. If you cannot afford the savings figure yet, he says to start with $5.

##The plan has four buckets: fixed costs, savings, investments and guilt-free spending.## Once the first three are covered automatically, what remains in checking is yours to spend.

[[lessons]]
$$**Decide once, then let it run.** A shop owner who pays a supplier by hand every month will sometimes pay late, while one who sets the payment for a fixed date never has to remember it. The principle is to put the decision into the setup, where you make it once with a clear head, instead of into each month, where it competes with everything else. That frees your attention for the choices that really change from month to month.$$

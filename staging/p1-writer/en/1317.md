A pigeon in a box presses a lever and a pellet of food drops out. It learns fast. Then the rule changes: sometimes the lever pays, sometimes nothing happens at all. What the bird does next is the opposite of what most people would guess.

[[story]]
In *Hooked: How to Build Habit-Forming Products*, Nir Eyal goes back to the 1950s and the psychologist B.F. Skinner, who wanted to understand how variability changes animal behavior.

Skinner started simply. He put pigeons inside a box rigged to deliver a food pellet every time they pressed a lever. The pigeons learned the cause and effect. Press, eat.

Then he added variability. Instead of a pellet for every tap, the machine released food after a random number of taps. Sometimes the lever gave food. Sometimes it didn't.

##According to Eyal, the intermittent reward dramatically increased the number of times the pigeons tapped the lever.## The less predictable the payoff, the more often the birds did what the box was built to make them do. Eyal adds that more recent experiments link this kind of variability to rising activity in the nucleus accumbens and spikes of dopamine, which he says drives our hungry search for rewards.

From the pigeon he moves to the screen. Variable rewards, he proposes, come in three kinds. Rewards of the tribe are social: feeling accepted, important, included. Rewards of the hunt are resources, the modern versions of food, such as money and information. Rewards of the self come from mastery, from finishing something and feeling competent.

Email, he argues, uses all three at once. We don't know who has written, and we feel a social duty to reply, which is the tribe. A message might carry an opportunity or a threat to our work, which is the hunt. And the inbox itself is a task, a fluctuating count of unread messages that we feel compelled to sort and clear and bring under control, which is the self.

His other example is the feed. Twitter's timeline mixes the mundane with the relevant. Sometimes there's a genuinely interesting piece of news, sometimes there isn't, and all it takes to keep hunting is another flick of the finger. He counts slot machines among the classic examples of the hunt, paying out at random intervals that the gambler cannot control.

The distance between a pigeon tapping a lever and a person refreshing an inbox over morning coffee, in Eyal's account, is shorter than it looks.

[[lessons]]
$$**Know which reward you're chasing, and decide when you'll look.** When you can't predict what a check will bring, the uncertainty alone keeps pulling you back, even when nothing important is likely to be there. A freelance designer who refreshes her inbox all day could set two fixed times for client mail, one before lunch and one late in the afternoon, and close the tab in between. Naming what you hope for, whether a person, an opportunity or an empty inbox, helps you notice the pull and lets you choose the moment to look instead of letting the lever choose it.$$

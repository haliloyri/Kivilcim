[[story]]
Peter Wohlleben manages a beech forest in Germany, and in *The Hidden Life of Trees* he starts with a patch of it. In a preserve of old beeches there were some mossy stones, gently curved, with hollowed-out areas. He had walked past them many times. One day he stopped and bent down.

Under the moss, one of the "stones" turned out to be bark, and the pieces formed a ring about five feet across. They were what remained of an enormous ancient stump whose inside had long since rotted into humus.

Wohlleben puts the felling of the tree at least four to five hundred years back. Beech wood on damp forest ground normally rots within a few years. He scraped the bark with his pocket knife and found a green layer beneath it, and green meant chlorophyll, so the wood was alive, with no leaf anywhere to feed it.

His reasoning is plain. After that many centuries a stump with no leaves could not have any sugar of its own left, so something had to be feeding it. He concludes it was the neighbouring beeches, passing sugar to it through their roots. In an interview he put their age at about 200 years. He admits he cannot know for sure, but he thinks it is really possible that they are the stump's own offspring.

Under the soil, he writes, there are also threads of fungi. In the Harz Mountains of Germany, scientists had found that most trees of one species in the same stand are linked through their roots. By the research he cites, trees can also tell their own roots from those of other species, and even from those of related individuals. A single teaspoon of forest soil holds many miles of them, and they link trees to one another. To Wohlleben, trees in a forest behave less like lone competitors and more like a community. Along city streets, where roots get cut, those links break and the trees stand as singles.

Later scientific reviews say some claims about this kind of sharing are overstated. That the stump was green under its bark is Wohlleben's observation; that its neighbours fed it is his reading of it. The argument goes on.

[[lessons]]
$$**Notice who keeps you going, and give something back.** A teacher who gets through a hard term might realise that a colleague quietly covers her playground duty whenever she is behind with marking. Thanking that colleague, or taking over a duty for her on a busy day, turns a favour she takes for granted into a tie she looks after. Support then runs in both directions, and she stops trying to hold the whole term up by herself, which also tells her whom to lean on when the next hard stretch comes, and what she could offer in return.$$

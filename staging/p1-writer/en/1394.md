On paper a visit to a key customer, a report for a board meeting, an unimportant phone message and a lunch with a coworker each take one line of the same size. A writer on time management says they are not equal at all, and he sorts them with five letters before the day begins.

[[story]]
Brian Tracy's *Eat That Frog!* spends its third chapter on a number. The 80/20 rule is named for the Italian economist Vilfredo Pareto, who first wrote about it in 1895, and Tracy carries the pattern over to your to-do list: about 20 percent of your tasks hold about 80 percent of the value of what you do.

Put ten items on a list, he says, and two of them may be worth as much as the other eight together. Often one item is worth more than the other nine combined. The jobs may take the same time, yet one or two bring five or ten times the value of the rest. That item is the frog.

People tend to do the opposite. Most of us put off the few valuable items and keep busy with the many trivial ones, which is how someone looks occupied all day and finishes little. Tracy's rule fits on a sticky note: resist the temptation to clear up small things first. Before starting, ask whether the job sits in the top twenty percent or the bottom eighty.

##To sort the list, Tracy uses five letters.## You write tomorrow's list, thinking on paper, and put an A, B, C, D or E in front of each item before you begin the first. An A is something you must do, with serious consequences either way, such as visiting a key customer or finishing the report your boss needs for a board meeting. These, he says, are the frogs of your life. With several, you number them A-1, A-2, A-3, and A-1 is your biggest, ugliest frog.

A B is something you should do, with only mild consequences: returning an unimportant phone message, reviewing email. Tracy calls these tadpoles, and the rule is that you never do a B while an A sits undone. A C would be nice, with no consequences either way, like coffee or lunch with a coworker. A D can go to someone else, and an E can be dropped altogether, often a task kept out of habit or because you enjoy it.

Once every line has its letter, the instruction is short. Start on A-1 immediately and stay with it until it is finished. Tracy suggests practising the method every day for a month, so that it hardens into a habit.

[[lessons]]
$$**Rank each task by what happens if you skip it.** A shop owner who looks at the day's errands side by side tends to treat them as equals and starts with the one that puts up the least resistance, which is usually the easiest to tick off. If she writes beside each what would actually go wrong if it were left undone, some turn out to be jobs someone else could take, and others could simply be dropped. She begins the day knowing which single job cannot wait, and the busy-looking small ones stop deciding her morning.$$

In the middle of a business meeting, a tool magnate stood up, convulsed, and died at fifty-four. His eighteen-year-old son inherited three quarters of the company and then did something the rest of the family fought. He bought them out, putting up his own assets and nearly all the company's cash, while the law still counted him as a minor.

[[story]]
Ryan Holiday uses Howard Hughes in *Ego Is the Enemy* as the case of a man who was handed a great deal and spent it. His father's company held patents and leases critical to oil drilling and was worth nearly a million dollars. The son, described as quiet, reserved and sheltered, put up his own assets and nearly all the company's funds to buy his relatives' shares. He ended up owning a business that would throw off billions in cash profit over the next century.

Holiday does not question the talent. Hughes was a mechanical genius, one of the bravest pilots of early aviation, and a filmmaker who could see whole industries changing. What he did with the money is the trouble. He left Houston and never set foot in the company's headquarters again, treating it as a till. In Los Angeles he decided to become a film producer and a celebrity.

Trading stocks from his bed, he lost more than eight million dollars in the run-up to the Depression. *Hell's Angels* took three years and lost $1.5 million on a $4.2 million budget, nearly bankrupting the tool company. Another four million went on Chrysler stock in early 1930. Then came aircraft. His two wartime contracts, worth forty million dollars, failed, and the Spruce Goose took more than five years and roughly twenty million to build, then flew once, for barely a mile, seventy feet above the water. It sat in an air-conditioned hangar in Long Beach for decades, at a million dollars a year.

The studio he bought next, RKO, lost over twenty-two million while its staff shrank from two thousand to fewer than five hundred. When he tired of defense contracting and handed it to executives, it began to thrive, in Holiday's words because of his absence.

That is the detail Holiday leans on. A bad businessman usually fails and disappears, so nobody gets to see what sank him. Hughes kept floating on profits from his father's company, which he found too dull to meddle with, and so the damage his ego did to himself, to the people around him and to what he wanted to build stayed in plain view. A young Joan Didion wrote that making a hero of him tells us something about ourselves. Holiday agrees, and his verdict is blunt: ego wrecked him.

[[lessons]]
$$**Check how the work runs without you.** Hughes's defense business began to thrive once he handed it off, which suggests his own grip was holding it back. A shop owner who approves every order and every price can test this by taking two days off and reading the order log when she returns. If the work went fine, some of those approvals were habit and pride, and she can let them go. If it did not, she now knows exactly which decisions still need her, and she can keep those without guarding the rest out of reflex.$$

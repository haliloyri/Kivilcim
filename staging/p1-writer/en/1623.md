One partner is on the third text of the evening. The other has just said, in a perfectly polite voice, that he needs some space. Neither of them thinks they are the problem, and in Amir Levine and Rachel Heller's account, neither of them is.

[[story]]
In *Attached*, published in 2010, Levine and Heller sort people into three attachment styles: secure, anxious and avoidant. Secure people are comfortable with closeness and with distance. The other two styles are where the trouble starts, because an anxious person and an avoidant person keep ending up together. The authors call it the anxious-avoidant trap.

The anxious partner wants closeness and worries, quietly or not, that the other person does not love them back. When the worry gets loud, they call, they text again, they try to provoke some kind of response. The book names this "protest behavior." It is not meant as an attack, since the goal is simply to bring the other person back.

The avoidant partner reads the same texts as pressure. When a relationship gets heavy, they pull back. They say they need space, or they start noticing the partner's small flaws and enlarging them. The book calls these "deactivating strategies."

##Each one's move sets off the other's, and each move looks like proof to the person on the receiving end.##

The loop runs in a fixed order. First the anxious partner senses something is off and reaches out. The avoidant partner feels crowded and withdraws. The withdrawal reads as abandonment, so the reaching gets louder, and the avoidant partner backs off further. Each person experiences their own move as self-defense and the other's as an attack, so each looks for the culprit across the table.

Why do these two pick each other? Levine and Heller suggest that near a distant partner, an anxious person's attachment system stays switched on. That constant alertness can feel like a strong pull, even like chemistry. What gets mistaken for excitement is sometimes the alarm itself.

The authors do not end on blame. Nobody in the pattern is the villain; two systems are setting each other off. Once you can see the loop, you have choices the loop did not offer before, such as waiting an hour before the next message, or telling a partner when you will be back instead of just asking for space. Researchers also tend to treat attachment as a spectrum rather than three fixed boxes, so the pattern describes a tendency, not a destiny.

[[lessons]]
$$**Change your own half of a loop.** When two people's habits set each other off, hunting for the guilty one keeps the loop spinning. Say a coworker sends reminder after reminder, and another answers a little later every time. Each blames the other, yet every reminder feeds the delay and every delay feeds the next reminder. The only half you control is your own, and changing it even slightly gives the other person room to answer differently, which is often enough to change how the whole exchange feels.$$

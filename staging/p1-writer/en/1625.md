A parent calls with a grievance, and the grown child answers before deciding what they want from the call. Lindsay Gibson traces that reflex back to a household where someone else's needs came first.

[[story]]
Gibson says in *Adult Children of Emotionally Immature Parents* that some children learn to put a parent's needs ahead of their own. They deny what they need and take on a role that is not really theirs. For these adult children the habit makes the loneliness worse.

She gives some of the background. Many emotionally immature parents grew up with a caretaker who limited their own emotional expression, and so they could not give their children room to show the full range of feelings. A child in that house has nowhere to put most of what they feel.

Gibson separates four parent types: emotional, driven, passive and rejecting. The driven one is compulsively busy and tries to perfect everyone, the child included. The passive one may be around and agreeable but avoids anything upsetting. The rejecting one withdraws from the child through detachment or anger. What they share, in her account, is that none of them puts the child's needs first.

On the surface everything seems normal. Shelter, food and clothing are in place. Nobody asks what the child is going without, and the child usually cannot say.

Gibson names three core problems: deep loneliness, emotionally disconnected relationships and low self-worth.

The cost of the role is loneliness. The more a person acts to fit another's needs and silences their own, the more their authentic self ends up where nobody can see it. One of the parent types in the book, the emotional one, swings between overinvolvement and abandonment, which makes the child's world unpredictable.

For healing, Gibson's first step is to reconnect with your authentic self and your true feelings. After that she suggests aiming to relate to the parent instead of being embedded in the relationship, and entering each interaction with a clear goal.

[[lessons]]
$$**Notice whose need you serve first.** When the other person's need always comes first, your own wish gets harder to find over time. A sister who watches her mother's mood at every family dinner and swallows her own opinion may drive home tired and alone without knowing why. Writing down the sentence you swallowed gives you something to decide on at the next dinner: say it or leave it. Over time you can see the gap between the role you play and what you actually want, and that gap shows where the loneliness piles up.$$

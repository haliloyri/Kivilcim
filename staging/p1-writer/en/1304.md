Close to three hundred specialists, around twenty-seven thousand predictions about politics and economics, each one looking about five years ahead. A psychologist collected them and waited to see what happened. The experts with the biggest names did not come out on top.

[[story]]
In *The Black Swan*, Nassim Nicholas Taleb sets up Tetlock's study with a simpler test. People are asked a question, such as the population of Rajasthan, and told to give a range they are 98 percent sure contains the answer. They can make the range as wide as they like. The test measures how much they trust their own knowledge, so only about 2 out of 100 answers should fall outside.

The miss rate came out close to 45 percent. The first group tested were Harvard Business School students. Taleb's comment is a question: are we twenty-two times too comfortable with what we know? It seems so, he answers.

Tetlock asked political and economic experts, about a quarter of them economists, to judge how likely various events were over roughly the next five years. Their error rates turned out to be many times what they had estimated themselves. A PhD gave no advantage over an undergraduate degree. Well-published professors did no better than journalists.

##The only regularity Tetlock found ran against reputation: experts with big names were worse predictors than those with none.##

What interested Tetlock most was why the experts never noticed how poorly they were doing. So he recorded how they explained their misses afterwards, the stories that protected their self-esteem. Some said they had been playing a different game. Some said the event was an outlier nobody could have seen. Others insisted they had been almost right.

Taleb then looks at economics. The papers he could find offered no convincing evidence that economists can forecast, and at best their predictions were slightly better than random. In the forecasting competitions run by Spyros Makridakis, he and Michele Hibon concluded that statistically sophisticated methods do not necessarily beat simpler ones.

Taleb's advice is short: invest in preparedness, not in prediction.

An editorial note is fair here. Tetlock's later research found a small group of forecasters who reliably beat chance on short-range questions. Forecasting is not hopeless, but long horizons and famous names deserve extra caution.

[[lessons]]
$$**Plan for being wrong instead of polishing a single forecast.** A plan that leaves wide room around the most likely outcome can survive a surprise that would sink a plan built on one number. A family preparing to move can set aside enough money for a few extra months of rent rather than trusting the agent's guess about when the old flat will sell, and then a slow sale is an inconvenience rather than a crisis. The habit lets you ask, however confidently a forecast is delivered, what you will do if it turns out badly wrong.$$

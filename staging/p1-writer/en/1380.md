[[open]]
If a rival at work told you that you are hopeless and always will be, you would start arguing before they finished the sentence. When the same sentence arrives in your own voice, it usually goes unchallenged. Martin Seligman built a five-letter method around that gap.

[[story]]
The first three letters come from the psychologist Albert Ellis. A is the adversity, the thing that happened. B is the belief you form about it. C is the consequence, what you feel and do next. In *Learned Optimism* (1990), Seligman takes this sequence and adds two letters of his own.

The weight sits on the middle letter. Most of the time people notice the event and their reaction and nothing in between. Seligman's point is that C does not come straight from A. It comes from B, the explanation you have just given yourself.

So the method starts with a pen. For a few days, the reader writes down ordinary bad moments in three columns: what happened, what I thought, what I felt and did. As the B column fills up, the sentence standing in front of each feeling starts to show. The ones Seligman most wants caught are the pessimistic explanations the book has already described: it's permanent, it ruins everything, it's all my fault.

Then comes D, disputation. ##Seligman suggests answering a pessimistic thought as if it had been said by someone whose aim is to make you miserable, a rival for your job, say.## Arguing with a rival comes easily. You look for counter-evidence without being asked.

He gives four ways in. Evidence: what actually supports the belief? Alternatives: what else could have caused this? Implications: even if the belief were true, is it really a catastrophe? Usefulness: does dwelling on it right now help? The strongest dispute, he says, is showing that the belief does not fit the facts.

Some moments are wrong for a debate. In the middle of a meeting or behind the wheel, Seligman's advice is to steer your attention elsewhere on purpose, or to set a specific later time to think the matter through.

The fifth letter is E, energization: the change in feeling and in the urge to act that follows a dispute that worked. That is why the record has a final column. It catches what the argument bought you, so you can see the method working on your own days rather than taking it on trust.

[[lessons]]
$$**Answer your harshest thought the way you would answer an unfair accuser.** Someone whose friend hasn't replied to a message for a day may decide "she's gone off me" and spend the evening on that sentence. If anyone else said it, they would push back at once: where's the evidence, could her phone be dead, could she just be busy? Putting those same questions to your own belief shows you that the feeling came from the sentence about the event, not from the event itself. It also shows you that the sentence is the one part of the episode you can rewrite.$$

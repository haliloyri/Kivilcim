[[open]]
Anxiety, loneliness, boredom, anger: on the Intuitive Eating site the four stand side by side, followed by a short sentence about what food can do for them. The sentence is blunter than most diet advice, and it carries no scolding.

[[story]]
Evelyn Tribole and Elyse Resch give one of the ten principles of *Intuitive Eating* (1995) to feelings. Its title asks you to cope with your emotions with kindness. The authors begin from an observation about dieting itself: restriction can trigger loss of control.

The principle then lists what to look for. If you feel anxiety, loneliness, boredom or anger, the authors say, deal with it by other means, because food only offers temporary relief. The site puts the point in one line: "Food won't fix any of these feelings."

Notice the word in the title. It says kindness, not discipline. The target is how you treat yourself while the feeling is there, and the principle sits next to another one about body respect, which tells you to accept your genetic body size and shape, as you could not change your shoe size. Harsh criticism of your body, the authors add, makes it harder to reject the diet mentality.

Kindness shows up elsewhere in the authors' list too. The first principle tells you to aim your anger at diet culture's false promises, not at yourself. Reviews have linked intuitive eating to self-esteem and self-compassion, which matches that tone, though a link in observational data does not show that the approach produced it.

An extension-service explainer on intuitive eating, which is not the book itself, gives a practical version. When the urge to snack arrives under stress, pause with curiosity and perhaps write a few lines instead. If food has become someone's only coping tool, it suggests building a broader emotional toolbox.

The research is thin and mostly observational. Reviews suggest the approach may reduce eating-disorder symptoms, but results vary, and people whose doctors prescribe a specific diet may not be able to follow it. Anyone struggling with an eating disorder should work with a clinician, not with a principle list.

[[lessons]]
$$**Name the feeling before you decide about the food.** Someone who opens the cupboard after a tense phone call can say "I'm wound up, and I'm not hungry" before reaching in. The name does not forbid the snack, but it shows what the snack is being asked to do. You can then choose to eat anyway, or to take a short walk first, and either choice is made with your eyes open instead of on autopilot. Over a few days you may see which feeling shows up most often, which is worth knowing whatever you decide about the snack.$$

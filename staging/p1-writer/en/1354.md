His prized possession was a shiny red Ferrari parked in the very center of his driveway. He also owned a mansion, a private jet and a summer home on a tropical island. At fifty-three, Julian Mantle looked as if he were in his late seventies, and he could not sleep more than a couple of hours at a stretch.

[[story]]
Julian is the central figure of Robin S. Sharma's *The Monk Who Sold His Ferrari* (1997), a fable told by John, a younger lawyer who had known him for seventeen years. Julian was one of the country's most distinguished trial lawyers, as famous for his three-thousand-dollar Italian suits as for his string of courtroom victories.

He worked eighteen-hour days. When he did sleep, he woke within a couple of hours, guilty that he was not working on a file. His marriage had failed and he no longer spoke to his father. The income ran to seven figures, and none of it showed in his face.

The end came on a Monday morning in courtroom number seven, while he was arguing the Air Atlantic case. ##It was the same courtroom where he and John had won what the firm called the Mother of All Murder Trials.## In front of a packed room he collapsed with a massive heart attack, and the bailiff knelt to start CPR.

His doctor gave him an ultimatum: give up the Law or give up his life. Julian left the firm. He sold the mansion, the plane and the island, and finally he sold the Ferrari. Then he went to India.

There a temple caretaker told him of the Great Sages of Sivana, said to live somewhere high in the Himalayas. Sivana meant "oasis of enlightenment," and nobody knew where to find them. Julian set off without a guide, climbed alone and eventually reached them. Within three weeks among the sages he began noticing beauty in ordinary things, a starry night, a spider's web after rain.

About three years after he left, John was packing up at the end of a long day when his assistant announced a visitor who would not go away. The man who walked in looked to be in his mid-thirties, tall and lean, with a lineless face. He asked whether this was how John treated all his visitors, even the one who had taught him everything he knew about winning in a courtroom. John knew the voice at once.

The sickly cough was gone, and so was the grey, exhausted look of the man who had collapsed in courtroom seven. The rest of the book is the night Julian spends telling John what he learned on the mountain.

[[lessons]]
$$**Read your early warning signs before an event makes the decision for you.** The body and the people around us usually signal trouble long before a crisis, and noticing those signals early leaves the choice in your hands. A cook whose back has ached for weeks but who keeps saying he will see a doctor "when things calm down" may one morning be unable to straighten up at the counter, and then the decision is no longer his. Writing the signs down and acting on one of them, however small, lets you change direction while you still have room to choose how.$$

Give a group of business students eleven days to analyze a case, and for the first stretch many of them will cling to an approach that is not working. Then, somewhere around day six, something shifts. Nobody announces the deadline is close; the calendar does it for them.

[[story]]
The study is Connie Gersick's, and Daniel H. Pink uses it in *When: The Scientific Secrets of Perfect Timing* to explain what midpoints do to us. The student groups she followed spent their early days on a weak first approach. They stayed with it. Around the halfway mark, day six of eleven, they hit a moment of concern about time, dropped that approach and adopted a new strategy.

Pink finds the same pattern on a basketball court. Jonah Berger and Devin Pope analyzed more than 18,000 NBA games. Teams that went into halftime trailing by a single point won more often than you would expect. The effect was roughly half the size of home-court advantage, and lab experiments pointed to motivation as the reason.

Midpoints, Pink writes, can pull us down into a slump or fire us up like a spark. After the midpoint, teams become less open to brand-new ideas but more open to coaching than at any other point. His advice is to make midpoints visible. When you reach one, say "uh-oh" and get to work, rather than "oh no" and give up.

##Life has its own midpoints, and the end of a decade is one of them.## Social psychologists Adam Alter and Hal Hershfield call people in the last year of a decade "9-enders": 29, 39, 49. About half the people in a typical marathon are running one for the first time, and among those first-timers, 9-enders are overrepresented by 48 percent.

Alter and Hershfield suggest that approaching a new decade prompts a kind of meaning audit. People look back over their lives, notice a gap, and sometimes answer it with something as demanding as twenty-six miles. Nothing in their legs changed between 28 and 29. What changed was the number. The researchers also reported that 9-enders ran their marathons faster than people two years younger or older, which they read as harder training. A caveat belongs here, though. Later work questioned these findings: a 2015 commentary found no evidence that the search for meaning intensifies near a new decade, and a reanalysis argued the marathon effect fades when whole decades are compared.

[[lessons]]
$$**Mark the middle of a long project so it works as a wake-up call instead of a slump.** The halfway point of a project passes on its own whether you notice it or not, and when nobody marks it, it tends to turn into a quiet loss of energy. A student writing a thesis can put a dated "halfway review" in her calendar and rewrite her plan on that day. Fixing the middle in advance means that discovering you are slightly behind can push you forward instead of draining you, much like a team down by one at the break.$$

Some patients arrive at a psychiatrist's office with a great deal to say about their pain and very little to say about one particular fact. The psychiatrist's job, as he saw it, was to find that fact and help them look at it.

[[story]]
M. Scott Peck, a psychiatrist, builds *The Road Less Traveled* on a blunt claim about the people he treated. He saw patients, so the claim comes from the consulting room. The tendency to avoid problems, and the emotional pain that comes with them, lies at the root of mental illness. That was his central diagnosis, and he returns to it throughout the book.

He quotes Jung to sharpen it: neurosis is always a substitute for legitimate suffering. A patient who steps around a hard fact does not escape the pain, because it comes back later in a different and longer-lasting form, and in Peck's reading the later pain is worse than the one the patient sidestepped.

The remedy Peck sets against this is dedication to truth. He pictures each person as carrying a map of reality, and a map is useful only while it matches the ground. Dedication to truth means seeing reality as it is and redrawing your map whenever the old one no longer fits, even if the redrawing is uncomfortable. Many people would rather keep the old map than do the work of redrawing it.

##Facing the truth is painful because it usually demands change.## A person who sees that the old way cannot continue has to give up some comfort, and that is exactly the price many people avoid paying. Redrawing the map costs something: old plans, old habits, sometimes a comfortable belief.

Peck extends the point beyond individuals. Groups and institutions, he says, suffer in the same way when they refuse to look at what is in front of them. A team can look away from a late project just as a patient looks away from a fact.

[[lessons]]
$$**Look at the fact early, while it is still small.** A woman who has quietly lost interest in her job keeps telling herself she is just tired. Each month of looking away adds to a dull unease, and the unease turns up in her sleep and her temper. Once she names the fact in one plain sentence, she can decide what to do about it. Naming it does not force her to act that day.$$

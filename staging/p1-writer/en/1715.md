[[story]]
Nir Eyal had written about how technology pulls people in. One summer afternoon he was playing with his young daughter, working through a book of activities page by page. One of the activities asked what superpower each of them would like to have.

While she thought about her answer, Eyal looked at his phone. When he raised his head, she was no longer in the room. For someone who studies how technology captures attention, losing one of his most valuable moments with his daughter to a screen was hard to swallow.

Eyal later read it this way: by looking at the phone, he had told her the phone mattered more than she did. Asked today which superpower he would choose, he says his answer would be being indistractable.

*Indistractable* begins with this memory and moves the question away from the phone and away from willpower. In Eyal's account, the roots of distraction are internal triggers, the wish to escape uncomfortable feelings such as boredom, loneliness or anxiety. The phone is only the nearest way out.

The book's two key words come from here. Eyal calls the opposite of distraction "traction," the actions that pull us toward what we actually want. Both words share the same Latin root, a word meaning "to pull." When we do not choose the direction, something else pulls us in its own.

Eyal's framework has four parts: master internal triggers, make time for traction, hack back external triggers, and prevent distraction with pacts. It is a system built after that afternoon with his daughter.

##What costs us our attention is often the urge to escape discomfort, well before the device.## So the first step is not to blame the phone but to understand what we are feeling at that moment. Eyal compresses this into the idea that time management is really pain management.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you reach for the phone, ask why you are reaching.** A student who glances at a phone halfway through a textbook is often not escaping the notification but the unease of a hard paragraph. Naming that feeling for a few seconds can make putting the phone down easier than fighting it with willpower. Once you spot the discomfort, you can choose the same action a little more deliberately next time.$$

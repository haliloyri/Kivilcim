Coffee in a thermos, a packed lunch, a book, a stack of Spanish-language tapes. One commuter had all of it on the seat beside him, two days after an earthquake had wrecked his freeway. The driver one car ahead had none of it, and he was shouting at a TV reporter about California.

[[story]]
Jack Canfield lived through the 1994 Northridge earthquake in Los Angeles. In *The Success Principles* he recalls watching CNN two days later, as a reporter walked between cars stuck on a damaged freeway. A drive that normally took an hour was taking two or three.

The reporter knocked on the first window. The driver was angry and said he hated California: first there were fires, then floods, and now an earthquake. No matter what time he left in the morning, he said, he was going to be late for work.

The reporter knocked on the window behind him. This driver was all smiles. He had left home at five in the morning and figured his boss couldn't ask more than that under the circumstances. He had music cassettes, his Spanish tapes, a cell phone, coffee, his lunch, even a book. "It's no problem," he said.

##If the earthquake had decided how people felt, everyone on that freeway would have been angry.## Canfield's point is that they weren't. Leaving prepared or leaving unprepared made the difference between the two cars.

He frames it with a formula he learned from Dr. Robert Resnick, a psychotherapist in Los Angeles: E + R = O, Event plus Response equals Outcome. When you don't like an outcome, you have two options. You can blame the event, or you can change your response until the outcome changes. Canfield argues that the only things you really control are the thoughts you think, the images you hold in your head and the actions you take.

The same chapter follows a friend's Lexus dealership in Southern California. When the Gulf War broke out, people stopped walking into the showroom. The usual response was to keep running newspaper and radio ads and wait, and sales kept sliding.

So the dealership tried new things. The one that worked was loading up a fleet of new cars and driving them out to country clubs, marinas, polo grounds and parties in Beverly Hills and Westlake Village, then inviting people to take one for a spin. A high percentage of those drivers went on to buy or lease. By the end, the dealership was selling more cars per week than before the war began.

The war hadn't changed. Neither had the freeway, for the man with the thermos.

[[lessons]]
$$**Look for your share of the outcome before blaming the event.** When something outside your control keeps producing the same bad result, there is often a piece of preparation or behaviour that is still yours to change. A nurse whose handovers always run late can't shorten the shift before hers, but she can write her notes during the last hour instead of at the end. Asking what your next response could be turns a complaint into something you can test, and it shows you quickly whether the event or your habit was doing most of the damage.$$

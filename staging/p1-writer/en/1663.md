"I hope I don't end up like her." A twenty-five-year-old student thought this about a stranger on the tube. A moment later, the man next to him in the washroom looked over. He had not only thought the words, he had said them out loud.

[[story]]
Eckhart Tolle tells the story in *A New Earth*, in the chapter on the voice in the head. He was a first-year student at the University of London, a mature one at twenty-five, and twice a week he took the tube to the university library. One morning a woman in her early thirties sat down opposite him.

The carriage was full, yet the seats on either side of her stayed empty. In a loud, angry voice she argued without pause, as if someone sat in the empty seat beside her: she had called that person a liar, how dare they accuse her.

She got off at Tottenham Court Road and walked toward Bedford Square, still arguing. Curious, he followed about twenty steps behind. She turned in at Senate House, the university's central building and library, which was exactly where he was going, and an elevator swallowed her up.

In the men's room, washing his hands, he thought: I hope I don't end up like her. The man beside him glanced over, and Tolle realised he had mumbled it aloud. *Oh my God, I'm already like her.*

##His own mind ran as incessantly as hers.## Her underlying emotion seemed to be anger, his was anxiety. She thought out loud, he thought mostly in his head.

For a moment he stood back from his mind and saw it from a deeper perspective, a brief shift from thinking to awareness. Looking at his face in the mirror, he laughed out loud: life was not as serious as his mind made it out to be. Tolle says it was only a glimpse, soon forgotten, and that he spent the next three years in anxiety and depression, completely identified with his mind.

Elsewhere in the book he offers a way back to that moment. Notice the voice in your head, perhaps while it is complaining about something. Whenever you notice it, you realise that you are not the voice but the one who is aware of it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Catch the voice and you are the one listening.** A tenant rehearses a talk with the landlord in the shower, winning every point in advance, and might catch the voice halfway through a sentence, the way one catches a radio left on in another room. If she lets it finish without agreeing with it, the one who speaks and the one who listens stop being the same person. That small distance helps her walk into the real conversation with less anger, and it makes the next rehearsal easier to spot much earlier, before it has won all its points again.$$

[[open]]
Couples argue about plates left in the sink, tone of voice, who called whom. Sue Johnson reads many of these fights as one question in different words, and she pictures the person asking it as someone knocking on a brick wall.

[[story]]
Sue Johnson's *Hold Me Tight* (2008) is built as seven conversations, and the first is about recognising the "demon dialogues," patterns that trap a couple in the same loop. Behind the book sits her own therapy. In 1985 she and Les Greenberg formulated and tested what became Emotionally Focused Therapy, a method she later joined to attachment theory. The first couples manual followed in 1988, and her nine-step model runs in three stages: calming the conflict, restructuring the bond, and consolidating the new patterns.

Attachment theory sits at the centre of the model, and the therapist uses it to guide the work with emotion. Johnson treats it as the defining theory of adult love, and describes it like this: a partner is the person you turn to when you need someone to be there. The aim of the therapy is to reshape that bond into what she calls effective dependency, and at its best a secure attachment.

Attachment styles make this concrete. Anxious partners seek reassurance and may use blame, while avoidant partners tend to solve problems dispassionately, as Johnson and Sims described in 2000.

A fight reads differently through that lens. When one partner pushes, complains or keeps insisting, Johnson sees an attachment protest. The pushing partner is, in effect, quietly asking whether they matter to the other. Hence the image from the book's pages: one person knocking on a brick wall, hoping the other will open it.

The model puts the problem in the cycle between two people, not in either of them. In the first stage of therapy the cycle is tracked, and the problem is reframed as the cycle instead of a partner. So the repeating pattern becomes visible before anyone is declared the guilty party, and both partners can look at the same loop together.

Does it work? A 2019 meta-analysis by Beasley and Ager concluded that the therapy significantly improves relationship satisfaction, with gains sustained up to two years at follow-up. An earlier 1999 analysis by Johnson and colleagues, pooling four studies, had found a larger effect than other couple interventions. The psychologist James Coyne later called that earlier analysis poor quality, which is worth keeping in mind.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask what a complaint is reaching for before you answer its topic.** A flatmate keeps complaining about unwashed pots, and each time you reply with the cleaning rota. If you answer only the topic, the same argument returns. If you ask what the complaint is reaching for, such as a wish to be noticed, you may hear something different, and the pots can still be sorted out afterwards, probably with less heat. That order lets you respond to the person first and to the chore second, and it takes only one calm sentence to try at the next real disagreement.$$

Three dots and three arrows on a bar napkin. That is how much room the first picture of an airline took up, and beside it the route maps of its rivals looked like a plate of spaghetti.

[[story]]
In *The Back of the Napkin* (2008), Dan Roam credits the start of Southwest Airlines to a drawing. Rollin King, a pilot and businessman, was winding down a small regional carrier, and he talked the idea over with his lawyer, Herb Kelleher: a small airline that would link large Texas cities rather than small towns.

The legend puts the scene in a San Antonio restaurant with a bar, in 1966. A napkin and a pen did the rest. The drawing was a triangle with Dallas at the top, San Antonio at the bottom left and Houston as the third corner: three dots for the cities, three arrows for the direct flights between them. Accounts differ on who held the pen.

Set beside the route maps of traditional hub-and-spoke airlines, the sketch looked neat and simple. Those maps were a spaghetti of lines running through a few big centers. The triangle cut the whole plan down to three corners and three sides.

Roam's reading is about selling. The picture made the airline easy to sell, to investors and to customers alike.

The story comes with a caveat. Kelleher and King both said later that there was no literal napkin, and Wikipedia calls it a founding myth. Texas Co-op Power reports that not long before King died in 2014 he said the story was not entirely true, calling it a hell of a good story. Southwest still keeps a bronze replica of the napkin at its headquarters at Dallas Love Field.

What is left is the shape of the plan itself, three Texas cities each linked to the others by direct flights. The company was co-founded in 1967 and first flew in June 1971.

[[lessons]]
$$**You should be able to draw your idea in a few marks.** Someone who wants to open a small repair shop can put the shop down as one dot, the three nearest streets as three more dots, and the customers' routes as arrows. What will not fit on that page is often not yet thought through. Drawing before you explain keeps the plan simple enough for other people to repeat, and that is the version they will end up backing.$$

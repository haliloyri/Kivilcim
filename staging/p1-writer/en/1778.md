The forwarded email carried a customer's complaint and exactly one line from the boss on top of it: a question mark. Whoever received one at Amazon knew exactly what it meant, and that every other task on the desk would have to wait.

[[story]]
Brad Stone writes in *The Everything Store* that Amazon employees who got a question mark from Jeff Bezos reacted as if they had found a ticking bomb. They had a few hours. In that time they had to fix whatever the customer had flagged and write a thorough account of how it happened, and a succession of managers reviewed the answer before it reached Bezos himself.

Asked why whole teams drop everything for a single character, Jeff Wilke, one of his senior executives, answered that every customer anecdote matters. Amazon researched each one, he said, because each said something about the company's own processes. In his words, they were precious sources of information. Stone describes these escalations as Bezos's way of making sure the customer's voice was heard inside the company all the time.

Another scene in the book made the same point with a telephone, and this one played out in front of an audience. In a meeting Bezos asked Bill Price, Amazon's vice president of customer service, how long customers waited on hold. Price said well under a minute, with little proof to offer. Bezos put the speakerphone in the middle of the conference table and dialed Amazon's 800 number himself.

About thirty executives sat and listened to hold music. Four and a half minutes went by before an agent answered, and Bezos said he was only calling to check, then hung up. Years later, on a podcast, he remembered the wait as longer than ten minutes. The book gives four and a half.

On that same podcast Bezos said a customer-service metric in a weekly business review deck had claimed waits under sixty seconds while complaints said otherwise. He concluded they had not been measuring the right thing. After that, the company set about measuring it properly.

Price has also recalled being in the very first meeting where Bezos left a chair empty at the table. It was for the customer, who can never come to the company's meetings. Bezos had hired Price after asking him to define customer service, and Price had answered that the best service is no service.

[[lessons]]
$$**Give the person a decision lands on a seat in the room, even if it stays empty.** A small bakery owner about to change opening hours can leave one chair free at the staff meeting and ask what the regular who comes at seven would say. That question tends to surface a cost nobody at the table would pay, because it falls on the absent person alone. You then decide knowing who will feel the change, and what they might say, instead of learning it later from their complaints or from the customers who quietly stop coming.$$

[[reflect]]
&&In your next decision, who will live with the result without being in the room, and how will you find out what they would say?&&

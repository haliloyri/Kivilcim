A volunteer sits at a dirty desk with a short story about someone's questionable act and is asked to rate how wrong it was. A second volunteer, in a clean room, reads the very same story. The two ratings do not come out alike.

[[story]]
Jonathan Haidt argues in *The Righteous Mind* that moral judgments usually arrive first as quick intuitions, and that reasoning mostly follows to defend them. He pictures a rider on an elephant: the rider is reason, the elephant is intuition, and the elephant has already picked the direction.

Haidt and Thalia Wheatley had earlier used hypnosis to make people feel a flash of disgust at an arbitrary word, and stories containing that word were judged more harshly. In experiments by Haidt and his colleagues, people read short moral stories and rated how wrong an action was. Some did this in a room with a foul smell or at a dirty desk, while others sat in a clean setting. Those exposed to disgust tended to rate the same wrongs more harshly.

The odd part was what they said about it. Nobody pointed to the smell or the desk, and the stories themselves had not become any worse. They explained their verdicts with reasons about the act itself, about who did what and why.

##The reasons came afterward, and the push came from the body.##

For Haidt this shows reason acting less like a judge and more like a spokesperson. A spokesperson does not make the decision, but announces it and defends it. A person who condemns an act sincerely believes the verdict rests on principle, while the room, the lingering disgust and the mood may already have voted.

Haidt reads all this as the elephant leaning before the rider speaks. One caution belongs here. Later attempts to repeat these experiments gave mixed results, and a 2015 meta-analysis found that incidental disgust has, at best, a small effect on moral judgment. Haidt's wider claim, that intuitions come first, does not rest on these studies alone, but the strength of this particular effect is disputed.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you pass a harsh verdict, check what your body and your surroundings are doing to you.** Someone who flares up at a colleague's mistake and writes a long email may be hungry and sitting in a stuffy office, and still find a principled reason for the anger. The dirty desk in Haidt's studies shows how a reason can show up after the feeling that drove it, and how well hidden that feeling can be. If you let a verdict wait a little, you give yourself a chance to separate the principle from the mood.$$

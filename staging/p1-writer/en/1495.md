Most organizations are built on two assumptions about people. When Gallup interviewed the world's best managers, they were working from the opposite pair. Which pair a manager starts from shapes where the training hours go, and it shows in how an organization hires, trains and promotes.

[[story]]
Over thirty years, Gallup interviewed 80,000 managers in hundreds of organizations around the world, looking for what the best ones had in common. The findings went into an earlier book, *First, Break All the Rules*. Marcus Buckingham and Donald Clifton took the question wider in *Now, Discover Your Strengths*.

Their study of excellence ran to a little over two million interviews. It covered the best teachers, doctors, salespeople, lawyers, professional basketball players, stockbrokers, accountants, hotel housekeepers, leaders, soldiers, nurses, pastors, systems engineers and chief executives.

The findings split into two pairs of assumptions. Most organizations are built on these: each person can learn to be competent in almost anything, and each person's greatest room for growth is in the areas of greatest weakness. ##The best managers assume the opposite: each person's talents are lasting and unique, and each person's greatest room for growth is in the areas of greatest strength.##

Gallup lists four signs that an organization is running on the wrong pair: spending more on training after hiring than on selection, managing performance through work rules, policies and procedures, building development plans around areas of opportunity, and promoting on accumulated skills and experience so that the most well-rounded people get the most prestige and pay. Remedial fixes such as a communication class count as damage control rather than development, and Gallup accepts them only occasionally.

The authors argue that parents, teachers, managers and psychology's focus on pathology push us to become experts in our weaknesses, while our strengths stay dormant and neglected. They define a weakness as anything that gets in the way of excellent performance. The goal they name is consistent, near-perfect performance. The best managers look for talent in every role, focus on outcomes, treat employees differently, and spend the most time with their best people.

Gallup turned this into a measurement, and the book now treats it as the starting point. The StrengthsFinder profile came out of about twenty-five years of work, sorts talent into 34 themes, and reports a person's five most powerful signature themes.

[[lessons]]
$$**Look for growth where a person is strongest.** The assumption the book recommends puts a person's greatest room for growth in the area of greatest strength. A shop manager with an employee who struggles with record-keeping but outshines everyone at talking to customers can give that employee more of the customer work instead of more record-keeping training. The records get paired with a colleague or tied to a simple template. The weak spot is managed, and the thing the employee does best has room to grow, which is where the training hours now go.$$

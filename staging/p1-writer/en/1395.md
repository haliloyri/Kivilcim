The telephone is off, one task sits on the desk, and the clock reads ten. A salesperson in a time-management book keeps that arrangement for exactly one hour and does nothing but phone prospects. The author builds his final rule around hours like this one.

[[story]]
Brian Tracy ends *Eat That Frog!* with a rule he calls single handling. Once you begin a task, you keep working without diversion or distraction until the job is 100 percent complete. When you are tempted to stop or turn to something else, he suggests telling yourself, over and over, "Back to work!"

The numbers in that chapter are his own estimates, and he gives no source for them. He says that concentrating single-mindedly on your most important task can cut the time it needs by 50 percent or more. Starting and stopping, picking a task up and putting it down, has been estimated, in his words, to stretch the time by as much as 500 percent. Treat both as a direction rather than as measurements.

The cost, he explains, comes from the restart. Each time you come back, you have to work out where you were and what is still left to do. You have to shake off inertia and build up momentum and rhythm again. ##Every break moves you back up what he calls the efficiency curve, where each part of the job is harder and slower.## The reverse also holds: once you have prepared thoroughly, begun, and refused to stop, he says, you gather energy, enthusiasm and motivation and work faster.

Chapter 19 turns this into a calendar. You set aside thirty, sixty or ninety minute segments for important work, switch off the telephone, remove every distraction and work non-stop. His example is the salesperson who resolves to phone prospects for one solid hour between 10 and 11 AM, and does it. The hour is fixed, so there is nothing to put off.

Tracy adds that working at home in the morning without interruption can get you three times as much done as a busy office would, which is again his own claim. He even turns a business flight into an office. Plan the work thoroughly before departure, he says, and once the plane takes off you can work non-stop for the entire flight.

All the planning, prioritising and organising in the book comes down to one simple move: pick the most important task, begin it, and stay with it until it is finished.

[[lessons]]
$$**Interruptions cost you the restart.** A bookkeeper who answers a message halfway through reconciling a set of accounts often has to recount the column she had already added up, so a short break leaves her with a long restart. If you give one task a single block and leave the phone face down until the block is over, that bill is never issued and the rhythm of the work stays intact. The messages can wait for the end of the block, and you answer them with a finished job behind you and a clearer head.$$

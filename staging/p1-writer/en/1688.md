[[open]]
A thought can be moved from the category of fact to the category of broadcast by five words placed in front of it. The book offers them as a phrase to try, not a ritual.

[[story]]
Russ Harris says in *The Happiness Trap* (2007) that the thinking self is like a radio playing constantly in the background. Most of the time it is Radio Doom and Gloom, broadcasting negative stories 24 hours a day.

Defusion is his answer to that broadcast. It means seeing thoughts, images and memories as just words and pictures, without fighting them, running from them or staying too focused on them.

One technique is to add the words "I'm having the thought that" to the front of the thought. Instead of "I'm a failure," you say, "I'm having the thought that I'm a failure." That gives the thought some psychological distance, and the notes say it brings relief from rumination.

Another technique is to imagine a cartoon character speaking your thought aloud. A third is to answer with "Thanks Mind, but I'm not playing today." All three do the same job: they separate the voice from the person who hears it.

Harris calls getting caught up in a thought being hooked. When it happens, you unhook yourself and return to your breathing. The Guardian's Oliver Burkeman picked out the unhooking idea as a highlight of the book.

A chapter excerpt contains a warning. Many readers, meeting defusion for the first time, try to use it as a control strategy, as a client called John does, and it is not meant for getting rid of thoughts. Behind all of this stands the observing self, which watches the thinking self's judgmental commentary neutrally. Notes compare emotions to weather and the observing self to the sky, and one practice for reaching it is ten deep breaths. Defusion is the first of six core principles in the book, alongside expansion, connection, the observing self, values and committed action. The last of them asks for a simple sequence: accept your situation, pick an action that fits your values, and take it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Name the thought before you decide what to do about it.** Someone about to give a speech at a dinner hears "everyone will be bored" and could argue with it all evening. If they instead say to themselves, "I'm having the thought that I'll be boring," the thought is still there but no longer sounds like a ruling. They can start talking and put their attention on the first sentence.$$

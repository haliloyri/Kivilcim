[[open]]
Lindsay Gibson sorts emotionally immature parents into four types, and none of them puts the child's needs first. A child raised by any of them can grow up with a roof, meals and clean clothes, and still be lonely in a way that is hard to explain.

[[story]]
In *Adult Children of Emotionally Immature Parents*, Gibson describes the four types. An emotional parent swings between overinvolvement and abandonment, which makes a child's world unpredictable. Driven parents are compulsively busy and cannot stop trying to perfect everything, other people included. Passive ones may be around and agreeable but avoid anything upsetting, while rejecting ones withdraw from the child through detachment or anger.

Gibson's view is that many of these parents grew up with a caretaker who limited their own emotional expression, and that their capacity for empathy stayed limited too. A child in that house has few chances to show the full range of feelings to an adult who will take them in.

What the children of these parents often share is a deep emotional loneliness, and many of them do not know why they feel it. It is easy to wave away, because life looked normal. The parents may have provided shelter, food and clothing, and with those in place it seems there is nothing to complain about.

Gibson names three core problems that follow: deep loneliness, emotionally disconnected relationships and low self-worth. Each makes the others worse. A child learns to hide feelings, and as an adult pays for it in close relationships, because nobody can respond to a feeling that stays out of sight. Gibson also notes that the loneliness often goes unexplained, which is part of why people carry it for so long. A person who values themselves little has a hard time getting close to anyone, and the distance feeds the loneliness again.

Some children cope by building a dream world. They picture a better future, and the picture helps them get through the present. Carried into adulthood, the same dream can set up unrealistic expectations, and Gibson gives the example of a marriage imagined as effortless.

[[lessons]]
$$**Name what was missing without denying the care you got.** Having basic needs met and having your feelings met are two separate things, and a person can ask about each one on its own. Someone who describes their childhood as fine but feels alone even with close friends can say, instead of "nobody was unkind to me," that there was nobody to tell their troubles to. Putting that missing piece into words gives you a place to start when you look for the reason behind the loneliness.$$

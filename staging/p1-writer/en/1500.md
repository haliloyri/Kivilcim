In one research team's picture of workplace culture, a person at stage three keeps the people around them apart so that they never compare notes. Everything flows through one pair at a time, and the one in the middle stays important. Tribal leadership starts by undoing that habit.

[[story]]
Dave Logan, John King and Halee Fischer-Wright argue in the 2008 book that leading a tribe comes down to two things: the words people use and the kinds of relationships they form. The org chart does not appear on that list, because a tribe, roughly 20 to 150 people, forms whatever the chart says. Tribes decide whether a new leader succeeds and how much work gets done, and they can make a company's culture initiative go nowhere. Most people never notice any of it.

At stage three, relationships run in pairs. The person who is great, and says so, sits in the middle of several of these pairs and keeps the other people apart so they cannot compare notes. Every message passes through that person. The others learn what the rest know only as far as that person chooses to tell them. The same person tends to hoard what they know, and when anyone asks for help the answer is that there is too little time and too little support.

The authors' first move is a question. Ask open-ended questions until you learn what the people in the room value. Their second move is a job. Build a project too big for anyone to finish alone, so that people have to partner. Pairs start to turn into triads, three-person relationships anchored on the same shared value, and the person in the middle no longer has to carry every message.

##The leader speaks at most one stage above where the group is.## For a stage-two group, that means finding the few people who want change and talking to each of them one-on-one in stage-three language. A rousing stage-four speech to a room that says "my life sucks" would land nowhere.

One summary lists the signs that it is working. People say "we" instead of "I". Their networks grow from a few dozen contacts to several hundred. They work less and get more done. At stage four, the authors suggest regular "oil changes", team conversations about what is working, what is not and how to fix it. Leaders who do this well earn a loyalty that the summary calls cult-like, and followers often move with them when they are promoted.

[[lessons]]
$$**Introduce two people around something they both care about.** A shop manager hears the same repair complaint from two colleagues, each of whom talks only to her. She can keep relaying messages, or she can sit them down with the complaint log and the one thing both want, which is fewer callbacks. A conversation built around a shared concern lasts after she leaves the room, and the next time the complaint comes up, the two can bring it to each other first. You get a team that solves things without waiting for you, and you can see it start when they begin to say "we".$$

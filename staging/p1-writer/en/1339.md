[[story]]
In *The Sleep Revolution*, Arianna Huffington goes back to the morning that started it. On April 6, 2007, she was lying on the floor of her home office in a pool of blood. She had collapsed from exhaustion, and on the way down her head had hit the corner of her desk. Her cheekbone was broken.

At the time she was building The Huffington Post, and for two years she had been working regular 18-hour days. She admits she had not understood the importance of sleep until that morning.

What followed was a tour of specialists. She went from doctor to doctor, from the brain MRI to the CAT scan to the echocardiogram, trying to find out whether a hidden illness or a heart problem lay behind the fall. Each test was another appointment and another wait. ##Nothing deeper was. The tests pointed to exhaustion and lack of sleep.##

Doctors' waiting rooms, she writes, turned out to be good places to ask herself a lot of questions. She had built a company that was by any normal measure a success, and that success had left her on the floor next to her desk with a broken face. The questions she started asking in those chairs became the book.

At its center is a belief she says drives the whole sleep crisis: that overwork and burnout are simply the price you pay to succeed. In that culture, going without sleep is worn as a badge of honour, and the person who sleeps least is quietly assumed to be the one who cares most about the work.

Huffington sets out how common sleep deprivation has become, especially among professionals and college students. She urges readers to make seven to eight hours a night a priority, and she warns against leaning on sleeping pills such as Ambien to get there.

Her reason for writing it is plain. "I don't want you to make the mistakes I did," she says. She learned the lesson with a broken cheekbone; the book is an attempt to let other people learn it more cheaply.

[[lessons]]
$$**Hear bragging about little sleep as a warning sign.** Where exhaustion is admired, people often notice the cost only when something breaks, because tiredness looks like commitment from the outside. A delivery driver who adds weekend shifts to a full week may feel proud of how much he carries, right up until the morning he nods off at a red light. Counting lost sleep as a real cost, the way you would count money or time, lets you decide what is really worth it before your body, or a red light, makes the decision for you.$$

[[story]]
In *The Culture Code* (2018), Daniel Coyle describes Dave Cooper, a military team leader whose units hold an after-action review right after each mission. Enlisted men lead it. There is no rank in the room, no agenda and no minutes. The team replays every decision and keeps asking why until the truth comes out.

Cooper says the most important words a leader can say are these: ##I screwed that up.##

Coyle sees what the sentence does. When the person with the most standing admits an error first, nobody else has to guard a reputation, and the others can say what went wrong on their side too. The review becomes a place for finding mistakes, where it could have been a place for placing blame.

He names the mechanism a vulnerability loop. One person signals vulnerability. A second person notices it and signals their own. The first notices that in turn. After a few rounds a norm exists: this is a place where people say what they do not know and what they got wrong, and trust and closeness grow with it.

Coyle finds a milder version at Pixar, where teams practise what they call plussing. When someone shows an early draft of a film, colleagues do not hand over a list of notes. They add a solution to what is there. The draft is treated as something to build on rather than something to mark. Coyle lists these candour practices together with after-action reviews and the BrainTrust.

The practice is one leader's method as Coyle reports it, and the book offers it as a model rather than a measured result. It also does not require a battlefield. Any team can hold a review right after a piece of work, hand it to the people lowest in rank, and let the most senior person speak first.

The sentence at the heart of this story is short. It still takes some nerve, since it is said in front of people who work for you. What Cooper's units show is that the nerve is the thing that gives everyone else permission, and that a norm can start with a single person speaking first.

[[lessons]]
$$**Say your own mistake first.** A kitchen manager who opens the post-service review by saying that she sent a dish out too late will hear the cooks mention their own slips within minutes. If she opens by asking who was slow, the room goes quiet and the same slips stay hidden. When you go first, you set the level of honesty for the whole conversation, and the group sees problems sooner. The person in charge also finds out what happened while it is still fresh, and that is hard to get any other way.$$

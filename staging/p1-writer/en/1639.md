"What will people think?" is the question perfectionism asks all day, according to Brené Brown, and healthy striving asks a different one. The difference shows up in what the person who carries it can no longer do, and that is why the heavy one is the harder one to put down.

[[story]]
Brené Brown wrote *The Gifts of Imperfection* (2010) after studying thousands of interviews. She sorted the people in them into two groups: those who lived with a sense of worthiness and those who struggled with a feeling of inadequacy. Perfectionism is one of the defenses the book pays most attention to.

The subtitle is *Let Go of Who You Think You're Supposed to Be and Embrace Who You Are*. Each of Brown's ten guideposts pairs something to cultivate with something to let go of, and perfectionism is what she places under self-compassion as the thing to release.

Brown calls perfectionism a twenty-ton shield. People lug it around thinking it will protect them, when it is actually the thing preventing them from taking flight. The weight is felt with every step, but few people think to put it down.

Her definition of perfectionism fits in one sentence: if I look perfect, do everything perfectly and act perfectly, I can minimize or avoid the painful feelings of shame, judgment and blame. So there is no higher standard at work. There is a defensive strategy, and its enemy is shame.

Brown separates this from healthy striving. Perfectionism is other-focused and keeps asking itself the same thing: what will people think? Healthy striving is self-focused and asks another thing: how can I improve? From the outside, the two can look like the same hard work. What differs is whose eyes the work is for.

This defense also has a loop. When shame, judgment or blame come anyway, a person often puts it down to not having been perfect enough. The answer is then to try to be more perfect, and the shield gets a little heavier. For this reason Brown describes perfectionism as something that holds a person back rather than something that protects.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask whose eyes you are working for.** Someone preparing a presentation who spends the last hour nudging slide margins may be preventing criticism rather than improving the content. When she asks herself what the work still lacks to be better, her attention returns to the content, and she can drop the unnecessary polish without lowering the bar. The standard stays high but stops acting as a shield, because the gaze moves from what people will think to the work itself. At the next presentation, you can spend that last hour testing the content instead.$$

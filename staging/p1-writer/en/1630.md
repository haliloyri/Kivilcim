Every circle of friends has the person who gets the first call. They drive to the airport at five in the morning, sit in the hospital waiting room, and take the shift nobody else wants. You have probably never heard that person say no. A physician found this same portrait again and again among his chronically ill patients.

[[story]]
Gabor Maté practised medicine for years, and in *When the Body Says No* he describes a pattern he kept meeting in people with serious chronic illness. They put other people's needs ahead of their own. They held their anger in. Saying no was very hard for them, and they were usually the first to turn up for everyone else.

Taken one at a time, none of this looks unusual. You put off a need here, swallow some irritation there, agree to one more request. What caught Maté's attention was how these small acts stacked up in the same person over years.

His explanation is that when someone cannot say no, the body sometimes says it for them, through exhaustion, pain or illness. ##In his view, the body's no is the one the person could not speak.## The book takes its title from that idea.

The route he proposes runs through chronic stress. A tense week is part of every life. He means the kind that never lets up, carried for years, which he says wears down the nervous system and the immune system. Swallowed anger and needs that nobody meets are, for him, a steady source of that load.

He is careful about blame. Illness is not presented as the patient's fault, and the aim is understanding, not guilt. A person who is sick does not have to cross-examine their own character.

A caution belongs here too. How strong the link between stress and disease is, and which way it runs, is still argued over in medicine. The book is no diagnosis and no treatment plan for anyone. It is one physician's reading of what he saw across his patients, and it reads best that way.

[[lessons]]
$$**Say the small no early.** A refusal that never gets spoken has to go somewhere, and Maté's observation is that it can end up in the body. A bakery clerk who agrees to every extra Saturday shift, because asking for a weekend off feels selfish, may be worn out and resentful long before she admits it. A plain "I can't this Saturday" is a low-stakes refusal that shows her where her limit actually sits, and practising it while the stakes are small is easier than finding the limit through exhaustion.$$

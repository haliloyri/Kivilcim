Recent college graduates rated how much they cared about money, fame and image, and how much about personal growth, relationships and community. They rated again a year later. What happened to the ones who got what they had said they wanted is the story.

[[story]]
Daniel Pink's *Drive* (2009) names purpose as the third driver of motivation, after autonomy and mastery. He sets purpose maximization against profit maximization, and he puts the work of Edward Deci and Richard Ryan at the center of his argument. A 2009 study from the same Rochester group, by Christopher Niemiec, Ryan and Deci, puts that contrast to a test.

The researchers followed recent graduates. At one year and again at two years after college, the graduates rated how important extrinsic aims were to them, meaning money, fame and image, and how important intrinsic aims were, meaning personal growth, relationships and community.

Caring about either kind predicted getting it. Whoever put weight on money, fame and image tended to reach them, and whoever put weight on growth, relationships and community tended to reach those instead.

##Reaching the intrinsic aims went with better psychological health. Reaching the extrinsic aims did not.## It was linked instead to indicators of ill-being, and press coverage of the study listed shame, anger and physical signs of anxiety such as headaches.

The authors traced the difference to basic needs. The health gains followed changes in how well people's needs for autonomy, competence and relatedness were met. Their suggestion was that striving for wealth and adulation does little to satisfy those needs, at least early in a career, and that the hours it takes can crowd out more nourishing activities.

Pink's practical advice from earlier in the book points the same way: pay enough to take the issue of money off the table, so that people can turn their attention to the work itself and to what it is for. The study appeared in the Journal of Research in Personality.

The study is correlational and rests on self-reports from one post-college sample, so it shows an association and does not prove that chasing profit causes distress. Pink's use of the framing is plain enough: he wants the reader to ask what a goal is for, and not only whether it can be reached.

[[lessons]]
$$**Check whether a goal feeds your basic needs, and not only whether you reach it.** A salesman who lands the biggest commission of the quarter and still feels flat has met a goal about money, while a colleague who spent the same hours coaching new hires may go home with more energy. The first goal gets counted and applauded, and the second often feeds the sense of choice, skill and belonging. When you pick your next goal, ask which of those three it will feed, and give that goal your best hours, before the easy-to-count goals take them.$$

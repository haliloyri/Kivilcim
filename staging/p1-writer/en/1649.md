[[open]]
Two people, one thing wanted: that is envy. Three people, one relationship at risk: that is jealousy. In everyday speech the two words are used as one, and Brené Brown's definitions pull them apart by the simplest measure available, a head count.

[[story]]
In *Atlas of the Heart* (2021), Brown puts envy and jealousy in the same section, "Places We Go When We Compare," next to admiration, schadenfreude and freudenfreude. The section is one of 13 land masses in a book that maps 87 emotions and experiences. Brown still keeps the two apart.

##Envy occurs when we want something that another person has.## The things people most often mean are looks, status and wealth, the big trio. Count the people in an envy scene and you get two: you and the person who holds what you want. Nobody else needs to be involved, and nothing you have is at risk. The neighbours show how far the word "comparison" stretches: schadenfreude is pleasure at someone else's misfortune, and freudenfreude is joy at someone else's good fortune, so the same scene of another person's luck can send you in several directions.

Jealousy is a different animal. It is the fear of losing a relationship, or a valued part of one, that you already have. Now the count changes. There is you, there is the person you value, and there is a third person, the one who seems to be drawing that person away. Brown calls that person the perceived rival, because the rival may be real or may only look that way from where you are standing.

One set of notes on the book describes jealousy as a response to feelings such as anger, sadness and fear. If that is right, the word covers a small bundle of feelings, and "jealous" is the label placed on top.

The two scenes ask for different conversations. In one, the question is about a thing. In the other, it is about a person and what you stand to lose with them. Using one word for both means walking into the wrong conversation with the right amount of feeling.

[[lessons]]
$$**Count the people in the picture before you name the feeling.** A coworker who says "I'm jealous of my cousin's new flat" is describing a wish for a flat, which has two people in it. A friend who feels uneasy when her closest friend spends every Friday with a new group has three people in the picture and a fear of losing time together. Once you see which count you have, you can choose between asking how to get the thing and asking for what you need from a person. Either question is easier to put into words than a general complaint that someone else has it better, and either one can be answered.$$

[[story]]
Matthew Syed's *Bounce* (2010) argues for the primacy of practice over innate talent, and one of its pillars is a study of violin students. Practice time was the only factor directly linked to how well they did. The top performers averaged about 10,000 hours of serious practice, the least skilled about 4,000.

Syed engages the idea that became known as the 10,000-hour rule. Excellence, in his account, generally takes around ten years, or 10,000 hours, of purposeful practice, often with a coach beside you. Hours alone are not the claim.

What counts is how the hours are spent. Purposeful practice means pushing past your comfort zone. Many people stop practising that way once they reach a comfortable level, while the top performers keep going beyond their current skills. Time spent repeating what you can already do does not move the benchmark.

Take Roger Federer's famous ability to read a serve before the ball is struck. Syed attributes it to learned, practised skill, not to quicker reactions.

The psychologist Anders Ericsson has a name for what the audience misses: the iceberg illusion. The moment of brilliance on stage is the visible tip, and years of unseen practice lie underneath it.

##Andre Agassi's father reasoned that a child who hit 2,500 balls a day would be unbeatable.## Syed uses the example to show how much volume sits behind a champion, and how early it starts.

The thesis has limits. Syed accepts that its rules do not apply directly to less complex sports such as running. Later research also tempers it: a 2014 meta-analysis by Macnamara, Hambrick and Oswald found that deliberate practice explained about 26 percent of the differences in performance in games, 21 percent in music and 18 percent in sports. Practice explains a lot there, but not everything.

[[lessons]]
$$**Count the hard hours when you count practice.** A cook who makes the same easy omelette every morning for years is little better than in her first month, because nothing in the routine asks more of her. If she picks the one step that still goes wrong, such as the fold, and works only on it for a week, a single awkward week of that work can teach her more than a year of comfortable repetition. Look at your own practice and find the part that still feels uncomfortable.$$

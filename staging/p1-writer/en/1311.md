A shower with a very long pipe between the mixer and the showerhead runs too cold, then scalding, then cold again. The same swing shows up on a car lot, where a dealer's quick, well-meant fixes made everything worse.

[[story]]
In *Thinking in Systems*, Donella Meadows builds a model of a car dealership. It is a simulation, not a real business. The dealer wants enough cars on the lot to cover ten days of sales, because deliveries and purchases never match exactly from day to day and suppliers are sometimes late.

Meadows adds three delays that are common in real life. There is a perception delay: the dealer averages sales over five days before acting, so she is not fooled by a blip. There is a response delay: she makes up only a third of any shortfall with each order. And there is a delivery delay, since it takes five days for the factory to send the cars.

Then sales rise by 10 percent and stay there. Inventory drops. The dealer waits to be sure the change is real, then orders more. While those orders are on the way, inventory falls further, so she orders a little more again. When the cars finally arrive, the lot overflows. She cuts back, but old orders keep coming in, so she orders too little and the lot runs low again. The swings go on.

Meadows insists this is not because the dealer is stupid. She has no timely information, she cannot know what customers will do next, and her orders cannot act at once.

The dealer decides the oscillations are intolerable and resolves to react faster. Shortening her perception delay from five days to two makes the swings slightly worse. Shortening her response from three days to two makes them very much worse. ##When she lengthens the response delay from three days to six instead, the swings are greatly damped and the system settles at its new level fairly efficiently.##

Meadows has a phrase for the failed fix: high leverage, wrong direction. People trying to repair a system often reach by instinct for a lever that really does have a strong effect, and then pull it the wrong way. The dealer's trouble was never that she reacted too slowly. Given how the system was built, she was overreacting.

[[lessons]]
$$**When your action takes time to show its effect, wait for the result before correcting again.** If results arrive late, every new adjustment made before seeing them stacks on top of the last one and makes the swing bigger. Someone settling into a new flat can turn the heating dial once and leave it alone for a while, instead of nudging it every time the room still feels cold, and reach a comfortable temperature sooner. Deciding in advance how long you will wait before touching anything again keeps impatience from passing itself off as action.$$

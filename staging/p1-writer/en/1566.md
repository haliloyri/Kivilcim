A tight stomach before a certain phone call is usually treated as a nuisance to push through. The authors of one leadership book treat it as something to find in the body first, before any decision gets made. The advice is physical and takes under a minute.

[[story]]
One of the commitments in *The 15 Commitments of Conscious Leadership* is to feel your feelings all the way through. Jim Dethmer, Diana Chapman and Kaley Warner Klemp name anger, joy, sadness and sexual feelings. They argue that you cannot hold one of them back cleanly, because repressing one affects the others.

The authors treat a feeling as a sensation in the body, something you can locate and describe, not only a mood you talk about. So the method starts in the body. First you locate the sensation and describe it as accurately as you can, whether it is a tightness, a heat or a weight. Then you take a few gentle breaths and allow it, accept it, or even appreciate it.

The second part is expression. You match the feeling with a sound or a movement, usually without words: a groan, a laugh, a jump on the spot. The authors suggest asking what the sensation would sound like and how it would move. Talking it over with a coworker is not the same thing. Venting that way, they say, can keep you stuck in a loop.

They are blunt about skipping all this. Suppressing a feeling, in their view, causes physical, psychological and relational problems. The book also makes a claim about time: a feeling that is allowed to pass lasts around 90 seconds, whereas one that is repressed or recycled can harden into a long-lasting mood. The 90 seconds is the book's own claim, not a measurement.

So the order is: find the feeling in the body, breathe, give it a sound or a movement, and only then decide what to do. The decision comes last. Nothing in the sequence asks you to make the feeling go away. It asks you to let it be there, fully, for a short while, and then to act.

[[lessons]]
$$**Find the feeling in your body before you decide anything.** A feeling you skip does not disappear, and it tends to leak into the decision or turn up somewhere else later, often at a worse moment. A team lead who feels a knot in his stomach whenever a particular client calls could notice it, take one slow breath and only then pick up the phone. That small pause does not remove the knot, but it lets him speak knowing what he feels instead of being driven by it.$$

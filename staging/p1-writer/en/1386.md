On a sluggish day, James Clear would rather do ten squats than nothing. Five sprints, a single push-up, anything that keeps a zero off the record. The reason sits in a rule he keeps for his own workouts: he can miss one, but he will not miss two in a row.

[[story]]
Clear puts the rule in the chapter of *Atomic Habits* (2018) about tracking habits, and the chapter opens with a desk. In 1993 a bank in Abbotsford, Canada, hired a twenty-three-year-old stockbroker named Trent Dyrsmid. Every morning he set two jars on his desk, one holding 120 paper clips and one empty. After each sales call he moved a clip from the full jar to the empty one. Within eighteen months, Clear writes, he was bringing $5 million to the firm.

The jars made progress visible, and Clear carries the same logic to a calendar or a notebook, marking every day the habit gets done. Then he admits that life is messy. Sooner or later a box on the calendar stays empty, and he treats that as something to expect.

What matters is the next box. His rule fits in two sentences: "Missing once is an accident. Missing twice is the start of a new habit." ##The first mistake is never the one that ruins you, he writes; the danger is the spiral of repeated mistakes that follows it.##

He names what starts the spiral. It is all-or-nothing thinking, the belief that if you can't do something perfectly, you shouldn't do it at all. One skipped evening becomes a lost week because the plan in your head had no room for a small version.

That is why Clear calls the bad workouts often the most important ones. On a day when you feel slow, ten squats, five sprints or a single push-up still put a mark in the record where a zero would have gone. Those sluggish sessions protect the gains built on the good days, and they keep the chain intact until the next strong one.

A study outside the book points the same way. At University College London, Phillippa Lally asked 96 volunteers to pick one daily behaviour, such as eating a piece of fruit with lunch or running for fifteen minutes before dinner, and to rate each day how automatic it felt. Reaching the peak took 66 days on average, with a wide range between people. A single missed day had little effect on later progress, while many missed days lowered the level people reached.

[[lessons]]
$$**Make the first session after a miss too small to skip.** Someone who skips their evening walk once often decides the next day that they must make up for it with an hour-long walk, and the plan feels so heavy that the second evening goes empty too. If they walk for five minutes instead, the mark stays on the calendar and the habit keeps going. Thinking this way lets you return to your routine by the shortest, easiest route, without punishing yourself with a big recovery plan that you will never actually get round to starting.$$

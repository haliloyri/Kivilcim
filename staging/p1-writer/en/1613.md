Parents who can afford to help their grown children usually do, and nobody calls it a mistake. One large study of American wealth took accountants whose parents sent them cash and set them beside accountants who got nothing. The helped group did not come out ahead.

[[story]]
Thomas J. Stanley and William D. Danko, in *The Millionaire Next Door* (1996), give a name to something affluent families rarely examine: Economic Outpatient Care. The phrase covers money and other economic gifts that parents hand to their adult children and grandchildren, again and again, long after the children have left home.

The authors set this habit next to what they saw in the people who had built the most wealth. Those people had not taken regular cash from their parents. Their own adult children, in turn, stood on their own feet and paid their own way.

The comparison that gives the idea its weight uses accountants. Take households of accountants who receive cash gifts from parents, and line them up against accountant households that receive none. The first group holds about 57 percent of the net worth of the second. Same profession, smaller pile.

##The households that received the cash ended up with less than households in the same line of work that received none.##

Stanley and Danko offer a reason. A gift can give its recipient a false sense of security, and some recipients use that feeling to buy a home above their means. The house is the visible part, and the savings that might have grown beside it are the part nobody sees.

The authors report a tendency: adult children who receive such gifts accumulate less than comparable people who do not. The accountants' gap is a pattern in their data, and it does not prove that every gift hurts. What the book looks at is money that arrives regularly, year after year, into the same account.

Even so, the question the book leaves is one few givers ask. The gift is easy to see on the day it arrives, and what it does to the person receiving it every year is much harder to see.

[[lessons]]
$$**Give help in a form that ends.** A shop owner who tops up her grown son's wages every time his rent is due never lets him learn what his own month costs. If she agrees on a fixed amount for a set stretch of time, he has to plan the rest himself and sees where his money goes. The same works when you are the one receiving help: ask yourself which cost the help is quietly covering, and take that one over first. Both of you keep the help while the helped person builds something of their own.$$

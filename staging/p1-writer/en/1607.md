The daily latte survives this author's money advice untouched, and so does a personal stylist. The author's attention goes to a short list of other things, and the biggest of them is rarely a cup of coffee.

[[story]]
Ramit Sethi, author of *I Will Teach You to Be Rich* (2009), is known as much for the spending he leaves alone as for what he asks of readers. Sethi is famously indifferent to cutting the daily latte, and he avoids endlessly tracking every penny.

His attention goes to a short list: the five to ten financial decisions that drive most of a person's results. He calls them Big Wins. Automating savings is one. Negotiating a salary is another.

His conscious spending plan changes the picture of what a budget is for. A budget looks backward at what you spent, he says, and a plan lets you look forward. ##Spend extravagantly on what you love, and cut costs mercilessly on what you do not.##

The plan has four buckets: fixed costs, investments, savings and guilt-free spending. One summary puts fixed costs at roughly 50 to 60 percent of take-home pay, investments at about 10 percent and savings at 5 to 10 percent, with guilt-free spending as the remainder. By that count, fixed costs are the biggest bucket.

On his website he shows how to do the cutting. The Next $100 principle says to pick your top two discretionary expenses, his own being eating out and going out, and cut them by 25 to 33 percent over six months. The freed cash goes to investing and travel. The big wins, he says, come from focusing on a couple of categories rather than trimming everything a little.

Once the bills are paid and the saving and investing are automated, what is left in checking is yours to spend. His own example is a personal stylist, because he enjoys dressing well.

The 25 to 33 percent is the target he sets for his own two categories, and no one is promised the same result.

[[lessons]]
$$**Cut where it is big, and keep what you love.** Someone who gives up a daily takeaway coffee but never looks at the yearly bill for a gym they do not use has trimmed a small line and left a large one. Look at your two biggest optional costs first, and ask for each whether you would miss it at all. That lets you keep a small pleasure without guilt and free real money from the place that was easy to overlook.$$

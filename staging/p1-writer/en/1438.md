[[open]]
A ten-year-old girl with a fatal disease is on a waiting list for treatment. Volunteers in a psychology experiment were given one decision about her, and only one thing separated those who said yes from those who said no. It was not anything about the girl.

[[story]]
Her name was Sheri Summers. She was ten, her disease was fatal, and she was waiting her turn for treatment. That was the whole picture: a name, an age, an illness and a place in a queue. In *Against Empathy*, Paul Bloom describes an experiment from 1995 in which volunteers heard her story and were then asked a direct question: should she be moved up the list so that she is treated at once?

The question had a price that anyone could work out. Every child already waiting would be pushed one place back. Nobody was asked to dislike Sheri. The volunteers were choosing between her and the children whose turn she would take.

The psychologist Daniel Batson and his colleagues had divided the volunteers into two groups. One group was first asked to imagine what Sheri felt, to put themselves in her shoes. The other group was given no such request. Her illness, her age and her place in the queue were described the same way to both groups, and both groups faced the same choice about her.

Then came the answers. In the group that imagined her feelings, about three-quarters wanted her moved up. In the other group, about one in three did.

##The girl, the list and the question were the same. Only the request to imagine her feelings changed.##

Bloom reads the result as a warning about what empathy does. It narrows attention to a single person, and the children behind her on the list stay out of view. The volunteers who moved Sheri up had not been told anything new about her. They had only been asked to look at her closely, and she was the one they saw.

[[lessons]]
$$**Count the people you are not picturing.** A strong feeling for one person can tilt a fair decision without anyone noticing, because the others affected never come to mind. A manager who has just heard a moving story from one employee about a missed deadline may be ready to grant extra time, while the teammates who covered for the delay are nowhere in her head. Before she answers, she can list everyone the decision touches and ask whether she would give each of them the same, which turns a pull of the heart into a choice she can explain to all of them.$$

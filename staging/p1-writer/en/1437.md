A face appears on a screen for a tenth of a second. Volunteers are asked how trustworthy the person looks, and their answers match the ones other volunteers give with all the time they want. Longer looks mostly make them more confident.

[[story]]
That is the finding of a 2006 paper by Janine Willis and Alexander Todorov in *Psychological Science*. The volunteers were university students, and the faces were photographs of actors. In five experiments, each on a single trait judgment such as trustworthiness, competence or likeability, faces were shown for varying lengths of time, while a comparison group could look as long as it liked. A 100-millisecond look was enough for judgments to match those made without a time limit, and trustworthiness showed the strongest match. Going from 100 to 500 milliseconds made judgments more negative and raised confidence. From 500 milliseconds to a full second, judgments changed little.

Brian Tracy's *The Psychology of Selling* (2006) starts from the same fact of life: people form quick first impressions. According to a summary of the book, that is why he tells salespeople to plan their opening statements word for word.

The rest of his list is concrete. Dress neatly and professionally. Be punctual, organized and polite, because lateness or disorder makes a customer think the product or the company is of lower quality. Keep materials clean and well kept, and the summary's example is a brochure with a coffee stain on it.

He also leans on what he calls the power of suggestion, subtle social cues that plant in the customer's mind the picture of someone professional, in control and expert.

One figure in the summary deserves suspicion. It reports that 95 percent of a first impression is determined by clothing, and it names no study. The experiment above looked at faces, not clothes, and it measured a judgment of a stranger's trustworthiness, which is a different thing from how a sales call goes.

What the evidence supports is narrower. A judgment of a face forms in a fraction of a second, and more time mostly adds confidence. In the researchers' own reading, extra time increases confidence in a first impression without improving it. It does not show that the first minute of a call decides the sale, and the sources do not claim it does.

[[lessons]]
$$**Settle your opening in advance.** People size you up fast, so the part of the first moment that you control is worth deciding before you walk in. A job candidate can fix beforehand how they will arrive, what they will wear and what their first sentence is, so the first moment is no longer a question mark. That leaves their attention free for the person across the table, which is where the actual conversation, and the actual trust, gets built. Punctuality is one of the cheapest pieces to get right, since it only takes leaving earlier.$$

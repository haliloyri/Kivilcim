"In the morning when thou risest unwillingly, let this thought be present." The man who wrote that sentence to himself ruled the Roman Empire. He was arguing with his own reluctance to leave a warm bed, and he expected nobody else to read the argument.

[[story]]
Marcus Aurelius wrote the notes we now call *Meditations* in Greek, probably between 170 and 180, much of it while commanding armies on the empire's northern frontier. Two place names survive at the ends of the early books. One says "Among the Quadi at the Granua." The next says "This in Carnuntum." The Greek heading usually given to the work means something like "things to himself", and the twelve books were very probably never meant for publication.

The first book is a list of debts. Marcus goes person by person, naming what each taught him. From his teacher Rusticus he learned not to show himself off as a man who practises much discipline, to stay away from rhetoric, poetry and fine writing, and to write his letters simply. Rusticus also gave him the discourses of Epictetus from his own collection.

Book Five opens in bed. He tells himself he is rising to the work of a human being, then asks whether he was made to lie under the bedclothes and keep warm. He answers his own objection in the same breath: but this is more pleasant. Then he points himself to the little plants, birds, ants, spiders and bees, each doing its work. Rest is necessary, he concedes. Nature has set limits to that too, he replies, and he goes past them with food and drink while stopping short in his work.

##The most powerful man in the empire needed a written note to get himself out of bed.##

Book Two begins with a different kind of preparation. "Begin the morning by saying to thyself, I shall meet with the busy-body, the ungrateful, arrogant, deceitful, envious, unsocial." Straight after the list, he reminds himself that he cannot be angry with such people, because they are his kin. We are made for co-operation, he writes, like feet, like hands, like eyelids.

How the notebook survived is unclear. The earliest clear mention of it by another writer comes around 907, from Arethas of Caesarea, more than seven centuries after Marcus died.

That privacy is why the text still reads the way it does. Marcus is not teaching anyone. He is reminding himself, often of the same few things, in the plainest words he can find, exactly as Rusticus had taught him.

[[lessons]]
$$**Write yourself short notes for the hard moments.** Deciding in advance what you want to think and how you want to act gives you something to hold on to when the difficult moment actually arrives. A teacher who dreads Monday mornings can write a few lines on Sunday evening about which class she will face first and how she wants to handle it. Reading those lines over breakfast puts her own voice back in her head before the day starts. Because nobody else will see the note, it can stay honest and plain, with no need to sound wise.$$

In one class the child does remarkably well. In the next she is scattered, unproductive and sometimes disruptive, and her teacher decides she is choosing when to work. Gabor Maté, a physician who has ADD himself, says the teacher has misread what is going on.

[[story]]
In *Scattered Minds*, Maté tells of a college lecturer he had diagnosed with attention deficit disorder. The man's GP wanted a second opinion, and a psychiatrist wrote that the lecturer was able to focus on something he was really interested in, which for patients with ADD, he said, is very difficult.

Maté's answer is short. That is not what is very difficult. What can be immobilizingly difficult, he writes, is to arouse the brain's motivational apparatus when there is no personal interest. Being able to focus on what interests you does not rule out ADD. It only shows that a person with ADD needs a much higher level of motivation than other people to focus. Ignorance of this, he adds, has led many doctors to miss the diagnosis.

##The hard part is starting the brain's motivation when nothing in the task interests you.##

He also describes how the condition shifts with the setting. The same child may perform remarkably well in some classes and be scattered in others, and teachers may conclude the child decides when to buckle down. Many such children are openly disapproved of and shamed in front of the class. Maté writes that they are not purposely inattentive or disobedient. Forces they do not consciously choose do the deciding for them.

Some activities can pull a child into compulsive, hyperconcentrated attention. Maté reads this as a sign of poor attention regulation rather than a gift. Hyperfocus that shuts out the surroundings is often passive attention, as with television or video games, where the mind cruises on automatic. Active attention, with the mind fully engaged and the brain doing work, is mustered only in special circumstances of high motivation.

The book touches on dopamine as well. Attention deficit is thought to be partly due to an undersupply of it, but Maté calls such biochemical explanations dangerous oversimplifications.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask what a task asks of a person, not only how well they do on a good day.** Someone who sits happily for hours on a task they love has not shown they can bring the same attention to one they find dull. A manager who watches an employee turn out a spreadsheet in an afternoon for a favourite project, while expense reports pile up, can ask what holds the person in each job. That turns effort into a question about the task, and you find out what is missing from the dull one, which is more useful than a reminder to try harder.$$

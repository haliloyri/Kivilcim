Wind on a lake blurs the reflections, and weather moves across a mountain. The lake is the same lake and the mountain the same mountain. Kabat-Zinn uses exactly these two pictures to describe what goes on in a mind.

[[story]]
Jon Kabat-Zinn offers two images for thoughts and emotions in *Wherever You Go, There You Are* (1994). The first is the weather on a mountain. We tend to take weather personally, he says, yet its most striking quality is that it is impersonal. What he asks is that you meet it and know it for what it is, without ignoring it.

The second image is a lake. A still lake reflects its surroundings clearly, and when wind stirs up waves the reflections blur. You can notice both states without judging either one, the way you would look out of a window at a choppy lake on a windy afternoon and simply say that it is windy.

##From that distance two things come apart: what actually happened, and the story we tell ourselves about it.## Summaries describe the book's meditation as seeing thoughts as thoughts and experiences as experiences, and noticing the gap between them. A harsh remark is the event, and "they think I'm useless" is the story laid on top of it.

While you practise, the question "Am I doing this right?" may appear. Kabat-Zinn advises against answering it. The question is one more thought, it passes like a cloud, and you return to the breath and the present moment.

Nothing here asks you to flatten the mind into a calm surface. In the book's words, meditation is about letting the mind be as it is. If there are waves, you see the waves, and the wind too. A thought is allowed to stay, and it simply doesn't get a verdict attached. For the mountain, weather is what happens on it, and the mountain does not have to redefine itself for every cloud.

[[lessons]]
$$**Meet a thought the way you would meet weather.** A worried thought comes and goes on its own schedule, and it does not have to be read as a statement about who you are. Say a friend has not answered your message for two days, and the thought "they must be fed up with me" settles in each time you check the phone. You can call it what it is, a thought passing through, and send a second message or wait without building a case against yourself. Meeting it that way lets you hear the worry and still decide what to do on your own terms.$$

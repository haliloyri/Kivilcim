A teenager in Tokyo could turn up late and nobody minded, as long as a text message had gone out first. Under that unwritten rule, texting someone counted as being present with them, and it belonged to a generation with phones of its own.

[[story]]
Howard Rheingold's *Smart Mobs* (2002) opens on the streets of Tokyo, where he stopped dozens of young people to ask about texting and their *keitai*, their mobile phones. He also went along with flocks of teenagers as they converged on public places, steered by text messages.

Why the young? The anthropologist Mizuko Ito, whom Rheingold cites, argues that the phone freed them from the tyranny of the land line shared by inquisitive family members. Her team found that over 90 percent of Tokyo teenagers had a mobile phone.

With the phone came new manners. Texting someone counted as being present with them, and arriving late was fine if you stayed in contact by text.

The handset was built for them. NTT DoCoMo designed and sold its i-mode phone to young people as both fashion and technology, with easy menus, affordable prices and content such as reservations, ringtones and fortune telling. The company hired a former magazine launch employee to market it. One user quoted in the book calls the phone a remote control for your life.

The same pattern turned up elsewhere. In Helsinki, a group of Finns in their twenties, funded by an American entrepreneur, built a "shared urban living space" that combined a physical location, a virtual community, a mobile social network and a cooperative organization. A year after launch it had 300 members.

In Seattle, protesters against the WTO used text messages, mobile phones and the internet to organize. In Stockholm, fare-jumpers texted each other to warn of ticket inspectors on a train, and party organizers texted their address books, so guests had to show the message at the door.

##Rheingold calls such groups smart mobs: people able to act in concert even if they do not know each other.## They emerge, he writes, when communication and computing technologies amplify human talents for cooperation. Press accounts nicknamed texting teenagers like these thumb tribes, for the way they typed. Rheingold compares such networks of thinking, communicating humans to the swarm intelligence of ants, bees, fish and birds. He chose the word mob on purpose, for its dark resonances.

[[lessons]]
$$**Say aloud what a message counts for.** Tokyo teenagers had settled that a text counts as being present, but a group chat among colleagues has no such shared rule, so one person reads a late note as polite and another as careless. When a small group never states what a message counts for, each member judges by their own habit, and irritation builds quietly on one side. Agree once, in a single sentence, what an early warning text means to your group, and notice how many small annoyances disappear.$$

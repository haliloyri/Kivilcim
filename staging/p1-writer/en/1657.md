Evelyn Tribole and Elyse Resch have a short instruction for where a failed diet's anger should go, and it is not at the person holding the diet book. They begin with what sits on the bookshelf before they get anywhere near a plate.

[[story]]
Tribole and Resch, both registered dietitians, build *Intuitive Eating* (1995) on ten principles, and the first is a clearing-out. Discard the diet books and the food plans that promise a better life through restriction. The authors add an instruction about the anger that follows a failed plan: aim it at the false promises of diet culture, not at yourself.

Their reasoning is on the authors' own site. "The lies from diet culture have led you to feel as if you were a failure," it says. A plan that cannot be kept reads as a personal flaw, and the principle exists to move that verdict somewhere else.

Another principle removes the second half of the diet, the list of forbidden foods. The authors ask you to give yourself unconditional permission to eat, because forbidding foods tends to fuel cravings and bingeing. Permission is meant to end the struggle, not to start a new project.

They also name the thing that polices all of this. The food police are an internal voice that monitors the unreasonable rules dieting has created and hands out guilt and criticism whenever one is broken. The authors want that voice quieted.

None of this is a new set of rules. The Intuitive Eating site says the approach is not a diet or a food plan, has no pass-or-fail rules, and works through body awareness, which it calls interoceptive awareness.

The evidence is more modest than the movement's confidence. Intuitive eating is not designed for weight loss. Observational studies link it to less overeating and better self-regulation, but they cannot show cause, and reviews find too little evidence on long-term weight. Dietitians also warn that results vary, and people whose doctors prescribe a specific diet may not be able to follow it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Treat a broken food rule as a problem with the rule.** Someone who eats a biscuit at a colleague's desk and decides the whole day is ruined has handed their evening to a rule they never chose. Noticing that the rule, not the biscuit, produces the guilt lets you eat the next meal without punishing yourself. You can write down one food rule you follow and ask who made it, then keep it only if you still agree with it. That small audit costs a few minutes and shows which rules were ever yours.$$

The complaint is accurate: the supplier was late again, the budget was cut, the client changed the brief. According to the first commitment of one well-known leadership book, an accurate complaint can still leave you with nothing to do.

[[story]]
The first of the fifteen commitments in *The 15 Commitments of Conscious Leadership* is responsibility. Jim Dethmer, Diana Chapman and Kaley Warner Klemp define it as taking full responsibility for the circumstances of your life, and for your physical, emotional, mental and spiritual wellbeing.

The commitment is worded as a promise: to take that responsibility and to support others in doing the same. The last clause means it is also about how a leader treats the people around them.

Below the line, they say, a leader lives in a "to me" story. Something has gone wrong and someone is to blame. The complaints tend to point outward, and the authors say that such a person takes either more or less than 100 percent responsibility, blaming others or blaming themselves.

They name three roles that show up here: the victim, the villain and the hero. The victim says it was done to them, by a boss, a market or bad luck. The villain is whoever gets blamed. The hero rushes in to carry someone else's load. All three sit below the line, because in each of them the share of responsibility is off, too little or too much.

Above the line the story is "by me". The leader asks what their own part in the situation is and what they can do next, and helps other people ask the same.

The logic is straightforward. In a "to me" story the cause is outside you, so the fix is outside you as well, somewhere you cannot reach. Waiting for the market or a colleague to change is a way of spending days.

The authors are careful about one thing. Blaming yourself also counts as below the line, so full responsibility does not mean guilt. It means seeing what is yours and then doing something about it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Notice when a fair complaint hands the fix to someone else.** The complaint may be perfectly accurate, but while you tell it, control stays with the other party. A manager whose team's reports always arrive late could ask what she controls here, such as the deadline she gave, the format she asked for or the reminder she never sent. That question does not punish past mistakes, it shows her something she can act on, and it works better without guilt attached.$$

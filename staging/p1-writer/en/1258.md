The advice reached John F. Kennedy fast and all pointing one way. Destroy the missile sites with the full might of the military, then send American troops into Cuba. Every second of delay, his advisors said, put the country's safety and reputation at risk.

[[story]]
Ryan Holiday opens *Stillness Is the Key* with this moment, in October 1962, when as many as seventy million people were expected to die in the first exchange of strikes, a figure that was only a guess. While Kennedy slept on the night of the fifteenth, the CIA had identified Soviet ballistic missile sites under construction in Cuba, ninety miles from American shores. Aggression met with aggression felt like the only answer. To his advisors, it was.

Kennedy had reason to distrust that feeling. In April 1961 he had let the CIA pressure him into the Bay of Pigs invasion, and it failed embarrassingly. A few months later Khrushchev dominated him in a series of meetings in Vienna. This time he pushed back.

He had recently read Barbara Tuchman's *The Guns of August*, about overconfident leaders rushing into the First World War and then finding they could not stop it. So he asked everyone to slow down. He also asked a question nobody in the room had prioritized: why had the Russians done this, and what advantage were they after?

His handwritten notes from those days show the slowing down on paper. He wrote "Missile. Missile. Missile." and "Veto. Veto. Veto. Veto." During one meeting he drew two sailboats on a yellow legal pad. On White House stationery he wrote a single sentence: "We are demanding withdrawal of the missiles."

##Instead of the strike, he chose a blockade, a step nearly half his advisors opposed.## Unlike bombing, it could be undone, and it bought both sides time. He called it a "quarantine," a softer word than blockade. When things got tense he walked in the Rose Garden, went for long swims, sat in his rocking chair, and left the room so his advisors could argue freely without him.

The quarantine did not end the tests. A Russian tanker approached the line, Soviet submarines surfaced, and an American U-2 spy plane was shot down over Cuba, killing its pilot. Kennedy told his advisors the first step did not worry him; both sides climbing to the fourth and fifth did.

On October 26, eleven days into the crisis, Khrushchev wrote to him. The two of them, he said, were pulling on a rope with a knot of war in the middle, and the harder they pulled, the tighter it would get. The Soviets then signalled they would remove the missiles, and Kennedy pledged that the United States would not invade Cuba.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before a big decision, buy time and keep your options open.** Under pressure, the first forceful move that comes to mind is often the hardest one to take back, while a step that buys a few days gives both you and the other side room to think again. A manager who receives a furious email from a client can draft the sharp reply, then walk over and ask a colleague what the client actually wants before pressing send. That pause lets you decide by looking ahead to what happens after your move, and to how the other person is likely to answer it.$$

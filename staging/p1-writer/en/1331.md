Late one night, William James sat reading the lectures of the philosopher Charles Peirce, at a point when his own life was coming apart and he had thought about ending it. Before he went to bed he had made a deal with himself. It would run for exactly one year.

[[story]]
Mark Manson tells James's story in *The Subtle Art of Not Giving a F*ck*, in a chapter about choice and responsibility. It was around 1872. The terms of the deal were simple and severe. For twelve months James would believe that he was 100 percent responsible for everything that happened in his life, and he would do everything he could to change his circumstances, however hopeless they looked.

There was a second clause. If nothing had improved by the end of the year, he would accept that he was powerless over his own life.

##James later called that decision his rebirth.## Most of what had gone wrong for him was not his fault. For a year he treated all of it as his to deal with anyway, and his life turned.

Manson uses the episode to take apart what he calls the responsibility/fault fallacy, the habit of treating the two words as if they meant the same thing. Some problems, he writes, are not our fault, and yet we are still responsible for them.

His illustration is a doorstep. You wake up one morning and find a newborn baby someone has left outside your door. You did not put it there, and nobody could reasonably blame you. But the baby is now in front of you, and you have to decide what happens next: whether you take it in, who you call, what you do. Whatever you choose, the results of that choice belong to you.

The distinction Manson draws is about time. Fault comes from choices that have already been made; it points backward. Responsibility comes from the choices you are making now, and it renews itself every hour. Arguing about fault can go on for years without moving anything. A responsible choice can be made this afternoon.

He puts it in one line that gets quoted a lot: "Accepting responsibility for our problems is thus the first step to solving them." That doesn't mean pretending you caused everything. It means noticing that the next move is usually yours, whoever made the mess, and that waiting for the right person to take it costs your time, not theirs.

The baby on the step and the man with Peirce's lectures meet at the same question. Whose fault it is matters less than what you will do now.

[[lessons]]
$$**Separate who caused the problem from who acts next.** Working out the cause of a problem looks backward and can drag on, while the next decision usually can't wait for that argument to finish. Someone who inherits a half-finished project from a colleague who quit can spend weeks explaining why it's in such a state, or can call the client today, list the gaps and make the first three decisions. Keeping fault and responsibility apart lets you stay angry about how things happened, if you need to, while still being the one who moves things forward.$$

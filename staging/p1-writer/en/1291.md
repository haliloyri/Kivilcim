[[open]]
One of Robert Kiyosaki's fathers had a Ph.D. and ran an entire state's schools. The other had left school early. Both were working men, both respected, and both gave a boy advice about money that could not have been more different.

[[story]]
Kiyosaki grew up with two fathers. The first was his own, whom he calls Poor Dad, a man with a Ph.D. who ran the state education system in Hawaii. The second was the father of his best friend Mike, whom he calls Rich Dad. This one had left school early and gone on to build a business empire in Hawaii.

Poor Dad's advice was clear and familiar. Go to school, get good grades, find a safe job. It was what a man with his résumé believed, and he said it to his own son.

At nine, Kiyosaki started learning from Rich Dad alongside Mike. The two boys worked in his store for ten cents an hour, and after a while Kiyosaki complained about the pay. Rich Dad did not raise it, and treated the complaint as part of the lesson.

He sat on the other side of almost every question. Poor Dad held that the love of money is the root of all evil. Rich Dad held that the lack of money is. Same words, opposite conclusions.

Then there was a phrase that came up whenever something cost too much. Poor Dad said it easily: "I can't afford it." In Rich Dad's house, the sentence was banned. He asked another one in its place: "How can I afford it?"

##The first sentence closes the conversation. The second sends the mind off to look for an answer.##

Time tested the two men differently. Poor Dad resigned from his state job to run for lieutenant governor, lost the election, and found himself out of work. Rich Dad went on to become one of the richest men in Hawaii.

Kiyosaki drew the conclusion that gives the book its backbone. The poor and the middle class work for money. The rich make money work for them.

[[lessons]]
$$**Turn a flat no about money into a question.** A shop owner whose oven has broken might say she can't afford a new one and leave it at that, which ends the thinking. If she asks how she could afford it, she starts to look at prepaid orders, a smaller model, or splitting the cost with a baker down the street. You get to see options that the flat answer hid, and you are still free to decide the answer is no once you have looked at them, which is a better position than never having looked.$$

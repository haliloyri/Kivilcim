[[story]]
Tara Brach planned to give birth at home, with a midwife and no drugs. She tells it in *Radical Acceptance* (2003). She wanted to be as awake and present as she could, and she trusted her meditation and yoga practice to help her "go with the flow." She felt rested and ready.

Early labor went the way she hoped. She knew that fighting a contraction only made it worse, so she relaxed into each one and breathed and made sounds without inhibition. She rode the pain as part of the process.

Then her son's head began to crown. The pain shot up, and it was no longer something she could breathe into. *This much pain has got to mean something is going wrong*, she thought. Her whole body tightened, her deep slow breaths gave way to the quick shallow ones of panic, and every bit of resolve she had brought into the room was gone.

Her midwife had seen this fear before. "Nothing's wrong, honey," she said. "It's all completely natural, it's just painful." Brach had to hear it several times before it began to sink in, and then, in the middle of the burning, the pressure and the exhaustion, she remembered to breathe deeply again. ##It was just pain, not wrong.##

The pain itself was real. When Brach answered it with fear, she added a second thing to it, the belief that something was wrong. She points to the saying "pain is inevitable, but suffering is optional," and to the Buddha's teaching that we suffer when we cling to an experience or resist it. Fear of pain, she writes, is often the most unpleasant part of a painful experience.

The same pattern ran slower through four years of chronic illness. Each bout of fatigue or indigestion flooded her mind with stories: something was very wrong, maybe she was seriously sick, she had pushed too hard, the black tea must have upset her stomach. Pain had become a comment on her character. She wonders whether the worries that tightened her muscles against it made her exhaustion worse.

Brach quotes Jon Kabat-Zinn, who calls symptoms, and our feelings about them, messengers. A king who disliked the message might have the messenger killed, he says, and denying the message or raging against it is no wiser a way to heal. When heat burns, we pull the hand from the fire.

[[lessons]]
$$**Separate the sensation from the alarm.** When something hurts, a second reaction often arrives right behind it: the belief that this should not be happening and that it means something is badly wrong, as if the pain were itself a verdict. Someone with a pounding headache on deadline day can feel that second layer clearly, the dread and self-blame stacked on top of the ache. Once you can tell the two apart, you can ask the one practical question, what your body is asking for, whether that is water, a break or a call to the doctor, and you can answer it before anything else.$$

[[story]]
In *The Culture Code* (2018), Daniel Coyle writes about Johnson & Johnson and a document called the Credo. It is 311 words long. It is carved into a granite wall at the company's headquarters in New Jersey and displayed at its business locations. Anyone walking in can read what the company says it stands for.

Then came 1982. The Tylenol poisoning was a crisis, and Coyle uses it as a case of a crisis testing a culture. When the pressure came, the company's answer was found in those 311 words. The poisoning was not something a slogan could fix, and the company turned to what it had already written down.

##The words on the wall were not new that year, and managers had already been arguing over them.##

That is the second half of Coyle's story. J&J held regular meetings in which senior managers discussed and challenged the Credo. They went through it and debated whether it still fit. Coyle gives that habit as much weight as the granite. A Credo that is only read is a decoration. A Credo that people argue over becomes something they can reach for when a hard choice lands.

Coyle ties this to what he calls purpose. His view is that purpose is built by linking where the group is now to where it wants to go, using a few clear signals repeated often. A wall with 311 words is one signal. The meetings, where managers test the words against actual cases, are the repetition.

A note on the sources: summaries disagree about details of the crisis, such as how many bottles were recalled, so no such figures are used here. What the book offers is the pattern. The text was physical, it was discussed in ordinary meetings, and in the crisis it was the first place the company looked.

[[lessons]]
$$**Discuss your values before a crisis, not during one.** A small repair shop may have a card on the wall saying it puts customers first, and no one has talked about what that means in practice. Once the owner asks the team, in an ordinary week, whether that card would change a decision about an expensive part, the sentence becomes something staff can lean on. When a hard choice comes, the group already knows which line to reach for. Without that talk, the card stays on the wall and the old habits decide the matter instead.$$

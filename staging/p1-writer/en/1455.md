The Dalai Lama cups his hands into a small circle, then opens them wide. It is how he shows the difference between looking at his own country's troubles alone and looking at the suffering of many people. Between those two shapes, he says, something about his own pain changes.

[[story]]
In *The Book of Joy* (2016), Douglas Abrams describes the moment the Dalai Lama explains how he lives with exile. He says that many of his people have become refugees and that there are many difficulties in his own country.

When he looks only at those difficulties, he says, he worries. When he widens his view to include other people's suffering, including Hui Muslims in China, his own suffering lessens. In his words: "When we see these things, we realize that not only do we suffer, but so do many of our human brothers and sisters."

##Abrams calls this a shift from anguish toward compassion, not a denial of pain.## The Dalai Lama does not pretend that Tibet is fine. He is changing what he looks at, and the angle changes what he feels.

Abrams adds an observation here. The Dalai Lama does not set his situation against other people's, as in "mine is worse" or "theirs is worse." He sees it as connected to theirs, which is what lets the wider circle lighten the load instead of only adding to it.

He then turns to the place he left. Had he stayed in the Potala in Lhasa, he says, he would have stayed in what has often been described as "a golden cage." His conclusion is blunt: "So, personally, I prefer the last five decades of refugee life."
He also describes what home means to him, in a sentence that fits on a postcard: "Wherever you have friends that's your country, and wherever you receive love, that's your home."

Abrams links all this to a saying and to an old Buddhist text. The saying is that pain is inevitable and suffering is optional. The text, the Sallatha Sutta, separates the physical pain itself from the added suffering that comes from how we respond to it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Widen the circle around your own trouble.** After a rejection at work, someone might look up and notice how many others in the same field are facing the same kind of door closing right now. The trouble stays real, and the loss still stings. But the narrower view says "this is happening to me" and the wider one says "this is something people go through", which leaves room to ask what the new situation makes possible. It also tends to ease the sense of being singled out, and that makes the next small step easier to see and to take.$$

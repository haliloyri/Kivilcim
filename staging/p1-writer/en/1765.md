A dial painter earned about a cent and a half for every watch face she finished, and she kept the brush sharp with her lips. The factory chemists handled the same paint with tongs.

[[story]]
In *Radium Girls*, Kate Moore takes the reader to the US Radium Corporation in Orange, New Jersey, in the years around the First World War. Young women painted watch and clock faces with a paint that glowed in the dark, made of radium powder, zinc sulfide, gum arabic and water. A brush lost its point after a few strokes, and supervisors showed them that shaping the tip with their lips was quicker than rinsing it. The routine was called lip, dip, paint.

Pay came by the dial, about a cent and a half each, so 250 dials earned roughly 3.75 dollars. The company had circulated literature to doctors about radium's harmful effects, and its own chemists used lead screens, tongs and masks. The painters had no protection at all and were told the paint was harmless. Some, believing it, brushed it onto their nails, teeth and faces so that they glowed.

With every stroke they swallowed a little radium, and it settled in their bones. Dentists noticed first: loose teeth, ulcers in the jaw, extractions that would not heal. Mollie Maggia was among the first to die. The company denied responsibility and pointed to other causes.

Grace Fryer spent about two years looking for a lawyer who would take the case. Raymond Berry, from Newark, filed suit in 1927, and four other women joined: Edna Hussman, Katherine Schaub and the sisters Quinta McDonald and Albina Larice.

The case ended out of court in 1928. Each woman received 10,000 dollars, an annuity of 600 dollars a year and her medical bills, a fraction of what they had sought. All five were dead by the 1930s. Moore presents their cases as a turning point for the rights and safety of workers who handle dangerous substances.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask what a reassurance rests on.** The people who called the paint harmless gave their own chemists tongs and screens while the painters put brushes to their lips, and nobody had shown the women what they were swallowing. If a landlord tells you that the old paint in a flat is nothing to worry about, a sensible first step is to ask for the test result or the safety sheet, and to notice whether he does anything different for himself. Once you can tell an assurance from evidence, you can accept a risk because of what was shown to you, and asking will feel like an ordinary step at work and at home.$$

[[open]]
Lying in an MRI scanner, a group of young adults looked at a set of upsetting images. Twelve hours later they lay down in the same scanner and saw the same images again. For half of them, something in the brain had quietly changed in between.

[[story]]
Matthew Walker describes the experiment in *Why We Sleep*, in a chapter called "Dreaming as Overnight Therapy." He had decided to test the old saying that time heals all wounds. His suspicion was that the saying needed an amendment: perhaps it was not time that healed, but time spent in dream sleep.

The theory rested on a chemical oddity. During REM sleep, the stress-related chemical noradrenaline is completely shut off in the brain, the only time in the twenty-four-hour day when that happens. Meanwhile the emotion and memory regions, the amygdala and the hippocampus, switch back on. Walker proposed that the dreaming brain reprocesses the day's charged memories in this calm chemical setting. He put it as two jobs at once: sleeping to remember the details, and sleeping to forget the painful emotional charge wrapped around them. Most of our strongest childhood memories are emotional, he notes, yet recalling them rarely brings back the original intensity.

The test was simple. Half the participants saw the images in the morning and again in the evening, awake in between. The other half saw them in the evening and again the next morning, after a full night of sleep. Both groups also rated how emotional they felt each time.

##Those who had slept reported feeling significantly less emotional, and the scans agreed.## Reactivity in the amygdala dropped sharply, and the prefrontal cortex, which brakes emotional reactions, re-engaged. The group that stayed awake showed no such softening; their reactions were as strong as the first time, or stronger. The quality of each person's REM sleep predicted how much relief they got overnight.

Walker later met Dr. Murray Raskind, who worked at a Veterans Affairs hospital near Seattle. Raskind had been giving veterans with PTSD a cheap blood-pressure drug, prazosin, and within weeks their recurring nightmares eased. Prazosin, it turned out, also suppresses noradrenaline in the brain, the very condition Walker's theory described. A caution belongs here: a large 2018 trial in veterans with chronic PTSD found prazosin no better than a placebo for nightmares.

[[lessons]]
$$**Give a painful day one night of sleep before you decide how you feel about it.** Sleep can soften the emotional weight of an event without erasing the event itself, which means the verdicts we reach at midnight are often reached at the heaviest point of that weight. Someone who received a harsh comment from their boss in a meeting can write the reply that evening and only press send the next morning, after breakfast. That small delay lets you look at the same event, details intact, with a calmer brain than the one that first reacted.$$

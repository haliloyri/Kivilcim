For many years one sentence sat in the textbooks: the adult brain does not make new cells. It told people that what was lost could never be replaced. A neurologist later wrote that this sentence had been dropped.

[[story]]
For a long time scientists believed that an adult brain cannot make new nerve cells. You lived with the ones you were born with. Losses were permanent. Neurologist David Perlmutter writes in *Grain Brain* that this belief has been given up. When the hippocampus is damaged, learning and remembering new things becomes harder, so the old view left people with a sense of helplessness.

According to Perlmutter, the hippocampus, the small region tied to learning and memory, can produce new neurons in adulthood. Its name is Greek for "seahorse", a small curled creature that this small curled region is said to resemble. A protein helps drive the process: BDNF, short for brain-derived neurotrophic factor. "Neurotrophic" means nourishing, and Perlmutter describes the protein as supporting the survival and growth of neurons.

He then lists what raises BDNF. Aerobic exercise comes first, and he counts it among the strongest ways. Calorie restriction or fasting and a ketogenic diet follow: when carbohydrate drops, the liver turns fat into fuel molecules called ketones, which he says encourage new brain cells. The omega-3 fat DHA, found in oily fish, and curcumin from turmeric are on the list too. He counts sleep as part of the picture. ##The list is made of ordinary things from daily life, not medicines.##

Part of the evidence comes from animal studies. How many new neurons an adult human hippocampus actually makes is still debated among researchers. So the list is a set of suggestions.

Still, the direction of the question is interesting, because it reaches well beyond the laboratory and into an ordinary Tuesday. If the brain is a fixed structure handed over at birth, there is nothing to do. Nothing. If it can change, today's habits are not only about today. Sleep, movement and what you eat become small, regular contributions to memory.

[[lessons]]
$$**Choose habits for more than today.** If the brain can still change in adulthood, the effect of daily choices may not end when the day does. Someone who spends every evening in front of a screen and decides to go for a walk instead is making room for pleasure and also for a habit that supports memory. Seeing it that way makes it easier to move walking, sleep or food from the "someday" list into the calendar, and it lets you keep the first step small.$$

Fall asleep at eleven, and the hours between five and seven in the morning are where a sleep scientist found something unexpected. Those two hours were doing work on the skills learned the day before. They are also the two hours most people treat as the easiest to give up.

[[story]]
The scientist is Matthew Walker, and in *Why We Sleep* he describes almost a decade of research on motor skills. People learned a movement sequence, slept, and were tested again. After a night of sleep they were faster and more accurate, and the movements had started to run below conscious effort, the way a well-drilled routine does for an athlete.

The gains tracked one feature of the night. They depended on stage 2 NREM sleep, and specifically on the bursts of brainwaves called sleep spindles in the last two hours of an eight-hour night. The more spindles appeared over the part of the brain that had been trained, the better the performance on waking. Other groups have found the same local effect. Since then, studies have shown sleep improving the motor skills of junior, amateur and elite athletes in sports from tennis to rowing.

Walker pictures the Olympic coach who keeps athletes practicing late, then wakes them early to train again. That coach may be cutting away the very phase that fine-tunes the skill. When tiny margins separate a gold medal from last place, he writes, an advantage like that is worth having. Usain Bolt seems to have agreed. He took naps on many occasions in the hours before breaking the world record and before Olympic finals he won.

Walker now gives this talk to national basketball and football teams, calling sleep a legal performance enhancer. With less than eight hours a night, and especially less than six, time to physical exhaustion drops by 10 to 30 percent and aerobic output falls. A 2014 study of young competitive athletes found that chronic short sleep across a season predicted a far higher injury risk. Sleep after a game, he adds, speeds muscle repair and restocks energy stores.

##To make it concrete, he shows teams the sleep-tracker data of one NBA player, Andre Iguodala of the Golden State Warriors.## After nights of more than eight hours, compared with less, Iguodala played 12 percent more minutes and scored 29 percent more points per minute. After short nights his turnovers rose 37 percent and his fouls 45 percent. It is one player's record, not a trial. In a locker room it still lands harder than a lecture.

[[lessons]]
$$**On the night after you practice a new skill, protect the end of your sleep.** The brain finishes much of the day's practice during the night, and especially in the early-morning hours, so the hour you cut to start early is often the very hour doing that work. A nurse learning to place IV lines can set her alarm later the night after a training day instead of getting up early to review her notes. That way part of the effort gets completed while you sleep, and the next session shows whether your hands feel the difference.$$

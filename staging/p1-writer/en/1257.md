Edward had money, a high-powered executive job and anxiety that never switched off. When someone suggested he see a professional, he explained why that was unnecessary. Three other people in his life simply had to change first, and then he would be fine.

[[story]]
The suggestion came from Susan Jeffers, who tells the exchange in *Feel the Fear and Do It Anyway*. Edward's list was precise. If his wife were more loving, if his boss stopped leaning on him, if his son stopped taking drugs, everything would settle. He saw nothing for himself to work on, because it was all their fault.

Jeffers opens her chapter on responsibility by asking whether you are playing the victim or taking responsibility for your life. Most of us think we already do the second. She disagrees. The victim role, she writes, is subtle and takes many forms, and plenty of independent, well-paid people play it without noticing.

Her claim is blunt: in some way, consciously or not, we are choosing to stay in the lousy job or the destructive relationship. Then she draws a careful line. She is not asking you to believe you are responsible for everything that happens to you, only that you cause your reactions to it. Her first definition of taking responsibility is never blaming anyone else for what you are being, doing, having or feeling. The second is not blaming yourself either.

She puts herself in the dock too. For years she spent hours on the phone with girlfriends, going through the faults of the men in her life: one was always late, one was stingy, one played too much golf. She calls it the Moan and Groan Society, and the payoff was plain. As long as the men were to blame, she never had to create her own happiness.

Payoffs are the center of the chapter. A woman named Jean hated her job and kept saying "if only": if only the market were better, if only her skills were stronger. Looked at honestly, staying gave her a lot. She never risked rejection in a job search. The work was easy, she knew she could handle it, it took no extra energy, and it was fairly secure.

##Once Jean saw those payoffs, she had at least three choices.## She could stay and stay miserable, stay and decide to enjoy the job, or go and find a better one. She left and found a new job, moving only once she saw that the payoffs, and not the market, had been holding her.

Finding your own payoffs, Jeffers says, takes a pencil and paper. If they hide from you, ask a friend; sometimes others see them first.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask what a stuck situation is giving you.** When we complain about something for months and still stay, it is usually protecting us from something we would rather not face, and the complaint keeps that protection out of sight. Someone who never raises the unpaid bills with a flatmate may be buying, week after week, freedom from one tense evening at the kitchen table. Once you name that protection on paper, you can choose between staying and changing with your own options in view, without hunting for someone to blame, yourself included, and you can decide what the first small move would be.$$

&&Take one complaint you have repeated lately: what does staying exactly where you are quietly spare you from having to face?&&

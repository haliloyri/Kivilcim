Two minutes is a strangely small number to build a rule on. Yet it is the point where, according to David Allen, a tiny task flips from being worth doing later to being worth doing right now, because filing it away starts to cost more than finishing it.

[[story]]
In *Getting Things Done* (2001), Allen states the rule in one line: if an action will take less than two minutes, it should be done at the moment it is defined. He does not offer it as a moral about discipline. He offers it as arithmetic.

The arithmetic goes like this. Every item you postpone has to be stored somewhere, tracked and looked at again later. Allen calls two minutes the efficiency cutoff, the point where it starts taking longer to store and track an item than to deal with it. Below that line, the list is a more expensive place for the task than your own hands.

##There is an order to it: first decide the very next action, then apply the rule.## You cannot judge whether something takes two minutes until you know what the action is. A vague worry about a client is not a two-minute task. Writing that you will phone the client's office to ask for the contract number is one, and the rule tells you to do it now rather than park it.

The rule sits inside a short routine for everything that lands in your inbox. For each item you ask what the action is. If it takes under two minutes, you do it. If it takes longer, you either hand it to someone else or defer it onto a list, so nothing sits half-decided in a pile.

He knows the rule can be misused. Allen warns against becoming a slave to spending your day on two-minute actions, and he says the rule applies mainly to new input, the things that have just arrived and are being sorted. Two minutes, he adds, is a guideline.

In his own working life, Allen says, there is no backlog of two-minute items because they are all done. He also reports that many executives told him this one habit was worth the price of admission for the whole method.

[[lessons]]
$$**Do the tiny thing as it arrives, but only after you know what it is.** Someone who gets a message asking which day suits for a visit can answer in a minute, and if she parks it in a list, she will pay for it later by reopening the message, finding the calendar and re-reading the thread. The same person should not apply the rule to the half-day report that also landed this morning. Knowing the cutoff lets you clear small things at once and keep your good hours for the work that needs them.$$

A well, a little girl at the bottom of it, and a crowd of people who could not stop thinking about her. Nobody should be faulted for caring. The uncomfortable part comes when you ask who else needed help and received far less of that attention.

[[story]]
In 1987, in Midland, Texas, a little girl got stuck in a well. The case became known as Baby Jessica, and Paul Bloom uses it in *Against Empathy* as an example of something odd about human attention: people care far more about one child they can picture than about crises that affect many more people.

Bloom does not conclude that feeling for her was wrong. His claim is narrower. Empathy, he argues, is a poor guide for decisions, though it can motivate kindness. A well gives the feeling a face and a place, and a larger problem rarely offers either. People who followed that rescue felt a pull toward action, and Bloom does not wave the pull away. He wants a second step to come after it.

So he proposes a different way of caring, which he calls rational compassion. It keeps the concern for other people's suffering and adds the reasoning of costs and benefits. The question changes from "who is in front of me?" to "what would help the most?" Bloom holds that people are capable of deciding in that way.

For a model of it, he points to the effective altruism movement, which draws on the philosopher Peter Singer. The movement asks donors to aim their giving where it improves the most lives. In practice that means setting what a gift costs beside what it achieves, and comparing the result with other uses of the same money. Bloom treats it as close to his own ideal.

Next to the well, the contrast is plain: rational compassion looks at the whole list of people who need help, including those who never made the news, and picks the action that does the most for them.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask where your help would do the most.** Good intentions point at the person in front of you, and a quick comparison can point somewhere else. A woman who wants to support a local clinic after reading one patient's story could first look at what that clinic would do with the money and what a few other places would do with the same amount. That comparison lets her keep the feeling that started the idea while choosing the gift that reaches the most people.$$

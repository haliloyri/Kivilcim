A taped voice, a balloon popping, a cotton swab of alcohol held under a baby's nose. Some four-month-olds flail and cry. Others lie calm. A Harvard psychologist wrote down which was which, and then he waited for years.

[[story]]
In *Quiet* (2012), Susan Cain starts by separating two words that most people use as one. Shyness is the fear of social disapproval or humiliation. Introversion is a preference for environments that are not over-stimulating, and two famous names show how far apart the two can sit: Barbra Streisand is outgoing and still gets severe stage fright, while Bill Gates is quiet and does not seem to care what others think.

The first signs of that difference, Cain says, appear before a child can talk. In 1989 the Harvard psychologist Jerome Kagan began a study of about 500 four-month-old babies. For forty-five minutes each baby met taped voices, popping balloons, colorful mobiles and a cotton swab with the smell of alcohol. It was a lot for a small person.

Some babies pumped their arms and legs and cried. Kagan called them high-reactive. Others stayed calm through the same forty-five minutes, and he called them low-reactive. He predicted that the criers would grow into quiet, introverted children, so he brought them back at two, four, seven and eleven and put new things in front of them, among them a clown and a woman in a gas mask. Most of the children matched his predictions.

Fear did come into it for some. By one reviewer's account, about a quarter of the high-reactive children apparently developed social anxiety disorder, and Carl Schwartz, who retested the group as adults, concluded that we can stretch our personalities, but only up to a point.

Kagan was careful about one point. High and low reactivity, he insisted, were not the only routes to introversion or extroversion. A crying baby is not a verdict, only a tendency that shows up early.

##The babies who flailed at the balloons were reacting to the amount of stimulation, not to anyone's opinion of them.## Set beside the definition of shyness, that gives a plain picture. Pulling back from noise and fearing people can look identical from across a room, yet they are two separate things.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask whether someone is afraid or overloaded.** A colleague who skips the loud team lunch every week might dread being judged, or might simply be worn out by the noise. Those two cases call for different help: reassurance for the first, a quieter table for the second. If you find out which one you are looking at before you coax anyone out of their shell, you stop offering courage to a person who needed calm. The lunch might then move to a quieter room, and the colleague might start talking.$$

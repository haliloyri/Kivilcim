On a hike, every rustle in the underbrush sends a jolt of fear through you, and it does so whether or not a snake is there. Robert Wright thinks that jolt explains more about your mind than you would expect, including why you cannot sleep before a talk.

[[story]]
In *Why Buddhism Is True* (2017), Wright starts from a design problem. Natural selection built the human mind to favor traits that get genes into the next generation. Contentment is not on that list. Never-ending dissatisfaction, he says, helped our ancestors do the job.

The hiking jolt shows how it works. A false alarm costs you a moment of fright. A missed snake costs more, so selection favors the alarm: better safe than sorry. The fear can be doing exactly what it was shaped to do, even when no snake is anywhere near.

Satisfaction follows the same logic. The happiness of reaching a goal tends to fade, and evolutionary psychology has an explanation. Without the fading, animals would stop pursuing food and sex. The glow after a win is short by design.

The old machinery also misfires. Public speaking makes many people anxious, sometimes to the point of lying awake before a presentation. Wright points out that we are not designed for that situation at all. Ancestral life had no large public talks, so an old response fires in a setting it was never shaped for.

His answer is mindfulness meditation, in a secular form without supernatural beliefs. Quieting the mind enough lets you start loosening some of the mechanisms of distortion. If you change how you relate to anxiety, so that you no longer identify with it, Wright calls that an incremental realization of not-self. Watching a feeling is not the same as pushing it down.

On a retreat, he reports, the bounds of himself dissolved. A bird singing outside the meditation hall felt no less a part of him than a tingling in his foot.

Critics question both halves of his project: the naturalized version of Buddhism and the evolutionary psychology behind it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Treat a recurring worry as an old alarm before you obey it.** A cashier whose stomach tightens every time a long queue forms can learn to say to herself that this is the alarm going off, not a report on how the shift is going. The label does not make the tightness vanish, but it gives her a second between the feeling and what she does next. Try it with the next worry that returns, and write down what you did in that second.$$

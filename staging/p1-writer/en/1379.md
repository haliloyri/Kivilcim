A life insurance agent's day is a string of refusals on the phone. Two agents can hear the same "no" from the same kind of customer and walk away telling themselves very different stories about it. Martin Seligman bet that the story mattered more than the sales talent, and Met Life let him test it.

[[story]]
Met Life's president, John Creedon, had a costly problem: many of the new agents the company trained each year soon quit. He brought it to Martin Seligman at the University of Pennsylvania, whose work on helplessness had turned into a question about how people explain bad events to themselves.

In *Learned Optimism* (1990), Seligman breaks that explanation into three dimensions. Permanence asks whether the cause is always there or just this time. Pervasiveness asks whether it spoils everything or one area. Personalization asks whether it is all your fault or partly the circumstances. A pessimist explains a setback as permanent, pervasive and personal. An optimist explains the same setback as temporary, specific and not wholly their doing. With good events the pattern flips: the optimist sees a success as lasting and earned, the pessimist as luck.

Seligman's team had a questionnaire that scored this style by asking people to explain imagined bad events. They gave it to Met Life agents. Those in the top half sold 37 percent more insurance in their first two years than those in the bottom half. In a second study of new hires, top-half scorers stayed in the job at twice the rate of bottom-half scorers. Seligman and his colleague Peter Schulman published both results in 1986, in a psychology journal rather than a company memo.

Then the company went further, and hired people it would normally have turned away. ##Met Life hired a "special force" of 129 applicants who had failed the industry's standard career-profile test but scored as strong optimists.## By Seligman's account, they outsold the pessimists in the regular force by 21 percent in their first year and by 57 percent in their second.

Everyone involved sold the same policies at the same prices and heard the same refusals all day. What the questionnaire picked up was the sentence an agent said to themselves after hanging up, and that sentence shaped whether they dialed the next number.

That is the book's real claim. Explanatory style is a habit of thought, Seligman argues, and habits of thought can be noticed, questioned and changed. The rest of *Learned Optimism* is about how.

[[lessons]]
$$**Check your explanation of a setback for "always," "everything" and "me."** When a dinner you cooked falls flat with guests, "I can't do anything right" stretches one evening across your whole life and character. "This recipe didn't work tonight, and the oven ran hot" leaves you something specific to fix next time. When you catch one of those three words in your own account, swap it for what actually happened. You then start the next attempt from a fixable detail instead of a verdict about yourself, and the next try feels worth making at all.$$

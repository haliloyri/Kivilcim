Report cards that always said something like: does not pay attention in class, does not sit still. A father who called him a lazy bum. A man of fifty-one who gave Gabor Maté a written account of all this and let the doctor print it as it was.

[[story]]
In *Scattered Minds*, Maté describes adults with severe attention deficit disorder in their thirties, forties and fifties who have never held a long-term job. Some have never read a book from cover to cover. Their self-esteem, he writes, is lost in some deep well. Most are firm in the conviction that their problems come from a basic, incorrigible flaw in their personalities.

For anyone who doubts this, he quotes John, a fifty-one-year-old unemployed single man, exactly as written and with his permission. John says he had jobs and did his best and could never be good enough. People ask whether he is listening or seems bored.

John also writes about school. His report cards always had something like "doesn't pay Attention in class, doesn't sit still." He was stuck in front of the class or at the back, sent to the principal's office, where he was strapped, tied down in a chair and sent out to the hall. His father was always telling him to sit still and called him a lazy bum.

##His father always rubbed his nose in it: he should have been a doctor or a lawyer, or he wouldn't amount to anything.##

John said the same to Maté in person. Maté's reading is that the shame left behind weighs more than the attention difficulty itself. Shame, he writes, is a physiological and emotional state that comes from feeling cut off and alone. Failures can reinforce it but cannot cause it, and over time it winds itself into a person's identity.

He also says that "lazy" explains nothing. The so-called lazy person can be a whirlwind of energy when a task arouses interest. Arguing with shame by logic does not work, in his view. It weakens when you stop feeding it, that is, when you stop doing things that make the person feel more isolated or deficient.

These are Maté's interpretations, and the book uses John's page as one patient's example.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask where the sentence you tell yourself came from.** A verdict heard again and again in childhood can turn into the sentence a person says to themselves years later. Someone who always says "I'm hopeless at maths" can recall which teacher said it first and then try one problem anyway. That lets you start to tell apart a sentence that is your own observation from one that someone else left behind. You do not have to argue with it at once, and knowing that it has an author is already something you can use the next time it comes up.$$

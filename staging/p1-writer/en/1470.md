In one writer's picture, ideas live in a house. Each door you open shows new rooms, each with doors of its own, and the house grows the more you explore it. One inventor drew plans for a room that nobody could reach for about a century.

[[story]]
Charles Babbage conceived a programmable computer in the 1830s, and Johnson picks him as a case where the idea outran the world. With the knowledge and materials of his day, the machine would have been entirely mechanical and very slow. About a century later, accumulated knowledge had widened what was possible, and modern computers were built on his basic blueprints.

Steven Johnson uses the story in *Where Good Ideas Come From* to explain a term he borrows from the biologist Stuart Kauffman: the adjacent possible. It is the set of ideas within reach, given what is already known and available.

Johnson pictures it as a house. Open a door and you see new rooms, and those rooms have doors of their own. So the boundaries of the adjacent possible grow as you explore them. Babbage's computer sat in a room too far from the front door to reach. Ideas that come before their time often fail for this reason: they skip the rooms in between.

A second example runs the other way. In the mid-1700s, Von Kleist and Cunaeus of Leyden developed the same electrical device, the Leyden jar, within about a year of each other. In Johnson's reading, the same doors had opened for both men at the same time, and neither needed to be a lone genius.

In his TED talk Johnson traces the programmable machine back further still. The flute leads to the organ, and the organ leads to the typewriter keyboard. The organ also leads to the music box, which becomes programmable once its cylinder can be swapped for another.

##An idea that arrives early is usually missing parts.## When the parts show up, the same idea can appear in several hands at once.

[[lessons]]
$$**Look one step past what you already have.** You rarely need to start from nothing, because the next step from your current tools and skills is usually within reach. A carpenter who adds a new material to the joint she already knows well has most of what she needs for her next piece. That lets you pick the nearest next step today instead of waiting for a big leap, and each step you take shows you a little more of the one after it.$$

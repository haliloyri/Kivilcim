Csikszentmihalyi counts five steps in the creative process, and the one everybody remembers sits in the exact middle. Two steps come before the flash and two come after. The longest of them all is the last one.

[[story]]
Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi built *Creativity* (1996) on in-depth interviews with about 91 exceptional people from many fields. When he describes how their work unfolds, he says the process normally runs through five steps.

First comes preparation. The person becomes immersed in a set of problematic issues, the kind that will not let go. Next is incubation: ideas churn around below the threshold of consciousness while the person is, on the surface, doing something else.

Third is insight, the moment when the pieces of the puzzle fall together. That is the step people retell. In his list it sits in the middle, with two steps before it and two after.

##After the flash comes evaluation, and then elaboration, which he calls the longest and hardest part of the whole process.## Evaluation means deciding whether the insight is worth pursuing at all. Elaboration is what follows a yes, the long stretch where the idea turns into something that exists.

He warns that the steps are more recursive than linear. A person in evaluation may go back to preparation, and a new insight can arrive in the middle of elaboration.

He sets this process inside a larger picture. Creativity, he says, arises from three parts: the domain, a body of symbolic rules and procedures such as mathematics or music; the field, the group of experts who decide whether a new idea enters the domain; and the individual, who uses the domain's symbols to make something new. All three are needed.

One caution belongs with incubation. Accounts of it come from creators looking back, sometimes years later, and a 2016 review calls their reliability questionable. The label itself came from Graham Wallas, who drew on Poincare's 1910 analysis of mathematical creation. Poincare did not use the word, and his own report concerned events from nearly thirty years earlier.

[[lessons]]
$$**Count the work after the spark as part of the idea.** A home cook invents a soup in one flash and then spends weeks adjusting the salt and the cooking time before anyone would call the recipe finished. If an idea of yours stalls after its first spark, it may simply be in elaboration, the longest step. Asking which of the five steps you are in tells you the next small job, because judging a spark and building it out are different kinds of work. Nobody calls the cook a failure for that; the pot simply still needs work.$$

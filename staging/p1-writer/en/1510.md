[[open]]
Marriage, children, a house, a car, a job: people work for each one and then stop noticing it. One Stoic remedy asks you to picture the loss on purpose, and then to put the picture away again.

[[story]]
People get what they want, William Irvine writes in *A Guide to the Good Life* (2008), and then they take it for granted. They marry, have children, buy a house, find a job, and each of these becomes ordinary. Once it does, they form grander desires. He calls this hedonic adaptation.

The Stoic answer he highlights is negative visualization, which he calls the single most valuable technique in the Stoics' psychological tool kit. You imagine losing something you value, a spouse, a car, a job. The Stoics thought that picture makes you value the thing more. Irvine puts it as learning to want the things you already have.

The examples are small and physical. Epictetus advised quietly reminding yourself, when you part from a friend, that this might be your final parting. Saying grace works the same way: you pause to consider that the food might not have been available. Epictetus pushed the exercise further. Kiss your child goodnight, he advised, and picture that child's death, so that you learn to appreciate the child's existence. Irvine describes the whole practice as contemplating the impermanence of the world around us, and a related habit is to treat each visit, each meal and each meeting as possibly the last.

Seneca supplies the attitude. Be the user, he says, but not the slave, of the gifts of Fortune. Your possessions, in his picture, are on loan from Fortune.

The point is to appreciate small things, not to turn gloomy, and the practice has a limit built in. The Stoics did it periodically, a few times a day or week, and not constantly. That spacing kept them from turning into pessimists. Irvine's own stated aim is tranquility, rather than virtue as an end in itself.

[[lessons]]
$$**Picture a loss briefly, then put it away.** Someone who has worked in the same bakery for years may no longer notice the warm kitchen before dawn, or the colleague who always arrives with a joke. If, once or twice a week, they pause to picture the place closed, the smell of bread and the voices in it become noticeable again. Then they put the thought down and carry on, which keeps it a reminder rather than a worry, and lets them enjoy the shift that follows.$$

The same question can be put to yourself in two ways: "What am I going to do?" or "What should Ethan do?" Psychologist Ethan Kross and his colleagues asked whether that tiny difference actually changes the noise in a person's head just before a stressful moment.

[[story]]
Ethan Kross is a psychologist who studies the voice inside our heads. In *Chatter* he gives that name to the negative talk that loops in our minds when we are under stress. Kross and his colleagues observed people who were about to face a stressful moment, such as speaking in front of others or going into a tense conversation.

While preparing, some participants silently asked themselves, "What am I going to do?" Others used their own name or "you," so they put the question as "What should Ethan do?" The difference was almost too small to notice, but the language changed something.

The people who used their own name looked at the problem from the outside, as if it belonged to someone else. That psychological distance eased the grip of the thought. They reported less overwhelming thinking and judged their own performance in a more balanced way.

Kross calls this distanced self-talk. One word does not solve a problem, he says, but it can put enough space between you and the thought that you are able to decide. The book connects the idea to an observation known as Solomon's paradox: we handle other people's problems more wisely than our own.

The trap Kross describes is less the thought itself than its looping. Before a stressful evening the same question plays in the head over and over, growing a little with each pass. Using your own name means entering that loop with a different voice: the same question, asked as if of someone else.

The sentence stays the same length and the problem stays the same. The only thing that changes is whose problem it seems to be. For the participants in Kross's research, that meant staying with the problem while looking at it from a small distance.

##Using your own name instead of "I" can add a step of distance between you and the voice in your head.## It does not make the problem disappear. It only lets you look at what to do about it from a calmer place.

[[lessons]]
$$**Speak to yourself about your own problems the way you would to a friend.** Someone heading into a difficult conversation tomorrow can ask silently, using their own name, what Anna should do first. That small change brings the voice of an adviser in place of a voice full of worry. You are then looking at the thought instead of being swallowed by it, which leaves room to decide what you will actually do and replaces the late-night replay with one concrete step.$$

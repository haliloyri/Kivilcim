Green and yellow flames climbed six and seven stories above a research campus, and fire engines from eight towns could not stop them. The man who owned the place walked through hundreds of onlookers looking for his son. He wanted him to fetch his mother and her friends.

[[story]]
At sixty-seven, Thomas Edison went home early from the laboratory one evening. Shortly after dinner a man rushed in with news that a fire had broken out at his research and production campus, a few miles away. Ryan Holiday tells what followed in *The Obstacle Is the Way*, where he had already shown the same man at work. In 1878 Edison tested six thousand filaments for his light bulb, one of them made from the beard hair of one of his men, before bamboo finally worked.

The strange chemicals stored in the buildings fed the blaze, and the engines could not contain it. Edison made his way through the crowd and the devastated employees, calm but quick, until he found his son. "Go get your mother and all her friends," he said, with childlike excitement. "They'll never see a fire like this again." The son must have stared. Edison reassured him: "It's all right. We've just got rid of a lot of rubbish."

There was a good deal more than rubbish in those buildings. Years of priceless records, prototypes and research turned to ash. The structures were made of what was supposedly fireproof concrete and insured for only a fraction of their worth, so Edison and his investors were covered for about a third of the damage. He still was not heartbroken, not as he could have been. The next day he told a reporter he was not too old to make a fresh start, and that he had been through a lot of things like this: it keeps a man from being afflicted with ennui.

Within about three weeks the factory was partly running again. Within a month the men were working two shifts a day, turning out products the world had never seen. The loss came to almost a million dollars, more than twenty-three million in today's money, yet that same year Edison would bring in nearly ten million in revenue. ##He took a spectacular disaster and answered it just as spectacularly.##

Holiday asks what else Edison should have done. Weep, rage, quit and go home? None of it would have accomplished anything, so he did not waste the time. To do great things, Holiday adds, a person has to be able to endure tragedy and setbacks and find some joy in whatever happens.

[[lessons]]
$$**After a loss, give the first job to the next step.** On the night of the fire Edison did not dwell on what was gone. By the next day he was talking about a fresh start, and in three weeks the factory was partly running. A shopkeeper whose store has just flooded can begin the first morning by listing which shelves could open tomorrow, instead of adding up the damage for hours. That keeps your energy on what you can still change, without pretending the loss was small, and the tally can wait until you are calmer.$$

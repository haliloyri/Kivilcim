[[open]]
A volunteer is working through word puzzles when the researcher interrupts with resumes to evaluate. The puzzles are put away and the task changes completely. A few minutes later a quick test shows that the puzzles have not gone anywhere.

[[story]]
The researcher Sophie Leroy published a 2009 paper titled "Why Is It So Hard to Do My Work?" Cal Newport brings her findings into *Deep Work* (2016), where they explain why a quick switch between tasks costs more than the switch itself.

Leroy set up a simple chain in the lab. Participants solved word puzzles. They were interrupted to evaluate resumes. Then they took a quick lexical decision test, a fast reading task designed to measure how much of the first activity was still in their heads.

People experiencing what she named attention residue performed worse on the task they had moved to. ##When you switch from one task to another, your attention does not follow at once; part of it stays behind on the first task.## The more residue a person carried, the worse the performance on the next job.

Leroy also found that the residue is thickest when the work on the first task was unbounded and low in intensity. A task with no clear stopping point leaves the most behind, which is the usual state of a half-written message or a report you plan to get back to.

This is why Newport argues for working in uninterrupted stretches on one task and finishing it before you move on. If you hop between tasks all day, each one gets done with a share of your mind somewhere else. Someone who glances at the inbox every few minutes never works with a clean head, however short each glance seems.

Notice how little the experiment asks of anyone. The volunteers only moved from one thing to the next, and the leftover was enough to show up in the measurement.

That makes the cost hard to see from the inside. A switch feels instant, and nothing announces the part of your attention that stayed behind. The only trace is a task that goes slightly worse than it should have, and rarely does anyone trace it back to the interruption. Her paper's title asks why work feels so hard after a switch, and residue is her answer.

[[lessons]]
$$**Close a task before you leave it.** A cook who walks out of the kitchen with a half-finished sauce is still thinking about the pan while she greets the guests. If she writes one line on the board about where the sauce stands and what comes next, her mind can let go of it. You can do the same with any open task before a call or a message: name the stopping point, and the next job gets a clearer head. A line like that takes half a minute and spares the second task from carrying the first one around.$$

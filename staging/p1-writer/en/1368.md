A games company that had struck gold with free downloads decided it should own the machines its games ran on. It spent $100 million building data centers of its own. Those buildings were supposed to carry the next stage of growth, and the company was certain the players would keep coming.

[[story]]
The company was Zynga, and Paul Jarvis uses it in *Company of One* (2019) as a warning. After the data centers went up, the industry shifted and Zynga struggled to keep pace. It laid off many employees. Then it closed the data centers it had just paid for.

His second case is a shoe startup from Australia. Shoes of Prey let customers design their own shoes on a website, and sales were good enough to tempt it into the physical world. It opened stores inside shopping malls around the globe, took venture capital, moved to the United States and hired heavily. Store sales came in below projections. In 2018, after mass layoffs, the company collapsed.

Jarvis puts a figure behind the stories. The Startup Genome Project found that 74 percent of the high-growth tech startups it studied failed because they scaled too fast. The common factor was speed.

He describes the mechanism plainly. Rapid growth usually needs outside money, and outside money comes with an expectation of returns. Over time an investor's agenda can pull against the company's purpose and against the promises it made to customers. Spending on revenue you have only projected is, in his words, a gamble. The mall stores of Shoes of Prey were built on exactly that kind of projection.

The book adds a blunt point about forecasts. Missing a projection is, statistically, the more likely outcome. Yet most growth plans are written as if the forecast will hold, and the lease, the payroll and the loan are signed on that assumption. They fall due every month whether the sales arrive or not.

Jarvis does not ban growth. He asks two questions before each step: is this growth truly needed, and if it is, can it be done with the same resources or fewer? Even when both answers are yes, the step is sized to the cash already in the till.

Against this he sets the company of one, which protects four traits: resilience, autonomy, speed and simplicity. A business with low rent, a short payroll and little debt can ride out a bad season without pulling the shutters down. When it grows, it pays for each step with money it has already earned.

##Jarvis goes as far as calling growth, when it becomes the primary focus, an entirely harmful strategy.## He means it as a provocation for founders who assume bigger is always safer. The servers Zynga built and the shops Shoes of Prey opened were bought to make those companies stronger, and both ended up as costs to be shut down.

[[lessons]]
$$**Price the bad month before you grow.** Every step of growth brings fixed costs that still need paying when sales disappoint, and those costs are easy to ignore while the plan looks bright. A café owner thinking about a second branch can work out, before signing anything, whether the first café could cover the new rent if the second one sold far less than hoped. Doing that sum puts the downside on the same page as the hope. It also leaves room to wait a season, which is often cheaper than closing a branch you opened too early.$$

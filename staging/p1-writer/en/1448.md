He had shed blood by land and sea, and plots were forming against him in the city itself. In a letter he wrote about the thing he had long prayed for: the time when he could finally rest. Seneca picks him as the clearest case of a man still waiting.

[[story]]
Seneca's case, in *On the Shortness of Life*, begins with Augustus, who never ceased to pray for rest for himself and to seek release from the affairs of state. In a letter he spoke of his deep desire for that time, which he had long prayed for. The most powerful man in Rome was spending his days in expectation of days that were still to come.

Ordinary people make the same plan on a smaller scale. Seneca quotes the sentence they rehearse: "After my fiftieth year I'll retire to a life of leisure; my sixtieth year will bring release from all my duties." Then he answers: "How late it is to begin living just when life must come to an end!" Even Cicero, he notes, wrote that he was "lingering in my Tusculan estate, half-free." The wise man, Seneca says, will never be half-free and will enjoy complete, unalloyed liberty.

The people Seneca addresses live as if they were going to live forever. He gives the habit a name and puts it in one line: "The greatest impediment to living is expectancy, which relies on tomorrow and wastes today." You are arranging what lies in Fortune's control, he tells them, and abandoning what lies in yours. The whole future lies in uncertainty, so live immediately.

Then he comes to the friend he is writing to. Paulinus manages what Seneca calls the revenues of the world, including the public grain supply, and Seneca urges him to withdraw "to a more peaceful haven" and take up calmer, safer, more important pursuits. Offices like his, he says, are bought at the cost of life. He also warns that it is harder to obtain retirement from oneself than from the law. People who spend their lives this way, he adds, should be buried by the light of torches and wax tapers, as if they had lived briefly.

Once the plan has a slot, it stops being a hope and becomes something she either keeps or deliberately moves.

[[open]]
A man got stuck in the doorway of a post office in a European city. The door was not locked, and he was not a fool.

[[story]]
Don Norman tells this in the first chapter of *The Design of Everyday Things*, as something that happened to a friend. The friend was trapped in the doorway of a post office in a European city. Secondary accounts describe a row of glass swinging doors with an identical row behind it, and nothing to show which side to push. Norman's conclusion, and the book's, is that the fault lay with the doors.

The book makes this practical with two signals. A flat plate says push. A long vertical handle says pull. Norman borrowed the word affordance for this idea from the psychologist J. J. Gibson. Colleagues went on to call any door that confuses a Norman door.

The idea does not stop at doors. In the book's stove example, the burners sit two by two while the controls sit in a line of four. A user who turns the wrong one is not blamed. Norman blames the mapping between controls and burners. Teaching people to get it right does not prevent the mistake, but redesigning the stove does.

The same stance runs through Norman's account of accidents. He has said that after the 1979 accident at Three Mile Island he was called in, with other psychologists, to help retrain the plant's operators. The book's preface argues that most accidents are blamed on human error, yet in almost every case the error was the direct result of poor design.

The book first appeared as *The Psychology of Everyday Things*. According to one blogger, bookshops and libraries shelved it with psychology textbooks, which is why a later edition took the word design. The point it keeps returning to is that when a mistake keeps happening, the place to look is the setup, and not only the people.

[[lessons]]
$$**When the same mistake keeps happening, look at the setup first.** The post office doors and the stove controls both say that what needs changing is not the people who err. If everyone in an apartment block drops rubbish into the wrong bin, the colours and positions of the bins can be changed before another warning sign goes up. Once you stop blaming people, it becomes easier to see where the error is born, and the fix often turns out to be smaller than another notice.$$

Before a standardized test, a class of Danish schoolchildren got twenty to thirty minutes to eat, play and chat. Nothing about the test changed. The break did something to the scores that the time of day had been doing in the opposite direction.

[[story]]
Daniel H. Pink tells this in *When: The Scientific Secrets of Perfect Timing*. In Danish public schools, students scored worse on standardized tests the later in the day they sat them. Yet when they had a twenty- to thirty-minute break beforehand, scores went up. Pink stresses the size of it. The improvement from the break was larger than the hourly decline, so with a break after every hour, scores would actually rise over the day. He likens the boost to students getting about three extra weeks in the classroom.

The afternoon trough matters more in an operating room. Pink describes a study of roughly 90,000 surgeries at Duke's hospital that counted anesthesia adverse events, meaning errors, harm or both. Problems were significantly more frequent for cases starting in the 3 and 4 p.m. hours. At 9 a.m. the rate was about 1 percent; at 4 p.m. it was 4.2 percent.

For actual harm to patients the rate went from 0.3 percent at 8 a.m. to 1 percent at 3 p.m. Most of these events were not serious. The researchers still concluded that afternoon circadian lows impair physicians' vigilance, affecting complex tasks like anesthesia care. End-of-day fatigue and afternoon hand-offs between staff were part of the picture. Pink adds research from the University of Pennsylvania showing that hand-washing in hospitals deteriorates considerably in the afternoon, and describes hospitals that have added short vigilance time-outs to the shift.

##Pink's favorite fix starts with a cup of coffee.## He calls it the nappuccino. You drink the coffee, then lie down right away for a short nap. Because caffeine takes about twenty-five minutes to enter the bloodstream, it kicks in just as you wake.

Length matters more than people think. The ideal nap is ten to twenty minutes, and anything longer brings sleep inertia, the groggy fog after deeper sleep. Five-minute naps did little to reduce fatigue or sharpen thinking, while ten-minute naps had effects lasting nearly three hours. Pink also offers rules for the break itself: moving beats sitting still, being with someone beats being alone, and outside beats inside.

[[lessons]]
$$**Put a short, real break right before careful work in the afternoon.** Gritting your teeth through the trough tends to add mistakes, while a genuine twenty-minute pause can often make up more than the dip was costing you in the first place. An accountant who prepares reports after lunch can take a quick walk around the block with a colleague before checking the figures, instead of grinding straight into the spreadsheet. That way the task that needs your closest attention does not land in the minutes when your attention is at its weakest point of the day.$$

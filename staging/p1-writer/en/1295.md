On one page, Tim Ferriss writes out the thing he is afraid to do. Then he fills the rest of the page with things most people never write down. By the time he stops, the worst case does not look like the only number on the sheet.

[[story]]
Ferriss calls the exercise fear-setting, and he describes it in *The 4-Hour Workweek*. You pick the thing you are afraid to do, and instead of turning it over in your head, you work through it on paper in three steps.

First comes the nightmare. You write down the worst that could realistically happen if you did it, in full, with the details you would normally avoid looking at. It sits there in your own handwriting where it can be examined.

Then comes the repair. Beside the worst case, you write what you could do to fix the damage or get back on your feet, even if only for a while. Ferriss's observation is that this part is easier than people expect, because a mind running on fear skips it entirely.

The third step is the one that surprises people. ##You write down what it costs to do nothing.## You count it in emotional, physical and financial terms, and you stretch it over time: where will you be in a year, in five years, in ten, if nothing changes?

Fear, in this exercise, finally gets an opponent. The nightmare is vivid and arrives with details. Doing nothing is quiet, so it usually never makes it onto a page at all.

Ferriss draws two broader points from this. What we fear doing most, he says, is usually what we most need to do. And people tend to choose unhappiness over uncertainty, putting up with a known bad situation because the unknown one has not been written out yet.

He is not asking you to ignore risk. The page is built so that the risk of acting and the risk of staying put sit side by side, and you can read them as a pair.

[[lessons]]
$$**Count what waiting costs, next to what acting costs.** A tenant who has put off a hard conversation about a broken heater can list what each cold month costs in sleep, in electricity bills and in mood. Beside that list the awkward conversation, which seemed enormous, takes up a smaller place. Seeing both sides together lets you decide with both numbers in front of you, instead of only the one that fear keeps repeating, and you can pick a first small step such as a short note to the landlord.$$

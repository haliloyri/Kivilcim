The tidying consultant arrives, the client opens the door, and for a while nothing is touched. She walks to the middle of the house, kneels on the floor and greets the home in silence. The cupboards stay shut and the piles stay where they are until the greeting is finished.

[[story]]
Marie Kondo describes this habit in *The Life-Changing Magic of Tidying Up*, and it comes from a specific past. Before she became a tidying consultant, she spent five years as a miko, a shrine maiden at a Shinto shrine. "I began this custom quite naturally based on the etiquette of worshipping at Shinto shrines," she writes of greeting houses.

##The tense expectancy when a client opens the door, she writes, "resembles the atmosphere when one passes under a shrine gate."## So she greets the house first. Everything else waits. She calls it her way of showing respect for the house and its contents. Kneeling formally in the centre of the home, she introduces herself in her mind, and only then gets up and begins.

The attitude is old. It goes back to her teens. "I began to treat my belongings as if they were alive when I was a high school student," she writes. At home she greets her own house when she walks in, and accounts of the book describe her thanking her handbag for its work when she sets it down each evening.

Her method is often described as partly inspired by Shinto, which holds that things can carry a spirit, a kami. Kondo does not spell that link out at every step. But her habit of treating objects as things that have worked for her rests on the same ground.

The idea has a practical side too. "Just like the gentle shake we use to wake someone up, we can stimulate our belongings by physically moving them," she writes. That is why, in her method, nothing is judged while it sits on a shelf. Cupboards are opened, and even things forgotten at the back of a drawer are taken out and handled one at a time.

The greeting, the thanks and the gentle shake belong to one stance. Kondo relates to her belongings less like an owner surveying property and more like someone acknowledging things that have served her. Her tidying begins before the first object is lifted, with a quiet word to the house.

[[lessons]]
$$**Treat the things that serve you with some care.** Seeing an object as something that has worked for you all day changes how you set it down and where you put it. Someone who usually drops a bag by the front door can start emptying it onto the table each evening and putting it back in its place. Within days she notices what she has been carrying around for weeks without using. A small habit of respect leaves less clutter between you and your belongings, and a little more attention to what you actually need.$$

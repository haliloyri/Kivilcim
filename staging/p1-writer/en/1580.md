Alec Baldwin's character in Glengarry Glen Ross made "Always Be Closing" the motto of a whole era of selling. Daniel Pink thinks that era is ending, and the clearest reason sits on a car lot, at a Chevy dealer's.

[[story]]
Pink traces the old model back to "Always Be Closing", the formula the film made famous: keep the pressure on until the customer signs. It worked best when the customer knew little. Two decades earlier, a Chevy dealer knew far more about a Malibu than the person standing beside it, and the information sat on the dealer's side of the desk.

That has reversed. A buyer today can arrive having read enthusiast sites, and Pink says such a person can arguably know more about cars than the dealer does. He calls the shift one from information asymmetry to information parity, or something close to it. ##Deception mostly worked because buyers lacked information or expertise.##

With reviews, ratings and price comparisons a click away, sellers have more reason to be fair. Pink sets the old Latin warning beside a new one. *Caveat emptor*, buyer beware, is still good advice, but *caveat venditor*, seller beware, is now just as relevant. Lying and cheating have not disappeared, only lost some of their easiest ground.

What replaces pressure? Pink offers new ABCs: attunement, which is understanding the other person's point of view, buoyancy, which is staying afloat through a tide of rejection, and clarity.

Clarity is the one that fits a world of equal information. It means helping people see their situation in a new way and notice problems they had not spotted, including cases where the buyer has misjudged the problem. A seller who only repeats what the reviews say adds nothing. One who sees what the buyer missed is still worth the visit.

Pink's examples of the new style lean on questions instead of pressure. In 1980 Ronald Reagan asked voters whether they were better off than they were four years earlier, and Pink says a question like that gets people to persuade themselves. Mitt Romney's campaign tried the same line in 2012, and it backfired, because voters measured their lives against 2008 instead.

[[lessons]]
$$**When the other person can look up the facts, your value is the problem they have not noticed.** A pharmacist whose customers have already searched their symptoms is most useful when she notices the question they did not think to ask. Passing on information is easy now, because everyone can find it, and a customer can tell when you are reading a script. Before your next conversation, think about what the person across from you is likely to be missing, and start there instead of with a summary.$$

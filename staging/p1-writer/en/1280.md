"Nice and polite." More than half the senior leaders in one study used those words, and they weren't praising their companies. They were naming the cover story their organizations used for the one thing they ranked as the biggest obstacle to courage at work.

[[story]]
The study belongs to Brené Brown, who describes it in *Dare to Lead* (2018). Her team interviewed 150 C-level leaders from around the world and asked them what, if anything, about the way people lead would have to change for organizations to succeed in a complex, fast-moving world full of problems that seem impossible to solve.

It was a long question. What came back was a single line: "We need braver leaders and more courageous cultures."

So the next question was what gets in the way. The leaders named ten behaviors and cultural issues that block courage inside organizations. Each item came out of what they saw in their own companies. At the top, ranked as their greatest concern, was this: "We avoid tough conversations, including giving honest, productive feedback."

When the leaders explained why people avoid them, the reasons split three ways. Some said it came down to a lack of courage. Others said a lack of skills: people simply don't know how to have these conversations well. And more than half described a cultural norm of being "nice and polite" that was used as the excuse for not having them at all.

There is an odd shape to these findings. The same executives who asked for braver cultures located the biggest barrier not in the market or in their competitors but in their own meeting rooms, in the conversations they and their people keep postponing. The book's subtitle carries the same weight: *Brave Work. Tough Conversations. Whole Hearts.*

The first of the skills Brown teaches is built for exactly this. She calls it rumbling with vulnerability: staying inside a hard conversation, generous and curious, instead of leaving it or never starting it. The goal isn't to make the conversation harsher. The goal is to stop avoiding it.

Her shortest line in the book answers the "nice and polite" norm directly. ##"Clear is kind. Unclear is unkind."## Feedback that never gets said doesn't protect anyone. The person keeps working without knowing what is expected, and the conversation slides into next week, and then into the week after that.

[[lessons]]
$$**Choose a clear sentence over vague politeness.** Smoothing over a problem feels considerate in the moment, and it spares you an awkward few minutes, but it leaves the other person guessing about what is actually wrong. A tenant who smiles at a noisy upstairs neighbor for months may end up in a far worse argument once the resentment boils over, when naming the hour and the noise once, calmly, could have settled it. Boiling what you need to say down to one specific sentence keeps the conversation short and gives the other person a real chance to fix things.$$

Ten or twelve minutes. That is how long one time-management author says it takes to plan a whole day, and he wants those minutes spent at the end of the workday, not at your desk the next morning. The sheet of paper he asks for decides more than most people expect.

[[story]]
Brian Tracy opens *Eat That Frog!* with a saying he says has been told for many years: if the first thing you do each morning is eat a live frog, you can go through the day knowing the worst of it is behind you. His frog is the biggest, most important task on your plate, the one you are most likely to put off. It is also the one that can do the most for your results. That task is the frog.

Two more lines follow. With two frogs, eat the ugliest one first. And if you have to eat a live frog, it doesn't pay to sit and look at it for very long. Tracy's point is plain: start.

##Tracy has you prepare that start the evening before, not in the morning.## In chapter two he asks for tomorrow's list at the end of the workday. You carry over everything unfinished and add everything the next day will need. All you need is a sheet of paper and a pen.

He claims the list keeps working while you sleep. Your subconscious turns it over through the night, and you may wake with ideas for doing the job faster. If something new turns up during the day, it goes on the list before you act on it.

Tracy puts a number on the effort: ten or twelve minutes to plan the day, he says, saves at least two hours of wasted time. It is his own estimate, and he cites no study for it. Ticking off each finished item, he adds, gives you a visible picture of progress, which helps you get moving.

By morning the choosing is done. The paper is on the desk, the frog is somewhere on the list, and nothing is left to sit and look at.

[[lessons]]
$$**Name the hard task the evening before.** A decision left for the morning has to compete with the first call and the first message, and the easiest job tends to win. A baker could write on the closing-time note that the difficult special order comes first tomorrow, so she walks in already knowing where her hands go before the oven is even warm and the first customer asks for something else. When the choice has been made the night before, the morning asks for action only, and you spend no energy bargaining with yourself while the day is still waking up.$$

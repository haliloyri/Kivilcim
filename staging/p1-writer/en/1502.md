Two feelings can use the same body: a tight chest, a quick pulse, a mind that will not settle. One of them tells you something is happening right now. The other can run for weeks about something that may never happen.

[[story]]
Gavin de Becker, in *The Gift of Fear* (1997), separates the two with care. The first is true fear. He describes it as a brief, urgent biological response to a real threat. It arrives fast, it demands attention, and it comes with a push to act.

The second is worry, or anxiety. De Becker's description is blunt: it is something we manufacture. Worry is voluntary and has no tie to actual danger. A person can start it, feed it and keep it going alone, with nothing in the room that threatens them.

In the body the two can feel alike. De Becker wants the reader to keep the signal and drop the manufactured part.

##He urges you to heed true fear and warns against inventing reasons to be afraid.##

His reasoning for the second half is practical. Constant worry costs a person stress, and it also distracts from real threats. A mind full of imagined risks has little attention left for the single thing that is actually happening.

The distinction rests on two details in his definitions. True fear is tied to a real threat, so it has an object in the present: something is happening, here, to you. Worry is manufactured, so it has an author, and the author is the person doing the worrying. One arrives from outside and asks for a response. The other is produced from inside and can be started again whenever the mind returns to it, which is what de Becker means when he calls it voluntary.

Tempo is the clearest difference between them. True fear comes in seconds and asks for a move. Worry can replay the same scene at midnight while you stare at the ceiling and again over breakfast, and it asks for nothing you can do. The distinction between signal and worry is easy to state and harder to apply on a bad night.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask what is happening right now.** A shopkeeper lying awake at night is rehearsing how the next delivery might go wrong, and every version feels urgent. If he asks whether anything threatening is happening at that moment, the answer is usually no, and he can write the problem on a list for the morning. When the answer is yes, the same question tells him to act at once, and it spares him from carrying imagined problems into the shop. Over a few weeks he learns which of his fears were signals and which were stories.$$

After the first year, one account holds $10,800 and the other $10,550. Nobody would lose sleep over a gap of $250. John Bogle built a whole argument on it, and followed the two accounts for fifty years, until the gap was a different size altogether.

[[story]]
In *The Little Book of Common Sense Investing* (2007), Bogle takes $10,000 and fifty years and runs the money down two paths. On the first it sits in the stock market and pays no costs. On the second it goes into a fund that returns 5.5 percent a year after costs.

At the end of year one the balances stand at about $10,800 and $10,550. That is roughly 2 percent of the value gone, a difference most people would shrug at.

Year ten tells a different story. The first account is near $21,600 and the second near $17,100, so 21 percent is already missing. By year thirty it is about $100,600 against $49,800. Half of what the market built has been taken out along the way.

At fifty years the figures read about $469,000 and about $145,400. The shortfall is $323,600, and costs have swallowed nearly 70 percent of what the market would have accumulated.

Bogle sets a real stretch of history beside the exhibit. In the 1980s and 1990s stocks averaged 17.5 percent a year, and the average fund returned 15. A gap of 2.5 points a year looks small on a page. Fifty years show what it weighs.

Bogle puts it in terms of who carried what. The investor supplied 100 percent of the capital and bore 100 percent of the risk, yet earned only 31 percent of the market's return. The rest went to the cost of owning the fund.

His own sentence for it is short. "The miracle of compounding returns is overwhelmed by the tyranny of compounding costs." Where returns are concerned, time is your friend. Where costs are concerned, time is your enemy.

The arithmetic behind it never changes: gross return minus costs equals net return. A cost that looks tiny in any one year is a cost on every year that follows.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask what a cost takes each year, then picture it over decades.** A café owner who accepts a delivery app's cut on every order barely notices it on a single sale, yet across a year it can grow into one of the biggest lines in the books. Small recurring costs behave like returns running in the wrong direction, because each year's loss stops working for you too. Before you sign up for anything that charges a little on a schedule, write down the yearly amount and how many years you expect to keep it.$$

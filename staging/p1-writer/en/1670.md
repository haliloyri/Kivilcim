A baby is howling and what it needs is a pair of arms. Thich Nhat Hanh says anger is that baby, living inside each of us. Most of us were never taught to be its mother.

[[story]]
In *Anger* (2001) the Vietnamese monk Thich Nhat Hanh describes anger as a howling baby, suffering and crying. The baby needs its mother to embrace it. That is all the baby needs, and in his picture anger needs the same thing from us.

One passage in the book pictures a mother who strikes her crying baby. She cannot see that she and the child are one. We do something similar when we fight our own anger: we treat it as a stranger to be silenced or scolded, forgetting that it is part of us. This is why he says each of us is the mother of our own anger.

What does a good mother do? According to summaries of the book, she picks the baby up, talks to it quietly and tries to find what is making it cry. She does not shout over the crying or try to force it quiet. Her arms come first, and her curiosity follows.

Those arms are mindful breathing. As soon as anger rises, he advises, begin to breathe with awareness, which gives you the caring energy of a mother. He adds that simply embracing your anger and breathing in and out is enough.

Baby and mother live in the same person, so the arms that hold the anger have to be our own. There is no one to hand the baby to, and the one who is angry is also the one who picks it up.

Not speaking or acting in the heat of it belongs to the same approach. Shouting at a baby to calm it only makes the crying louder, and the logic of his image runs the other way: you hold the baby to soothe it, not to cut off the noise.

He also reads anger as a message that something needs taking care of. A baby cries to announce a need, and so looking after anger does not mean losing to it. It means finding out what it is crying for.

[[lessons]]
$$**Do not shout at your anger, open your arms to it.** After a sharp exchange at a morning meeting, someone can spend the evening scolding themselves for losing their temper again. Or they can put a hand on their chest, take a few breaths and admit that the anger is still there. The second way leaves a calm gap in which to look at what the anger was crying about, and it makes you a little less harsh with yourself. The next morning you can walk into the same room in a softer tone, and you have not pushed the anger away to get there.$$

Arianna Huffington used to go to bed in her workout clothes. Now she keeps pajamas, nightdresses, even T-shirts that are only ever worn for sleep. It is one small piece of an evening routine she describes as "a sacrosanct ritual", and it starts with a phone being shown out of the room.

[[story]]
For two years, while she was building The Huffington Post, Arianna Huffington worked regular 18-hour days. She says she didn't understand the importance of sleep until 2007, when she collapsed from exhaustion in her home office and broke her cheekbone on the corner of her desk. In *The Sleep Revolution* she describes the nights she built afterwards.

Her top recommendation is the simplest one: get every device out of the bedroom. She does it at a set time each night. She switches them off and, in her words, "gently escorts" them out of her bedroom. ##Because the time is fixed, the decision doesn't have to be made again every evening.##

Then comes the bath. Before bed she takes a hot bath with Epsom salts, with a candle flickering nearby. It is slow on purpose, the opposite of the pace of the long day she has just finished at her desk.

Clothes are part of it too. The workout gear she once slept in is gone from the bedroom. In its place are clothes kept only for sleep, so that changing into them means one thing. Over time the body learns that this particular T-shirt belongs to the end of the day.

Some nights, if she wants something warm and comforting, she has a cup of chamomile or lavender tea. It's an option, not a rule.

The routine sits inside a larger argument. The book urges seven to eight hours a night as a priority, and it warns against leaning on sleeping pills such as Ambien to get them. Huffington's route to those hours is not a pill but a sequence of small, physical steps that slow the evening down one at a time.

Behind all of it is the belief the book sets out to dismantle: that overwork and burnout are simply the price of success, and that going without sleep is a badge of honour. Her evening says the reverse. Sleep isn't what's left over after the day is done. It is time she protects, and the phone leaving the room is the first visible sign of that.

[[lessons]]
$$**Tell your body the day is over with signals it can learn.** Willpower is weakest late at night, but a few small, physical steps repeated in the same order can teach the body that sleep is next. A nurse who gets home late from a shift and immediately plugs her phone in the kitchen, then changes into clothes she only sleeps in, may notice within a couple of weeks that those two moves alone start to slow her down. A routine like this saves you from renegotiating your bedtime with yourself every single night when you are tired.$$

So few men from Waltham fought the next day that some historians decided the town must have been pro-British. It was not. The people there simply had not been told the British were coming until it was too late.

[[story]]
Malcolm Gladwell tells the story in *The Tipping Point* as a comparison of two riders. Paul Revere set out north and west of Boston, and William Dawes, a tanner, took the towns to the west. Both carried the same urgent message, that the British were coming, through just as many towns over just as many miles.

Revere's ride set the countryside afire. Dawes's did not. The local militia leaders were not alerted, and Waltham, one of the main towns on his route, heard too late.

Gladwell puts the difference in the men. Revere was gregarious: a fisherman and hunter, a cardplayer and theatre-lover, a regular at the pubs and a successful businessman, a member of the Masonic lodge and of several social clubs. The historian David Hackett Fischer credits him with an uncanny genius for being at the centre of events. In each town he knew whom to wake, the militia leader and the key people, and he had mostly met them before.

##Dawes was probably an ordinary man who, once he left his hometown, would not have known whose door to knock on.##

Fischer writes that Dawes did not awaken the town fathers or militia commanders in Roxbury, Brookline, Watertown or Waltham.

Gladwell gives such people three names: Connectors, Mavens and Salesmen. Maven comes from Yiddish and means one who accumulates knowledge. Revere was a Maven too: in autumn 1774 he set up a secret group that met at the Green Dragon Tavern to monitor British troop movements. In Gladwell's summary, Mavens are data banks, Connectors are social glue, and Salesmen have the skill to persuade people who are unconvinced of what they hear.

The account rests on Fischer's history, and calling Revere a Connector is Gladwell's own interpretation.

[[lessons]]
$$**Decide who will carry your announcement before you make it.** How far a message travels depends on which doors its carrier knows how to knock on. A volunteer promoting a neighbourhood fun run can send the details to the one person who runs the local chat groups rather than posting in ten places and hoping. That way you choose who carries the news instead of leaving it to chance, and you can check that the carrier knows whom to tell in each street.$$

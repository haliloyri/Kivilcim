[[open]]
A software method takes its name from a rugby formation, and the idea behind the name came from an old paper about cars, photocopiers and printers, not from software at all.

[[story]]
*Scrum* (2014), by Jeff Sutherland with his son J.J. Sutherland, traces the method back to an article in the Harvard Business Review. In 1986 Hirotaka Takeuchi and Ikujiro Nonaka published "The New New Product Development Game," built on case studies of firms in the automotive, photocopier and printer industries. They called what they saw a rugby approach: one cross-functional team that tries to go the distance as a unit, passing the ball back and forth.

The word for that formation became the name of the method. In the early 1990s Jeff Sutherland, John Scumniotales and Jeff McKenna developed a similar approach at Easel Corporation and called it scrum. Elsewhere, Ken Schwaber was using what would become scrum at his own company, Advanced Development Methods.

Sutherland's earlier career was in the air. In 1967 he was deployed to Udorn, a Royal Thai Air Force base, flying RF-4C Phantom reconnaissance missions that included missions in the Vietnam War. He later earned a master's degree in statistics at Stanford and a doctorate in biometrics at the University of Colorado School of Medicine.

##Sutherland and Schwaber merged their two versions into one framework and presented it in a research paper at OOPSLA '95.## The rhythm they described is built from sprints. A sprint normally lasts between one week and one month, and two weeks is the most common length. It ends with working increments shown to the people who asked for them, and a retrospective picks one or two improvements for the next round.

The book's case for why this matters is a hard one. A summary of it says the FBI's Virtual Case File project spent about $170 million over five years and delivered nothing usable. The restarted effort, run with Scrum, delivered working pieces every few weeks.

[[lessons]]
$$**Pass the work along with the whole team, not down a line.** In a kitchen where one person writes the menu, another buys the food and a third cooks it, the first dish that fails shows up only at the stove. If all three plan together each morning, the problem with the fish shows up while the menu can still change. Whoever has to carry a piece of work forward can sit in on its planning from the start.$$

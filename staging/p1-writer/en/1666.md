In Thich Nhat Hanh's walking practice, you take three steps with each in-breath and three with each out-breath. If your lungs ask for four steps, you give them four. The one thing the walk is not for is getting somewhere.

[[story]]
Thich Nhat Hanh, in *Peace Is Every Step* (1991), describes walking meditation as walking only to walk and never to arrive. You go slowly, alone or with friends, and if you can, somewhere beautiful. Kids are welcome, and you may hold a child's hand.

He starts from an ordinary observation. We walk all day, but our walking is usually closer to running, and he says that kind of walking prints anxiety and sorrow on the Earth. The alternative is to print only peace and serenity, and any child can do it.

The method is built on breath. Outside, a little slower than your usual pace, you take three steps with each in-breath and three with each out-breath, saying "in, in, in, out, out, out" to yourself. Naming the breath makes it more real, he says, the way saying a friend's name does.

Your lungs set the count. If they want four steps, give them four, and if they want two, give two, because the in-breath and the out-breath do not have to match. ##If you feel happy, peaceful and joyful while you walk, he says, you are practicing correctly.##

Then the feet. He asks you to notice where they meet the ground and to walk as if you were kissing the Earth with them. If something beautiful catches your eye, a tree, a flower, children playing, you may stop and look, still following your breath so thoughts do not take over, and when you want to go on you simply start again.

Behind all this sits a Buddhist word he translates as wishlessness or aimlessness. You do not set something in front of yourself and run after it, because everything is already here. So in walking meditation you do not try to arrive anywhere, and he warns that if you keep thinking about what you want to realize, you lose your steps. There is nothing to run after.

He puts it next to washing dishes, which is at once a means and an end: you wash to get clean plates and also just to wash. Walking works the same way. Only when you let go of the past and the future, he writes, does every step make a flower bloom under your feet.

[[lessons]]
$$**Let one walk be the whole activity.** A teacher who crosses the school yard each morning planning the day's problems has treated the walk as dead time before the first lesson, and has heard none of it, not the traffic, not the gate, not the first greetings. If you give a single stretch of your day to the steps alone, you notice the weather, your pace and what your shoulders are doing before the rest of the day gets its hands on you. That small stretch of attention is yours to spend, and it costs nothing but the decision to go nowhere in particular.$$

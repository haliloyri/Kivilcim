[[story]]
It happened on the last day of his sophomore year of high school. A baseball bat hit James Clear in the face, breaking his nose and shattering the bones around his eyes. In *Atomic Habits* he describes a long recovery, and it was about a year before he was back on a baseball field.

A young athlete who has been away that long usually wants his old level back as fast as possible. Clear had no leap available. His body and his routines had to be rebuilt from somewhere close to the beginning.

Instead of a grand comeback plan, he turned to small things he could repeat every day: a steadier bedtime, showing up to practice, studying at set hours. None of them would have turned heads on its own, but each could be done on a good day or a bad one. Choosing steps that small meant building a routine that would not fall apart if a day went wrong.

At Denison University he kept walking the same road. He did not keep reminding himself of big goals; he kept his routines and did the same small things day after day. The repetitions piled up slowly, and the comeback came from hundreds of ordinary days stacked on top of each other, not from one strong decision.

By his final year Clear was recognised as the university's top male athlete and as an Academic All-American. The teenager who had spent a year away from the field a few years earlier had become one of its most recognised names.

He says the experience taught him to trust small systems, the kind that keep running on a bad day. One person's story does not promise the same result to everyone. The book itself opens with this accident rather than with a theory.

[[lessons]]
$$**Shrink a habit until a bad day cannot stop it.** A shopkeeper coming back from an illness might skip the old pace and aim only to open on time and prepare the first order of the day. Even on a tired evening he can do that much, and doing it makes the next day easier to start. You can restart a habit you dropped the same way, by choosing the version you could still manage on your worst day and watching what actually gets repeated. The old pace can come later, after the small version has become routine.$$

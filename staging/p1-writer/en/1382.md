When exams came around, some Harvard students shut their doors and cut out friends, family and lunch breaks to study harder. It looked like the sensible move. Shawn Achor collected data on what actually predicted how those students were doing, and the answer pointed the other way.

[[story]]
Shawn Achor spent years working with students at Harvard, and in *The Happiness Advantage* (2010) he describes a pattern he kept seeing there. As pressure rose, many students turned inward. They pulled away from friends, family and the people who supported them, at exactly the moment they needed them most.

One moment in his account captures the pressure. Soon after arriving, students face a harsh piece of arithmetic: half of them are now below average. For people used to being the best, it is a hard fall. The low mood that follows pulls them inward, back to the room, the desk and the pile of notes.

Achor studied the well-being of about 1,600 Harvard undergraduates and measured a long list of factors: grades, family income, SAT scores, age, gender, race. ##Social support turned out to be a far greater predictor of happiness than any of the others.## The correlation between social support and happiness was 0.7. For scale, Achor notes that most psychology findings count as significant at around 0.3.

He was not alone in finding it. In a study titled "Very Happy People," researchers looked for what set the happiest 10 percent apart from everyone else. One characteristic stood out, and only one: the strength of their social relationships.

Achor also turns to one of the longest-running studies in psychology. The Harvard Men Study followed 268 men from the time they entered college in the late 1930s. In 2009 George Vaillant, who directed it for decades, told The Atlantic he could sum up its findings in a single word: love.

This part of the book borrows the language of investing. Under stress, Achor writes, the most successful people hold tighter to their social support. Instead of divesting, they invest. The stressed students in their rooms were doing the reverse, selling off the one asset his own numbers rated highest, one cancelled dinner at a time.

There is a limit worth keeping in view. A survey of 1,600 students shows a strong link; on its own it does not prove which way the cause runs. Achor's reading is that support feeds happiness and resilience, and he builds the rest of the chapter on that interpretation.

[[lessons]]
$$**When pressure rises, shorten a standing plan instead of cancelling it.** An architect facing a deadline may treat the weekly dinner with friends as the first thing to cut, because it seems like the one item nobody will miss. That hour is one of the few connections helping them carry the hard week. Once you stop seeing people as the easiest line to delete from a busy calendar, you keep hold of the support you will need most when the stress peaks. A shorter coffee still counts as showing up, and it keeps the habit of meeting alive for the weeks after the deadline.$$

About ten years in monasteries, and then a method sized at just ten minutes a day. The man who made that trade did not pick an hour or a long morning. His book's whole unit is one short sitting a day.

[[story]]
Andy Puddicombe left his sports science studies in 1994 and went to Asia. Accounts of his life say a bereavement played a part. By the same accounts, he trained for about ten years as a Buddhist monk, in Nepal, India, Myanmar, Thailand and Russia among other places, and received full ordination in the Tibetan tradition at a monastery in the Himalayas. He came back to the UK in 2004. The author bio of his book calls him a former Buddhist monk with a degree in Circus Arts.

What he brought back was built on a very different scale. *The Headspace Guide to Meditation* teaches one exercise called Take10: ten minutes, once a day. The book first appeared in Britain as *Get Some Headspace*. Its publisher's blurb promises that you can quiet the mind and feel less stressed and less tired in just ten minutes a day.

The exercise itself is plain. It opens with a brief check-in on your body, the sounds around you and your mood. Then you sit undisturbed with an upright back and resting hands, take a few deep breaths and let your breathing settle into its own rhythm. When the mind wanders, you notice it without irritation and go back to the breath. If the mind is loud, you can count breaths from one to ten and back.

##Readers who quote the book pick out one line: we are still only talking about ten minutes.## Another line they quote says these ten minutes will set up your entire day. Both circulate in reader-submitted collections, so the exact wording may differ.

The method became a company as well. According to biographical sources, Puddicombe and Richard Pierson started Headspace in London in 2010, at first as an events company, and by January 2012 a first app was out.

The ten minutes are not meant to end when the sitting does. The book describes them as a training ground for the rest of the waking day and carries the same attention into eating, walking, running and falling asleep.

[[lessons]]
$$**Size the practice to the day you actually have.** Someone who wants to learn an instrument and waits for a free weekend often never starts, while the person who keeps a short, fixed slot after dinner every evening does end up playing. When the length of a practice fits a real gap in your life, keeping it stops being a test of willpower and becomes an ordinary part of the day. You are left with a plan that is not perfect but can begin tonight, and a missed day does not cancel the next one.$$

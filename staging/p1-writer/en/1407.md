A list that was perfect on Monday can be useless by Friday. Nothing on it has to be wrong for that to happen: the world just moves while the paper stays still. David Allen builds a whole stage of his method around that gap, and he gives it three words.

[[story]]
In *Getting Things Done* (2001), Allen describes a workflow in five stages: collect, process, organize, review and do. You gather everything that has your attention, decide what each item means and what to do about it, and put the result on the right list. That much gets everything out of your head and into place. The fourth, review, is the one that keeps the other four from rotting, and its most important form is the weekly review.

A system that skips it slowly turns into a pile of lists that no longer describe your life.

He asks for a regular look at all your lists, preferably once a week. The review has three phases, named Get Clear, Get Current and Get Creative. Each has a job.

Get Clear is the clean-up. You collect the loose papers and materials lying around, empty your inbox, and empty your head of whatever has crept back into it. ##By the end of it, nothing is waiting in a corner without a place.##

Get Current is the check. You go through your next-action lists, your calendar for the weeks just passed and the weeks coming up, the list of things you are waiting for others to do, and your project list. This is where the lists catch up with a week that did not go as planned. Items that are done come off, items that changed get rewritten, and a promise that nobody remembered gets noticed.

Get Creative is the last and the loosest. You look over the someday and maybe items, the ideas you parked because there was no room for them yet, and ask which one is ready now. After the clean-up and the check, there is space to think about something other than catching up.

The result is a system you can look at without suspicion. Without the review, a list is only as trustworthy as your memory of when you last touched it.

[[lessons]]
$$**A list you trust has to be refreshed on a schedule.** A shared grocery list on the fridge works for a few days. After a week of nobody crossing things off or adding new ones, everyone starts buying from memory and the list is ignored. A short, regular look that removes what is done and adds what is missing keeps it usable. Your own to-do list needs the same appointment on a fixed day, so you never have to decide when to look. Keeping it current that way costs far less than rebuilding it after you have stopped believing it.$$

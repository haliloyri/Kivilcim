A professor had worked in the same UCLA building for twenty-five years. Asked where the nearest fire extinguisher was, he couldn't say. When he went to look, he found it right beside his office door, inches from the doorknob he had turned every working day of those twenty-five years.

[[story]]
That professor appears in *Make It Stick* (2014) by Peter C. Brown, Henry L. Roediger III and Mark A. McDaniel. He was part of a study in which faculty and students in UCLA's Psychology Building were asked to identify the extinguisher closest to their office, and most of them failed. Years of seeing something had not taught them where it was.

The authors put a student next to him. A first-year comes to a professor's office in distress after getting a D on his first introductory psychology test. He can't see how it happened. He went to every lecture and took diligent notes. He read the textbook and highlighted the important passages. To prepare, he highlighted his notes too, then went over the highlighted notes and text several times, until all of it felt familiar.

The professor asks some questions. Did he use the key concepts at the back of each chapter to test himself? Could he take a term like "conditioned stimulus," define it and use it in a paragraph? Had he turned the main points into questions and tried to answer them, or put the ideas in his own words? ##The answer was no in every case.## He saw himself as a model student, and he did not know how to study.

He is in large company. Surveys of college students show that highlighting, underlining and poring over notes are by far the most used study strategies. In studies at Washington University and the University of New Mexico, 148 students read passages from textbooks and *Scientific American*, some once and some twice in a row. Under those conditions the second reading brought no benefit at all. Rereading once after a real gap in time can help; rereading straight away mostly costs time.

The method survives because of how it feels. As a text grows familiar, reading it gets smooth, and that fluency passes for mastery. Being able to repeat the phrases in your notes says nothing about whether you grasp what they mean, how to apply them, or how they connect to what you already know.

The authors' remedy is retrieval: closing the book and making yourself recall. It builds stronger memory than rereading, and it works as a reality check. Students who never quiz themselves tend to overestimate how much they know, and they find out in the exam room.

[[lessons]]
$$**Treat familiarity as a feeling, and test it with the book closed.** Going over material again and again makes it familiar, and familiarity is easily mistaken for knowing it. Someone starting a new job can easily read the procedures manual several times and feel ready, then discover the gaps the moment they try to write the steps on a blank sheet. Testing yourself this way moves that discovery from the exam or the client meeting to a quiet moment when you can still fix it, and it shows you exactly which part to study next.$$

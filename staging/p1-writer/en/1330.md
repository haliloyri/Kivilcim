Someone quits a job to escape a demanding boss and starts a company. A year later the boss is gone and a table of investors has taken his place. Mark Manson tells this kind of story to make a blunt point: problems don't go away, and the only real choice you get is which ones you keep.

[[story]]
The chapter of *The Subtle Art of Not Giving a F*ck* where this appears is called "Happiness Is a Problem." Mark Manson's argument is that problems never disappear. They get traded in, or upgraded. Solve one and you have usually bought the next, often a bigger and more interesting one.

That sets up the question he puts at the center of a section titled "Choose Your Struggle": "What pain do you want in your life? What are you willing to struggle for?"

It sounds like an odd thing to ask. Most of us describe what we want in terms of results: a great job, a strong body, a good relationship. Manson's view is that nearly everyone wants those things, so wanting them tells you very little about how your life will turn out. ##The question that decides it, he writes, is "What pain do you want to sustain?"##

He uses himself as the example. For years Manson fantasized about being a rock star, about the stage and everything that comes with it. The fantasy never turned into anything. Looking back, he saw why: he wanted the result and not the process that leads to it. He loved the image of winning and had no appetite for the long, uncertain work in between.

Then he describes the people for whom the same life does work. Those who actually enjoy the stress and uncertainty of the starving artist's existence, he notes, are the ones who end up living that life and making it. For them the struggle is the part they chose, and they would choose it again tomorrow.

One line from the book carries the whole idea: "Everything worthwhile in life is won through surmounting the associated negative experience." Someone who wants a strong body has also chosen the gym on tired evenings. Someone who wants to write a book has chosen the blank page on the mornings when nothing comes.

So Manson's question doesn't ask you to picture the finish line. It asks about an ordinary Tuesday six months in, when the work is dull and nothing has paid off yet, and whether you would still want to be the person doing it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Pick goals by the struggle you're willing to live with.** It is easy to love the final picture of a goal, but what decides whether you get there is the repetitive, uncomfortable part you will meet every week on the way. A person who dreams of opening a café usually pictures the full tables, when the real job includes 5 a.m. deliveries, broken machines and a spreadsheet every night. Asking which part of the daily grind you would actually enjoy lets you test a dream honestly before you give it several years of your life.$$

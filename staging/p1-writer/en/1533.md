[[open]]
In one large study of sales calls, sellers who announced early on what their product could do tended to meet objections. The better sellers held those announcements back until the buyer had said, in their own words, what they needed.

[[story]]
Neil Rackham's team examined 35,000 sales calls over roughly twelve years before *SPIN Selling* (1988) appeared. One of the things the book sorts out is the difference between three things that sellers usually blur: features, advantages and benefits. A feature is a fact about the product. An advantage describes how the product can help. A benefit shows how it meets an explicit need the buyer has expressed.

The book also grades needs by strength. An implied need is a weak signal, such as a general concern the buyer mentions in passing. An explicit need is a specific want the buyer states. The seller's job, as summaries put it, is to develop implied needs into explicit ones, because a benefit can only answer the second kind.

The distinction matters because of what each one does to the buyer. According to accounts of the research, leading with features or advantages triggers objections that slow the sale. Summaries add that weaker sellers provoke objections by listing features, or advantages tied to price, before they understand what the buyer needs. The more they describe, the more there is for the buyer to push back against.

Skilled sellers prevent those objections instead of answering them. They develop the buyer's explicit needs first, and they address causes rather than symptoms. Only then does anything they say count as a benefit in the book's sense, because only then is there a need for it to meet. The book advises selling benefits, not features and advantages.

The same logic shows up in how many problems a seller raises. One source says that uncovering several problems is safer than fixating on a single one, since a single focus can invite objections about the weak areas of the offering. The size of the sale matters too: an opening benefit statement works better in a small sale than in a large one.

This is where Need-payoff questions earn their place. When the buyer states the benefit, it carries more weight than the seller's version and sounds less pushy. One summary puts the buyer's side plainly: people buy when the pain of the problem outweighs the cost of the solution. Inc. ranked the book first on its 2013 list of the best how-to-sell books.

[[lessons]]
$$**Let the need come before the pitch.** A benefit only exists once there is a need for it to meet, so describing what something does before you know what the person wants gives them nothing to say yes to. A colleague who recommends a new scheduling tool and opens with its features will probably hear that the team already has a system. Asking first which part of the week eats the most time lets the tool answer something the team has actually said. Once the need is on the table in the other person's words, what you describe can be judged against it, and the conversation stops being about your product.$$

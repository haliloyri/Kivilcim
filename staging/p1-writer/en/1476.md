Someone who wanted a new Ferrari and got a used Fiat feels deprived. Yuval Noah Harari uses that picture to explain, in his chapter on happiness, why happiness follows expectations more than conditions, and why a richer age is not automatically a happier one.

[[story]]
Chapter 19 of *Sapiens* (2011), "And They Lived Happily Ever After," asks whether people today are happier than people before. Researchers treat happiness as subjective well-being. They hand out questionnaires, ask people to rate statements about their lives on a scale of 0 to 10, and compare groups. Harari notes that history holds little research on the question, and suggests people today are not significantly happier than in earlier eras.

One example of the method: compare 1,000 people earning $100,000 with 1,000 earning $50,000. As the chapter is summarised, money raises happiness up to a point, and beyond it extra income changes little in the long run. Family and community count for more than money or health. Illness lowers happiness in the short term, and causes lasting distress mainly when it keeps worsening or brings constant, debilitating pain.

Expectations do much of the work. The one who wanted the Ferrari and got the Fiat feels short of something. Harari also points to lottery winners, who end up with about the same long-term happiness as people after a debilitating car accident. Conditions matter less than how they compare with what we expected.

Biochemistry adds a thermostat. The system tends to hold happiness near a set level, and that level differs from person to person. One person swings between 6 and 10 and settles at 8. Another swings between 3 and 7 and settles at 5. Those numbers are an illustration, not data. If this is right, history barely moves the dial: the French Revolution changed institutions, not French biochemistry.

Then Harari turns to meaning, and he disputes that happiness is a surplus of pleasant moments. A study by Daniel Kahneman found that raising children is full of unpleasant moments, yet parents call their children the chief source of their happiness. ##Happiness, Harari argues, comes from seeing your whole life as meaningful.##

He quotes Nietzsche: if you have a why to live, you can bear almost any how. Medieval people lived hard days, and belief in an afterlife may have made those lives feel meaningful. Harari offers this as a possibility, not a verdict. A life with meaning can satisfy despite hardship, and a life without it can feel miserable amid comfort.

[[lessons]]
$$**Name the expectation behind a complaint.** A new hire is annoyed that her desk sits by the door, until she notices she has been comparing it with a corner office nobody offered her. When you feel short of something, write down what you are measuring it against and where that yardstick came from. That separates the cases where the situation needs fixing from the cases where the yardstick does, and it is much easier to see on paper than in your head, and it keeps the conversation about the yardstick instead of about who is to blame.$$

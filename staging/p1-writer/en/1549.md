A company can hang a big target on the wall, and still get seven different answers when seven people in the same room are asked what they are doing this quarter. Gino Wickman's vision ladder is built to close that gap.

[[story]]
In *Traction: Get a Grip on Your Business*, Gino Wickman divides a company into six components: vision, people, data, issues, process and traction. Vision comes first on the list. He does not leave it as a slogan, because he breaks it into concrete pieces.

The first piece is core values: the company picks three to seven of them. The second is the core focus, which is the need the company exists to satisfy. After those comes a ladder that runs from far to near: a ten-year target, a three-year picture, a one-year plan, and at the bottom, quarterly goals. Wickman also wants a target market chosen, since a company that tries to please everyone ends up pleasing no one fully.

The two middle rungs are filled with numbers. The three-year picture and the one-year plan each carry a revenue and profit target and measurables to watch, and the one-year plan adds the goals for that year. A far-off sentence turns into something with a date and a figure attached.

One example from a book summary shows how much a core focus can decide. A company called Image One dropped its computer-networking division and returned to its real focus, which was simplifying clients' printing. Closing a division was how it answered the question of what the company was.

At the bottom of the ladder, Wickman calls the quarterly goals Rocks. The team comes up with ten to twenty goals at the start of a quarter, narrows them to about seven and gives each one to a single person. Each leader also carries one to three personal Rocks. Ninety days is treated as about as long as a person can stay focused on one project.

In this way a ten-year target can come down to something sitting on a particular desk this quarter. As long as the vision stays in the founder's head, the rest of the company has no way to climb down that ladder.

[[lessons]]
$$**Tie the far goal to one job for this quarter.** The owner of a small bakery who says she wants "the best bakery in town" has a sentence that takes her nowhere on Monday morning. If she turns it into one three-month job, such as sorting out the order list, and puts one name on it, the distant goal takes a real step. That moves the vision off the wall and into somebody's calendar, where it can actually be done.$$

Geoff ran a global microlending organization while still young, spent most of the year travelling and slept a few hours a night. Then his health gave way, and a famous study about violinists suggests his schedule had left out something the best performers protect.

[[story]]
In *Essentialism*, Greg McKeown describes Geoff as a man at the top of his career, the young chief executive of a global microlending organization. He was on the road for most of the year. He slept only a few hours a night and kept going.

Then his health broke down. For weeks he could barely function. The hours he had taken from sleep came back as weeks taken from his work.

From there McKeown turns to research that many people think they already know. K. Anders Ericsson studied violin students at a music academy in Berlin, whom their teachers had sorted into the best and the merely good. His work later became famous through Malcolm Gladwell as the "10,000 hour rule." The best violinists practiced more than the merely good ones. That is the half of the study everyone repeats.

##McKeown points to the second factor that separated the best from the rest, which was sleep.## The top players averaged 8.6 hours of sleep in every 24 hours, roughly an hour more than the average American. They also napped in the afternoon, about 2.8 hours a week. So the best players were not trading practice for rest. They did more of both, and the sleep sat right next to the practice in their days.

The researchers' reading, which McKeown adopts, is that rest allowed the best players to practice with greater concentration. They got more out of each hour they spent with the instrument. The number of hours mattered, and so did the attention they brought to them.

McKeown ties this to the main argument of his book. Doing less but better depends on choosing what matters, and in his view a tired mind chooses badly and finds it easier to say yes to everything. He writes that sleep enhances your ability to explore, to make connections and to do less but better throughout your waking hours.

One editorial note belongs here. Ericsson's group was small, and the finding is a correlation. It shows that the best players slept more. It does not show that sleep on its own made them the best.

[[lessons]]
$$**Count sleep as part of the work you want to do well.** A tired mind struggles to separate what matters from what merely shouts loudest, so it grabs the nearest request and decides in a hurry, often in ways it would not choose after a night of rest. A nurse coming off a run of night shifts can leave the decision about changing jobs until after two nights of proper sleep, and make it with a rested head. Thinking of sleep this way helps you see, before you trade away another hour of it, which decision you would be making tomorrow with a worn-out mind.$$

[[open]]
Faith and courage. Out of a list of more than a hundred values, those are the only two Brené Brown keeps for herself. She asks her readers to make the same cut, and most of them discover how many good words they have to cross out to get there.

[[story]]
In *Dare to Lead* (2018), Brown devotes a part of the book to what she calls living into our values. Her definition is plain: living into our values means we do more than profess them, we practice them. We walk our talk. The exercise that follows is built to make that possible.

It starts with a list of more than a hundred values: accountability, family, patience, fairness, joy, wisdom, and many more. Almost all of them sound like something a decent person should care about. Then comes the instruction. Narrow the list down to two core values.

Brown's reason is short. If everything is a priority, nothing is a priority. A long list of values looks admirable on a wall, but in a hard moment it doesn't tell you what to do. Two values can.

The cut is difficult, because most people recognize themselves in a lot of the list. Crossing out a word like fairness or family can feel like disowning it. Brown doesn't soften that part of the exercise. She shows her own result instead: faith and courage, chosen from the same long page and kept as the two things she leads from.

Choosing two words is only the beginning. For each value, Brown asks for three or four behaviors that show it in action, things another person could actually see you doing. Next to them goes a second column, which she calls slippery behaviors. These are the things you catch yourself doing when you drift out of that value.

That second column is what separates the exercise from a slogan. A value now has two edges on the page: what it looks like when you live it, and what it looks like when you're sliding away from it. ##A big word like courage only becomes useful once a few concrete behaviors are written underneath it.##

By the end of the exercise, the reader is left with two words and two short columns. A page that began with a hundred appealing words has turned into something much smaller and more practical: a record of which behaviors to repeat, and which ones to notice and stop.

[[lessons]]
$$**Define each value by the behavior it asks for and the behavior that betrays it.** A value only starts working when you can name what you do when you honor it and what you do when you slip. A father who says he values patience might write down that he snaps at his kids when they are slow to get in the car, and then recognize the moment the next time it arrives. Keeping both columns lets you see where a busy day pulled you off course and change one behavior without beating yourself up.$$

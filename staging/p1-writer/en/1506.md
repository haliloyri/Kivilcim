[[open]]
Two people are about to split a dessert on a table between them. One of them takes the knife and cuts, and the other picks a piece first. The cutter has every reason to make the halves equal.

[[story]]
Roger Fisher and William Ury give this rule in *Getting to Yes* (1981) as a fair way to divide a dessert. The one who cuts is not the one who chooses, so the cutter makes the pieces even in their own interest. Whoever holds the knife knows that any larger piece will go to the other person, so the blade may move a finger's width before it comes down. Nobody has to lean on anybody, and neither needs to trust anyone's good will, because both accepted the rule beforehand. The authors list other fair procedures too: taking turns, drawing lots, and letting a third person decide.

The larger principle in the book is to insist on objective criteria. These are standards independent of either side's will: market value, replacement cost, precedent, scientific judgment, professional standards, efficiency, reciprocity. The authors' phrase is to yield to principle, not to pressure. A standard of that kind sits outside both people at the table, the way the knife rule sat outside both people at the dessert.

The Law of the Sea Conference shows it at a larger scale. The United States and India disagreed over whether mining companies should pay a fee to start mining. Then both delegations looked at an economic model developed at MIT, a set of numbers neither government had written, and both reconsidered their positions.

##Neither side had to back down, because both were looking at a model that belonged to neither of them.##

The authors also suggest a quick test: would the other side accept these terms if the roles were reversed? It is the dessert rule turned into a question you can ask at any table. If the answer is no, what you are holding is an insistence dressed up as a standard.

[[lessons]]
$$**Agree on the yardstick before anyone names a number.** Two neighbours splitting the cost of a shared garden wall will argue less about who pays what once they have a price list from a local builder they can both read. A written figure is easier on pride than one person's claim, and both can check it. The builder's list belongs to neither neighbour, so neither is giving in to the other. That gives each side a way to say yes without losing face.$$

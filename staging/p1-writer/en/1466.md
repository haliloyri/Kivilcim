[[open]]
When Bryan Stevenson walked onto the TED stage, the first story he told was about his grandmother. Asked why, he had an answer ready. Everyone has a grandmother. His first statistic would not arrive for about five minutes.

[[story]]
Carmine Gallo takes the 2013 talk apart in pieces that accompany *Talk Like TED*. It was called "We need to talk about an injustice." Stevenson is a civil rights attorney who argues before the U.S. Supreme Court and won Miller v. Alabama, which bars mandatory life sentences without parole for juveniles convicted of murder. He built the eighteen-minute talk on three personal stories and opened with the one about his grandmother.

The stories carried a single theme. Many young people in underserved communities grow up without a sense of identity, Stevenson argued, and that leads them to make seriously bad decisions. Gallo says he made the case with people, not a chart, and that the first statistic came about five minutes in.

Gallo counted the talk by his own method and put the share of stories at about 65 percent. His accounts of the other third differ: in one it is data at about 10 percent, in another it is logic at 25 percent and credibility at 10. The 65 is the stable part, and it is his tally. He adds that the talk drew the longest standing ovation in TED history and that people in the hall donated a combined one million dollars to Stevenson's nonprofit, the Equal Justice Initiative.

His explanation goes through the brain. Gallo cites Princeton researchers who found that when one person tells another a story, the same regions of their brains are engaged. Researchers call it brain-to-brain coupling, and Gallo calls it a mind meld. He says neuroscientists who study persuasion reviewed his findings. A story also puts language, sensory, visual and motor areas to work together. Most workplace talks, he says, go the other way, leaning on data and giving stories little room.

[[lessons]]
$$**Show a person before you show a number.** Stevenson told three personal stories before his first statistic, and Gallo counts that order among the things that carried the talk. A nurse explaining a new shift pattern to her team could start with one patient's confusing night, then give the figure the pattern is meant to improve. The team hears the number as the answer to a problem they can picture instead of another line in a table.$$

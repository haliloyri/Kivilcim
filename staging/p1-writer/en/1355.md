"John, on your deathbed you will never wish you spent more time at the office." John's father said it to him once, and John started spending a little more time at home. Years later the man who had once been his boss arrived from the Himalayas with the same idea, and a small hourglass to go with it.

[[story]]
John is the narrator of Robin S. Sharma's *The Monk Who Sold His Ferrari* (1997), a fable about his former mentor Julian Mantle, a star trial lawyer who collapsed in court, sold everything and went looking for the Sages of Sivana in the Himalayas. When Julian comes back, he spends a night telling John what the sages taught him. One chapter is entirely about time.

It begins with a visit. About six months after Julian settled into the sages' forest retreat, Divea, the youngest of them, came to his hut of roses. She brought a gift from all the sages, wrapped in fragrant paper: a miniature hourglass made from blown glass and a small piece of sandalwood.

Each of the sages, she told him, had received one of these as a child. They owned nothing and lived simple lives, but they respected time and noted its passing. ##The little hourglasses were daily reminders of their mortality.##

Time also has a symbol in the fable Julian's teacher, Yogi Raman, gave him: a shiny gold stopwatch lying in a garden. Julian puts it plainly. Time slips through our hands like grains of sand, never to return, and rich or poor, everyone gets days of twenty-four hours.

Divea's advice was short. The best time to plant a tree was forty years ago; the second best time is today. From there Julian arrives at what he calls a "deathbed mentality": living each day as if it were your last, and asking yourself every morning, "What would I do today if it was my last?"

John's first reaction is that it sounds kind of morbid. Julian answers that it is a philosophy about life. Asked honestly, the question pulls you toward the meaningful things you have been putting off, and away from the petty things that drag your days into crisis and chaos. It sits next to another technique in the same chapter: having the courage to say no.

For John the idea was not entirely new. His father's line about the office had already moved him, a little, toward home.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask the deathbed question on an ordinary day to find what you keep postponing.** Thinking about the end can sound gloomy, yet it quickly sorts the day's plans into what matters and what merely fills time. An accountant who has honestly meant to sign up for a pottery class "one day" for years might ask the question on a plain Tuesday morning and book the very first lesson before lunch. Asked often, the question also shows you how quietly postponing becomes a habit, and which smaller task you need to turn down to make room.$$

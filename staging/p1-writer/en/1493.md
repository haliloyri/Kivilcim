About 13 percent. That is how many people, in a Gallup survey described in Johann Hari's work chapter, said they were engaged in what they do for a living. The rest were either not engaged or actively disengaged, and Hari goes looking for what separates the people at the bottom of the ladder.

[[story]]
In *Lost Connections* (2018), Johann Hari lists disconnection from meaningful work as one of nine causes of depression and anxiety. He starts with a number. Summaries of the chapter describe a Gallup survey from 2011 and 2012 across 142 countries, in which about 13 percent of people said they were engaged in their work, 63 percent were not engaged and 24 percent were actively disengaged.
Then he turns to a longer study. Michael Marmot began studying British civil servants, the Whitehall studies, in the 1970s. ##The lower an employee ranked, the higher their stress and their likelihood of a heart attack.## All of them worked for the same civil service, so the gap tracked where a person stood on the ladder and not which employer they had. The order of the ladder was visible in the health of the people standing on it.

Hari's reading is that the key factor is control. With no control over your work, you are far likelier to be stressed and, in his account, to be depressed. One summary says he argues that control predicts depression better than the workplace, the status or the pay. He names three harmful factors: feeling controlled, effort without reward and low status.

His example of the alternative is a bike repairman called Josh. Josh and his colleagues turned their workplace into a cooperative, so that the people who did the repairs also made the decisions. The account says it worked, without giving figures.

Keep the scopes apart. Gallup's survey measures engagement, and Marmot measured stress and heart attacks. The step to depression is Hari's argument, and critics say he selects his evidence. Taken as an argument, it points to one practical question about any job: who decides what.

[[lessons]]
$$**Look for one decision inside your work that you can make yourself, and widen it.** A hospital cleaner who covers a set list of wards each shift can ask whether she may choose the order. If the answer is yes, she now decides when to take each floor, and the day has a shape of her own. The change is small and the gain is hard to measure, but it lets you see exactly where your say begins and ends, and gives you a place to ask for more.$$

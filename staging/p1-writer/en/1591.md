The last ten years of some lives are spent watching from a chair, alive but unable to do much of what once gave pleasure. Peter Attia gives that stretch a name, the marginal decade, and asks what it would take to hand those years back.

[[story]]
Peter Attia, a physician, splits medicine into two eras in *Outlive* (2023). Medicine 2.0 mostly aims at lifespan and at staving off death. Medicine 3.0 puts prevention first, treats each patient as an individual, and begins with an honest look at risk, including the risk of doing nothing.

It also gives more weight to healthspan, the years lived well. Its toolkit has five parts: exercise, nutrition, sleep, emotional health, and outside molecules such as drugs, hormones and supplements. The marginal decade is where the gap shows. A person is alive at the end, but cannot do much of what they enjoy.

Attia asks readers to name what they would want to do in that last decade, then work backward and train for it now. His aim is to turn the marginal decade into a bonus decade.

His tool is the Centenarian Decathlon. You pick ten physical tasks you want to still manage late in life, then set today's fitness target backward from them. Items reported from the book include getting up from the floor with at most one arm for support, carrying groceries for several blocks, picking up a child, and climbing stairs. Some versions of the list also include lifting a suitcase into an overhead compartment, standing on one leg for thirty seconds and climbing four flights in three minutes.

The clock matters. VO2 max, the oxygen your body can put to work, falls roughly 10 percent a decade and faster after 50. That is why Attia pushes people to build reserve in their forties and fifties.

He argues you need to be quite fit in your fifties to manage picking up a grandchild, hiking, or carrying shopping upstairs in your nineties. A staircase that feels trivial at forty can become a goal at eighty-five.

[[lessons]]
$$**Set today's target by working backward from what you want to do later.** A baker who hopes to hand the shop to a nephew in ten years starts writing the recipes down now, because the handover day is too late to learn they live only in his head. The same backward look works for your body and your calendar. Once you know what you want to be doing later, this week's small effort has a purpose you can check, and you can tell whether it is pointing the right way.$$

"This little volume." That is how James Allen introduced his book, with no promise of a system or a method. Inside, almost at once, he plants a garden, and he makes a point about it that is easy to miss: nobody has to sow the weeds.

[[story]]
Allen published *As a Man Thinketh* in 1903 and called it, in the foreword, the result of meditation and experience, not an exhaustive treatise. Its aim was to show readers one truth: that people "themselves are makers of themselves," by the thoughts they choose and encourage. Mind, he wrote, is the master-weaver of both character and circumstance.

The main chapter opens with the garden. A person's mind, Allen says, may be likened to a garden, which may be intelligently cultivated or allowed to run wild. Either way it must, and will, bring forth something.

Then comes the sentence that carries the whole image: ##"If no useful seeds are put into it, then an abundance of useless weed-seeds will fall therein, and will continue to produce their kind."## Allen italicizes two verbs in that line. Useful seeds are *put*. Weed-seeds *fall*. One takes work, and the other arrives on its own and settles in without asking.

A gardener, Allen continues, keeps his plot free from weeds and grows the flowers and fruits he wants. A person can tend the mind the same way, weeding out wrong, useless and impure thoughts and cultivating right and useful ones. Whoever keeps at it sooner or later discovers that he is the "master-gardener" of his soul and the director of his life.

He follows the seed all the way through. Every thought-seed that is sown, or simply allowed to fall into the mind and take root, produces its own, blossoming sooner or later into action and bearing its own fruit. The harvest follows the seed. "Nothing can come from corn but corn, nothing from nettles but nettles," Allen writes, and he argues that people understand this law perfectly well in the natural world but rarely apply it to the mind.

Allen pushes the image further than many readers today would follow, claiming that a person's outward circumstances also grow out of thought. That is a moral claim from 1903, not a measured finding. The garden itself makes a smaller and more testable point: a mind left unattended is not empty, and if you never look at what gets in, you don't get to choose what grows.

[[lessons]]
$$**Treat the thoughts you repeat as seeds you have allowed to stay.** Even a thought you never chose takes root once you give it room day after day, and it starts to shape what you do. Someone who scrolls through bad news in bed and wakes up with the same worry every morning didn't plant that worry on purpose, but they did let it fall into the soil each night. Noticing which thoughts arrived by themselves makes it much easier to pick, deliberately, something you would rather put in their place before you fall asleep.$$

Two amateur chess players from the United States, using three computers at once, won a tournament in which any kind of help was allowed. They beat grandmasters and they beat entries that were computers alone. Garry Kasparov wrote about how.

[[story]]
*The Second Machine Age* (2014), by Erik Brynjolfsson and Andrew McAfee, splits technology history in two. In the first machine age, the Industrial Revolution, machines automated or augmented physical tasks. In the second, they do the same to cognitive ones. The authors' advice to people caught in between is to race with the machine, not against it, and to aim for machines that complement humans instead of replacing them.

Their examples of the cognitive side are plain. Software grades essays more objectively, consistently and quickly than humans do, and Forbes.com runs previews of corporate earnings that algorithms generate with no human involvement. Racing with a machine like that means asking what is left for the person who reads the essay, and what the person can do better once the grading is no longer theirs.

Chess shows what such a race can look like. The former world champion Garry Kasparov described, in a 2010 article, a 2005 freestyle tournament on Playchess.com, where humans could use any help they liked. The winners were two amateur Americans using three computers at once.

Kasparov's formula for the result: a weak human plus a machine plus a better process beat a strong computer alone, and they also beat a strong human plus a machine plus an inferior process. One report names the winning team ZackS, two amateurs from New Hampshire, who beat a 14-year-old Russian grandmaster in the final, 2.5 to 1.5.

Their edge was in how they directed the search. According to that report, their skill at manipulating and steering the computers offset the grandmasters' deeper understanding of chess. Strength mattered less than the way the work was divided. The computer chess expert Kenneth Regan has compiled data showing that a striking share of the most accurate games ever played came from human-computer teams.

The authors make the same point in general terms: people still have something to offer when they race with machines. It is a conditional point. One commentator warns that computers may soon be strong enough that adding a human gives no advantage, as already holds in checkers, and lists what humans add today: pruning variations, spotting where different programs disagree, and managing time.

[[lessons]]
$$**Give the machine the search and keep the judgment.** A clerk who sorts invoices by hand lets software do the matching and spends the saved time on the odd ones that do not fit, where she knows the customers. Racing with a tool means deciding which part of your job is search and which is judgment, and then handing over only the first part. You end up doing less of the work a program does better and more of the work that needs you, and you can check each week, calmly and on paper, whether the split still makes sense.$$

On a Tuesday and a Wednesday in July, a mechanical engineer calls in sick and quietly works from home. He uses a free trial of remote-access software to run the design program locked to his office computer, and he counts everything he finishes.

[[story]]
Tim Ferriss builds the "Disappearing Act" chapter of *The 4-Hour Workweek* around an example person he calls Sherwood. Sherwood is a mechanical engineer who sells French sailor shirts on the side and wants to escape constant supervision. After cutting out time-wasters he produces twice as many designs in half the time. He has already moved about 80 percent of contact with his boss to e-mail and most of the rest to phone.

His escape runs in five steps. First he asks the company to pay for a four-week industrial design class. The more the firm invests in him, the more it costs to lose him.

The sick days are step two, and the book's most debatable move. Working from home, Sherwood uses a free trial of GoToMyPC to pilot the CAD software licensed on his office desktop. He leaves an e-mail trail and keeps records. The result is three more designs per day than his average and three extra billable hours.

Step three is paperwork. Those numbers become a bullet-point page. He presents remote work as a business decision, crediting the missing commute and less office noise.

Then comes the knock. Around 3 p.m. on a relaxed Thursday he asks his boss, Bill, for two minutes. He proposes working from home on Mondays and Tuesdays for two weeks, as a trial Bill can veto whenever he likes. He asks for two days so he can fall back to one. "OK, fine," Bill says. "But it's just a test."

##Ferriss's phrase for the strategy is to ask for an inch and turn it into a foot without setting off panic alarms.## The remote days become Sherwood's most productive, and at the next review he asks for four days a week, ready to accept three. Bill worries he is about to quit and that everyone will want the same. Sherwood answers that only people who prove higher productivity should get it. Bill gives in: "Don't go blabbing about it."

The last step is about timing. Sherwood asks for a full-time remote trial while his team is in the middle of a project that needs his expertise. Ferriss notes that what you get often depends more on when you ask than how. The boss agrees, and that evening Sherwood buys a $524 round-trip ticket to Munich for Oktoberfest.

[[lessons]]
$$**Turn a big request into a small trial that is easy to reverse.** People say yes more easily when they are not being asked to commit forever, and a trial gives you a way to argue with results instead of promises. An assistant in a small shop who wants to run its social media account can offer to do it for a month and report back, rather than asking for a new job title on day one. A trial like this lets you take the next step only once you hold evidence from the first, and it makes stepping back painless for everyone if it does not work.$$

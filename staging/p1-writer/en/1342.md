Dan Harris had a firm view of meditation: it was the "exclusive province of bearded swamis, unwashed hippies, and fans of John Tesh music." He held that view right up until he fell apart on live national television, halfway through reading six news headlines.

[[story]]
In *10% Happier*, Dan Harris goes back to the morning of June 7, 2004. It was shortly after seven, and the ABC News correspondent was filling in on Good Morning America, reading the hourly news update to about five million viewers. There were six stories to get through. Halfway in, panic hit. His heart pounded, he was gasping for air, and he could barely get words out. He cut the segment short with "Back to you."

The explanation came later, in a psychiatrist's office. Harris had spent years running into war zones for work: Afghanistan, Israel, the Palestinian territories, Iraq. In 2003, back home, he became depressed and started self-medicating with cocaine and ecstasy, mostly on weekends.

##The psychiatrist told him the drugs had raised the level of adrenaline in his brain, making a panic attack far more likely.## He told Harris to stop immediately. Harris did. That removed the trigger, but it left the question of what to do with a mind that had been running hot for years.

The next push came from an odd direction. His boss and mentor, Peter Jennings, had assigned him to cover faith, even though Harris was a lifelong agnostic. The beat took him to interviews with spiritual self-help figures such as Eckhart Tolle and Deepak Chopra. Some of what they said landed. None of it came with a method he could actually use.

Meditation, meanwhile, was still on his list of things for swamis and hippies. What changed it was his wife. She had liked the books of the psychiatrist Mark Epstein, and through them Harris met Epstein, became friends with him and, still skeptical, started to meditate.

It helped, and faster than he expected. Harris describes it as becoming a way to steady himself as he moved through the world, and he later went as far as a ten-day silent retreat.

So the book's opening story has two halves. In the first, a correspondent who has been carrying war zones and weekend drugs breaks down while reading the news. In the second, the same man sits down to try the thing he had made fun of.

[[lessons]]
$$**Look for the cause of a crisis in how you were coping before it.** When something breaks, attention goes to the moment it broke, but the real weight is often a habit you have been carrying quietly for weeks or years. A teacher who numbs every evening with two glasses of wine may snap at a class one morning and blame only the pupil who was loudest that day. Looking at what you did to get through the hard stretch shows you the cost of the thing you thought was holding you up, while there is still time to swap it for something that actually does.$$

[[story]]
*Scrum* (2014) was written by a man trained to count. Jeff Sutherland earned a master's degree in statistics at Stanford and a doctorate in biometrics at the University of Colorado School of Medicine. The method he built with Ken Schwaber asks teams to plan from the work they have finished, not from the work they would like to have finished.

The unit is the sprint, a term he and Schwaber introduced in their 1995 paper. A sprint normally lasts between one week and one month, and most often two weeks. When it ends, there is something to count: the work the team actually completed. That count becomes the basis for the next plan. A team's capacity for a sprint can be estimated from what it completed in the last one, and the history of those counts, its velocity, serves as a guideline to what the team can take on.

Remaining work is kept in plain view. The team puts a burndown chart on public display, with days remaining along the horizontal axis and the amount of work remaining along the vertical axis. Anyone can see whether the line is falling as fast as the days are.

##The plan starts from the last sprint's finished work, not from a date someone hopes for.## Each sprint ends by showing working increments to stakeholders, and a retrospective picks one or two improvements for the next round. What is counted is something that works and has been seen, not something that is described as nearly done.

The book's hardest case is the FBI. According to a summary, the Virtual Case File project spent about $170 million over five years and delivered nothing usable. The restarted effort, run with Scrum, delivered working pieces every few weeks.

[[lessons]]
$$**Plan from what you finished last time.** A freelancer who checks how many invoices she really sent last week before promising a client a number can answer calmly, while a freelancer who goes by how busy she felt overpromises. The count works as a guideline and not as a guarantee, so a week with a family emergency simply shows up in the next estimate. Looking at the real numbers each cycle lets you make promises you can keep.$$

[[reflect]]
&&If you counted what you really finished in each of the last three weeks, what would you promise differently for next week?&&

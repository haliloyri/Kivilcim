Two groups of waiters took the same orders in the same way, with one difference. The first group answered every order with friendly words like "great" and "no problem". The second simply repeated the order back to the customer. When the tips were counted, the gap between the groups was large enough to build a negotiation technique on.

[[story]]
The study belongs to psychologist Richard Wiseman, and Chris Voss uses it in *Never Split the Difference* (2016) to explain a tool the FBI relied on in hostage calls. The waiters who repeated the order earned an average tip 70 percent higher than the ones who offered praise. Voss calls the technique mirroring. You repeat the last three words someone said, or the one to three words that matter most, usually with a curious, questioning tone.

He had used it against real criminals. In 1993, two men robbed a Chase Manhattan branch in Brooklyn and held hostages inside. When one of them complained on the phone that their driver had been chased away, Voss handed the sentence back as a question: "We chased your driver away?" The man started talking and kept going, pouring out information.

The book's most ordinary example takes place in an office. A boss known for his "drive-bys" stops at an employee's desk and asks for two paper copies of all the paperwork, one to keep in-house and one for the client. It means about a week of extra work. The employee answers calmly with two words of his own: "Two copies?"

The boss has to explain, and as the questions continue, it turns out the client never asked for a copy at all. That leaves the in-house set. The employee asks where to store it. "It's fine. You can store it anywhere," says the boss. The employee mirrors that too, and the boss, slightly irritated, says to put them in his office and also make two digital backups.

##The next morning an email arrives: "The two digital backups will be fine."## The paper copies, and the week they would have eaten, are gone.

Voss's explanation is simple. Hearing our own words come back makes us feel heard, so we elaborate, and the person mirroring gains time and information without arguing. He pairs it with a particular voice, the slow, calm tone of a late-night FM radio host. The employee in the office never pushed back on the order. He only asked questions in a low voice, using the boss's own words.

[[lessons]]
$$**Before you push back, hand the last words back as a question.** Repeating the final words of a vague request invites the other person to explain it, and it buys you a moment to think without starting a fight. A mechanic whose customer says the car is making a strange noise can simply ask, "A strange noise?", and the customer will start describing when it happens and where it seems to come from. You hear the real need before you commit to anything. Sometimes you also discover that the request no longer makes sense even to the person who made it.$$

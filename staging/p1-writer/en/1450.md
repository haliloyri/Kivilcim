Somewhere in your chest a heart is beating, and you are not doing anything to make it. The same goes for your breathing and for your lunch, which is being digested without a word from you. Joseph Murphy starts with that ordinary body and ends up somewhere bolder.

[[story]]
Joseph Murphy opens *The Power of Your Subconscious Mind*, published in 1963, with the body. The heart beats, the lungs fill and empty, food is digested, and nobody makes a decision about any of it. You do not set a reminder for your pulse. It all runs anyway, and that plain observation is where his argument begins.

Murphy says the mind works on two levels. On top sits the conscious mind, which reasons, weighs and chooses. Beneath it runs the subconscious, which handles the body's work without being asked. It also does not argue or judge. It takes in whatever is pressed into it. He treats this as a lopsided arrangement: the conscious mind questions everything, the subconscious questions nothing, so what the conscious mind hands over matters a great deal.

His picture for this is soil. The subconscious is the ground, thoughts are the seeds, and the ground gives back whatever was planted, whether it was good or bad. Nobody sows thistles and expects wheat. The division of labour is simple in his telling: the conscious mind picks the seed, and the subconscious grows it. Most of his exercises rest on that split, choosing something on purpose and handing it to the part of the mind that does the growing.

He also tells the story of the opera tenor Enrico Caruso, who suffered from stage fright, with sweating and spasms in his throat. According to Murphy, Caruso told his conscious "little me" to stop interfering and leave the singing to his subconscious "big me".

From all this Murphy concludes that thoughts held with conviction and repeated end up shaping a person's habits and circumstances. He was a minister, and the book reads like a sermon with practical exercises, prayer-like techniques and Bible quotations alongside the argument. He does not test the claim in any experiment. He asks the reader to try it and see.

[[lessons]]
$$**Notice the sentence you repeat to yourself.** Like seeds, thoughts tend to settle by repetition and not by decision, so the phrase you say most often is the one doing most of the planting. Someone who tells himself before every team meeting that he is not the type who speaks up may never raise a hand, and the silence then confirms the thought. Murphy's soil image is a prompt to catch that phrase, write it down once, count how often it shows up in a week, and choose calmly which one you would rather grow.$$

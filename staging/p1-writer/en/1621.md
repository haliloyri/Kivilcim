Researchers had a mother take her baby into an unfamiliar room, leave, wait, and come back, and then they watched what the baby did when the door opened. They saw three different things. The most surprising of them looked like nothing at all.

[[story]]
In *Attached*, Amir Levine and Rachel Heller begin with the psychologist Mary Ainsworth's Strange Situation. A mother brings her baby into an unfamiliar room, leaves, and returns, while observers note how the baby reacts. The babies' reactions to the open door were what the researchers recorded.

Some babies cried when she left and settled quickly when she came back, and then went on exploring the room. Being upset was part of it, and what mattered was how fast the baby recovered once she was there. These were the secure ones. Their mother was a safe base from which to explore.

Others cried hard at the departure and, on her return, clung to her and pushed her away at once. They were hard to settle, wanting her and resisting her in the same moment. These were the anxious ones, and they stayed on high alert even with her back in the room.

A third group barely reacted. They showed little when she left and little when she came back. ##Babies who barely reacted still showed stress in their heart rate.## That is what later infant studies found, and these were the avoidant ones.

Levine and Heller treat the styles as showing up early enough to shape how adults handle closeness. Their claim is that adults carry the same three styles into their relationships. Secure adults are comfortable with closeness. Anxious adults crave it and worry about losing it. Avoidant adults prize their independence and tend to pull back when a partner comes close, and to the outside they often look composed, much as the quiet babies did. Knowing which one fits you helps explain your habits with partners, and the styles can change over time.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask before you decide that someone is fine.** A coworker who says nothing when a plan changes can look unbothered, and it is easy to take the silence as agreement. He may in fact be tense and simply showing less of it. Asking him a friendly, direct question later lets you hear how he really took it, so you respond to the person and not only to what he shows, and whatever he says gives you a place to start. The question costs a minute, and it keeps a misreading from hardening into a story about him that nobody ever checked.$$

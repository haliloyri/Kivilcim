When Kathryn Petersen takes over as CEO of DecisionTech, the plan on paper is not the problem. Her executives are capable and the meetings are polite. Nothing moves, and the reason sits around the table, not on the slides.

[[story]]
Patrick Lencioni told his team theory as a story. *The Five Dysfunctions of a Team* is a leadership fable rather than a case study, so the company and its people are invented, and Kathryn Petersen is its CEO. The book follows her as she tries to get a stuck leadership team moving.

Lencioni's model puts five dysfunctions in a pyramid. At the bottom sits absence of trust. Above it come fear of conflict, lack of commitment, avoidance of accountability and, at the top, inattention to results. Each layer rests on the one beneath it.

Trust, in Lencioni's sense, means that team members are willing to be vulnerable with one another. They can admit a mistake, a weakness or a gap in what they know in front of the others. A team that cannot do that has nothing solid under the next layer.

##Without trust, the second layer shows up as polite, artificial harmony.##

People who are guarded with each other prefer a peaceful meeting to a passionate argument about ideas. Everyone nods and the objections stay in the corridor. Lencioni's healthy team debates hard in the room, and the debate depends on the openness underneath it.

From there the chain runs upward. Without real debate, nobody fully commits to a decision, because the unsaid doubts remain. Without commitment, people hesitate to hold each other to account, and a team that avoids accountability drifts away from the results it shares. Every dysfunction feeds on the one below it.

The fable makes the point by what it leaves out. DecisionTech's trouble is not a missing strategy but the way its executives deal with each other, and that is why Lencioni builds the model from trust upward.

[[lessons]]
$$**When a team stalls, check first whether people can admit mistakes to one another.** In a restaurant kitchen, a cook who is afraid to say the sauce has burned lets the wrong plate travel to the table, while a calm "I burned it" saves everyone a few minutes. Lencioni's pyramid works the same way, because every higher layer rests on that first openness. Before you rewrite the plan, you can look at how freely the people around it speak, and aim your effort at the right layer.$$

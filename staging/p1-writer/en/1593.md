A treadmill at the Cleveland Clinic put 122,007 patients through a fitness test and then waited, a median of 8.4 years. The group that scored lowest faced about five times the mortality risk of the group that scored highest.

[[story]]
Peter Attia builds his case in *Outlive* (2023) on data of this kind. The Cleveland Clinic study appeared in *JAMA Network Open* in 2018, and its authors found that the risk tied to low fitness was comparable to, or greater than, the risk tied to coronary disease, smoking and diabetes. It is observational, so it shows a link and does not prove that fitness itself prevents death.

Attia calls VO2 max, the oxygen your body can use, perhaps the single most powerful marker of longevity. He calls exercise the most powerful longevity drug.

His prescription has three parts. The first is zone 2: a pace where you can talk but would not want to chat. He suggests about three hours a week as a minimum, for example four sessions of 45 minutes. The second is VO2 max intervals: four minutes hard, four minutes easy, repeated four to six times, once or twice a week.

The third is strength. He prioritises grip, carrying loads and hip hinging. Low muscle strength is associated with roughly double the mortality. One benchmark for a 40-year-old man is a dead hang from a bar of about two minutes. Another test is stepping off an 18-inch block and taking a full three seconds to reach the floor.

He starts patients on the four-minute intervals only after about six months of endurance work.

The clock runs against you. VO2 max drops about 10 percent a decade, faster after 50. Attia's target is the elite range for your age and sex, roughly the top 2 percent, then the elite range of someone twenty years younger.

One more caution: fit people may differ from unfit ones in other ways, so the numbers cannot say fitness is the cause.

[[lessons]]
$$**Replace a feeling with a number you can test again.** A cafe owner who thinks weekdays are slow counts the receipts for a week and finds the slow hour is a different one from the one she guessed. You can do the same with your body: time the same flight of stairs today and again in a month. Then you compare yourself with yourself, and you choose what to work on from the record, not from a hunch.$$

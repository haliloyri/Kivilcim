[[story]]
Lori Gottlieb is a psychotherapist in Los Angeles. In *Maybe You Should Talk to Someone* (2019) she writes about the stretch of her life when she sat in the other chair as a patient, and sets it beside the stories of the people she treats.

Then her long-term boyfriend ended the relationship. He did not want to live with her young son, though he had known about the boy from the start. She had expected to marry him.

Friends urged her to see someone. She chose Wendell and went in thinking of a few sessions, enough to steady herself. In the first ones she mostly cried, over the man and over the future she had already pictured.

She wanted Wendell to agree that her ex was a bad man. Her own word for him was "sociopath." Wendell did not agree. When the crying slowed, he suggested she was struggling with something bigger than the breakup, and a passing remark of hers, that her life was half over, pointed him there: fear of death, worry about leaving her son an orphan, the question of what her life meant. That was not what she had come for.

##He told her that her feelings did not have to match what she thought they should be.## Gottlieb had said exactly this to her own patients many times. It was the same sentence. Coming from the other chair, it did something different.

Another problem waited in the background: a contract for a book about happiness that she could not write. In therapy she accepted breaking it and repaying the advance. Then she began the book she actually wanted to write, the one you are reading about now.

That book keeps two chairs in view. In one, Gottlieb sits across from Wendell. In the other, she sits across from her own patients.

One of them is John, a successful Hollywood TV producer who is rude to nearly everyone and blames others for whatever goes wrong. It takes months before the grief under that rudeness shows: his young son Gabe died in a car accident.

[[lessons]]
$$**Let someone say your own advice back to you.** A baker tells her apprentices to rest, then bakes straight through her own sick days. When a friend says the same words to her, she finally hears them as meant for her and closes the shop for an afternoon. A sentence you know by heart can land differently when another person says it, because you are no longer the one deciding whether it applies to you. Give them the chance to say it, and notice what changes in how you hear it, especially on a day when you are the one who is tired.$$

A circus with no animals, no star performers and nobody selling snacks in the aisles sounds like a circus that has lost its reasons for existing. Cirque du Soleil took those things out on purpose, and the show got stronger.

[[story]]
Guy Laliberté had been a street performer. When he looked at the traditional circus, he saw what W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne call a red ocean: a crowded market where every competitor offers the same things and fights over the same audience. Their book *Blue Ocean Strategy* (2005) uses his Cirque du Soleil as a flagship case.

The authors give any industry four questions. What does the industry take for granted that adds no value? Eliminate it. What has been overdesigned in the race to match competitors? Reduce it, well below the standard. What compromise are customers forced to accept? Raise the factor that removes it. And what has the industry never offered? Create it.

Cirque answered all four. It eliminated star performers, animal shows, aisle concession sales and the multiple show arenas of the traditional circus. It reduced fun and humor, and thrill and danger. It raised the unique venue.

Then it created. A theme, a refined environment, multiple productions, and artistic music and dance took the place of the old acts. Laliberté's show combined circus and acrobatic skills with adult theatre, original music and a storyline.

The cuts mattered as much as the additions. Dropping animals and star performers removed expensive elements, which lowered costs, while the show aimed at a more refined, higher-priced experience. That is the point of the framework as Kim and Mauborgne state it: to break the trade-off between differentiation and low cost, so you do not have to choose one.

Summaries of the Cirque grid differ slightly on where a few items sit, and the pattern is the same in all of them. A thing the industry took for granted could be removed, and the show grew sharper for it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask what to drop, shrink, grow and add, in that order.** A small bakery can list what every bakery in town does, then notice that one of those items takes hours each week and that no customer ever mentions it. The fixed order matters, because it makes you give something up before you reach for something new, so the list does not turn into a wish list that only grows. Spend ten minutes with the four questions on one product or one habit, and write one answer under each before you decide anything.$$

Mark Manson put his own name on a rule about the beliefs we never test: "The more something threatens your identity, the more you will avoid it." He calls it Manson's Law of Avoidance. It explains why the things we are surest about are often the things we have checked least.

[[story]]
The law sits in a chapter of *The Subtle Art of Not Giving a F*ck* with a deliberately rude title: "You're Wrong About Everything (But So Am I)." Mark Manson isn't trying to insult the reader. His view is that growth is an endlessly repeating process. You never arrive at the truth; you just become a little less wrong each time, and then you do it again.

That makes certainty the enemy. Once you believe you already know something, you stop learning about it. Admitting that you don't know, on the other hand, is the point where learning can start again.

The trouble is that some beliefs are harder to hold loosely than others. ##The ones that feel like part of who we are get the most protection, because being wrong about them would feel like losing a piece of ourselves.## So we steer away from anything that might test them. A belief that is never tested never fails, and so it looks more certain every year.

Manson's way out is a short sequence he offers for becoming "a little less certain of yourself." It is three questions, asked in order.

The first is the plainest: "What if I'm wrong?" It doesn't overturn anything. It only asks you to treat the belief, for a moment, as a guess you are making rather than a fact you have found.

The second goes deeper: "What would it mean if I were wrong?" This is where the Law of Avoidance shows itself. The answer is usually less about the belief and more about what it holds up, the picture of yourself that would have to change if it fell.

The third question sets up a comparison: "Would being wrong create a better or a worse problem than my current problem, for both myself and others?" It ties back to an idea Manson builds earlier in the book, that problems never disappear and only get traded or upgraded. There is no option without a problem. The question is which problem you would rather have, and sometimes the honest answer is that being wrong would leave you with a better one than staying right.

[[lessons]]
$$**Test hardest the beliefs that feel like part of you.** The more a belief is tied to your sense of who you are, the more you avoid anything that could check it, so it can sit untested for decades. A tailor who has always said "I'm not a numbers person" may hand his costings to someone else for years and never see what that has quietly cost him. Asking whether being wrong would give you a better problem than the one you have lets you weigh the change itself, instead of only the discomfort of admitting it.$$

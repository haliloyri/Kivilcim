A Buddhist monk lay in a brain scanner and was asked to do two things with the same suffering in mind. One left the pain circuits quiet. The other left him feeling something he later compared to burnout. Neuroscientists wanted to know what the difference was.

[[story]]
The monk was Matthieu Ricard, and the lab was that of the neuroscientist Tania Singer. Paul Bloom draws on her lab's work. Her lab studies what happens inside the brain when people respond to the suffering of others. In one session Ricard was asked to practise compassion meditation, directed at people who are suffering.

He lay in the scanner while the researchers watched which areas switched on. The brain regions that most people use when they think about another person's pain did not activate during his compassion meditation.

Then he was asked to take an empathic stance, to share the pain of the people he was thinking about. The suffering in his mind was the same as before. This time the circuits tied to empathic distress did light up. Ricard likened that state to being burned out.

##The suffering was the same; only the instruction changed.##

A training study from the same group gave the contrast a second form. Volunteers who were trained in empathy ended up with more negative feeling and more activity in the regions linked to empathy for pain. Compassion training that followed reversed the rise in negative feeling and raised positive feeling.

Bloom takes the pattern as support for a distinction. Empathy means feeling what the other person feels, and it can drain whoever does it. Compassion means caring about the person and wanting to help, and in these studies it left people steadier. A person can offer the second without taking on the first. The studies were small, they came from one research group, and a long-practising monk is an unusual subject, so the evidence is suggestive rather than final. Nothing in these studies tested how the same people would feel after a long shift on a hospital ward or a crisis line.

[[lessons]]
$$**Offer care without absorbing the pain.** Wishing someone well and taking on their feelings are different acts, and the first one is much easier to keep up over many days. A teacher whose pupil's parent breaks down in a meeting can feel every bit of that grief herself, or she can say that she cares and ask what would help. The second route lets her stay useful to the family and still have energy for the rest of her day, and it makes it easier to show up again the next time a parent needs her.$$

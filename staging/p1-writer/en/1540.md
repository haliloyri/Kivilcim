[[open]]
Kindergarteners draw freely, with a crayon and no hesitation. Adults mostly stop, and a whole book about solving problems with pictures starts from that gap.

[[story]]
In the introduction to *The Back of the Napkin* (2008), Dan Roam points to something most readers have seen in a classroom: young children draw without a second thought, and adults do not. Somewhere along the way, he says, adults forgot how to express themselves visually. By the time they are asked to sketch a problem, many of them say they cannot draw.

Roam's own turning point came with a last-minute presentation. Short on time, he sketched a simple framework on a napkin, and the picture cleared his thinking and helped him present. From there he makes a broad claim: any problem can be made clearer with a picture, and any picture can be created with the same set of tools and rules.

The tools are ones everyone already has. The eyes are for looking. The mind's eye is for imagining. Hand-eye coordination is for drawing and showing. None of it asks for artistic skill, because visual thinking, in his account, is a way of thinking before it is a way of drawing.

His method moves in four steps: look, see, imagine, show. Looking collects the raw material and screens it. Seeing selects it and clumps it. Imagining reaches for what is not there yet. Showing makes it clear to somebody else.

When the book turns to developing ideas, it starts from a blank napkin. The most important element goes in the middle, and for a map of competitors that means starting with yourself, since you know you are there. The first box is placed before any detail is decided.

What Roam promises is clarity. A simple, hand-drawn sketch helps you discover and clarify your own idea and then explain it to someone else. The subtitle names both jobs, solving problems and selling ideas, and the book follows them in four parts: an introduction, then discovering ideas, developing them and selling them.

[[lessons]]
$$**A rough sketch is a thinking tool.** A shop owner who cannot explain her delivery round in words may put the shop in the middle of a page, the neighbourhoods around it and arrows between them, and suddenly see where the route doubles back. The drawing does not need to look good, because its job is to make the thinking visible. Once you stop waiting to draw well, you can start with a pen and whatever scrap of paper is nearby, and you can show the result to someone and ask what they see.$$

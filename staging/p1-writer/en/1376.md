"Take away thy opinion, and then there is taken away the complaint, I have been harmed." A Roman emperor wrote that to himself, and then pushed the thought one step further. Remove the complaint, he added, and the harm itself is gone.

[[story]]
The line comes from Book Four of the notebook Marcus Aurelius kept for himself, now read as *Meditations*. He returns to the idea again and again. In Book Eight he puts it most plainly: "If thou art pained by any external thing, it is not this thing that disturbs thee, but thy own judgement about it. And it is in thy power to wipe out this judgement now."

He pushes back on himself. What if the pain comes from failing to do something he believes is right? Then, he asks himself, why not act rather than complain. And if some obstacle truly cannot be overcome? Then he should not grieve, because the reason it is not being done does not depend on him.

Book Four also has the image that frames the whole idea. People seek retreats, he writes, houses in the country, sea-shores and mountains. He wants them too. Then he calls the wish common, a mark of the most ordinary sort of people. Whenever he chooses, he can retire into himself, and nowhere is quieter or freer from trouble than a person's own soul. He tells himself to return often to this little territory of his own, with principles kept brief.

##He names two thoughts to keep ready for that retreat: things do not touch the soul, and our disturbances come only from the opinion within.## The second is that everything he sees changes almost at once. He closes the note in a few words: the universe is transformation, life is opinion.

The notebook also shows where these thoughts came from. In Book One he thanks his teacher Rusticus for introducing him to the discourses of Epictetus, lent from Rusticus's own collection. In Book Eleven he copies a short line from Epictetus into his notes: no man can rob us of our free will. Right after it, he records that we should show no aversion to things that are not in our power.

Marcus wrote some of these books on campaign, and the place notes survive: among the Quadi at the Granua, and in Carnuntum. He had wars to run. In the notebook he argues mainly with the sentences his own mind built about them.

[[lessons]]
$$**Separate what happened from what you made of it.** The event and the meaning we attach to it usually arrive together, as if they were one thought, and pulling them apart is what gives you a choice about your next move. Someone whose message has gone unanswered all day can write down "no reply yet" before writing "he doesn't care about me". The fact stays the same while the anger has less to feed on, and she can decide calmly whether to send one more message tonight or simply wait until tomorrow, and the choice is hers.$$

[[open]]
Two leaders can sit in the same meeting with the same facts. One is closed, defensive and set on being right. The other is open and curious. According to one popular leadership book, the difference between them has nothing to do with skill.

[[story]]
The authors of *The 15 Commitments of Conscious Leadership*, Jim Dethmer, Diana Chapman and Kaley Warner Klemp, start from the picture of a line. A leader, they say, is always either above it or below it, and each of the fifteen commitments is described from both sides.

The authors attach a label to each side. A leader above the line is a conscious one and a leader below it is an unconscious one, meaning someone who has not noticed what they are committed to at that moment.

Below the line, a person is closed and defensive and committed to being right. Someone who is committed to being right hears a colleague's objection as an attack to be beaten. Above the line, the same person is open and curious and committed to learning, and the same objection sounds like information.

The line does not sort people into types. It describes where you stand at a particular moment. The same person can sit above it in the morning meeting and below it while reading an email after lunch. Nobody stays above it all the time, and the authors expect that, so a drop below the line is no cause for embarrassment. Dropping below is treated as ordinary, not as a failing.

Their first step is simple: notice where you are and say so. "I'm below the line right now" is a plain statement, made without shame. The move that follows is just as plain. You go from "I'm right" to a question, and ask what you might be missing.

That is why two leaders at one table can differ so much without differing in ability. They can have the same experience and the same intelligence and still stand on opposite sides of the line. What separates them is what each is committed to at that moment, being right or learning.

[[lessons]]
$$**Say out loud which side of the line you are on.** You cannot stop yourself from dropping below it, but you can notice and name it, and the authors treat that as where change begins. A project lead whose design is being picked apart in a review might hear his tone sharpen and say that he is getting defensive and needs a moment. That does not end the review, yet it buys him time to hear the criticism as information and to ask what he may have missed.$$

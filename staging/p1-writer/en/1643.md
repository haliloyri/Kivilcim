Two flat words, half a smile, and a swimmer already moving again. Brené Brown had just told her husband she was glad they were doing this together. What her mind built over the next fifteen feet of water is a story of its own.

[[story]]
The plan for the family vacation on Lake Travis in 2012 was simple. Each day Brown and her husband Steve would swim across a deepwater cove, about 500 yards each way. She had bought a new swimsuit and goggles the day before. Steve had swum competitively in high school and still swims seriously, while Brown swims for toning.

One early morning, before the rest of the family woke and with the kids left with visiting relatives, they swam out together. Halfway across they stopped to check for boats. Brown felt overwhelmed with gratitude and said so.

> I'm so glad we decided to do this together. It's beautiful out here.

Steve gave a noncommittal half smile.

> Yeah. Water's good.

Then he started swimming again.

Brown was about fifteen feet from him, and she wondered whether he had heard her. Maybe he was too moved to answer, she thought. The moment still felt weird. Embarrassment came first, and shame began to rise behind it. Brown says the body reacts to emotion before the conscious mind does.

A story took shape in her head: Steve was judging how she looked in her swimsuit. Brown's own tool for moments like this is a sentence that begins "The story I'm telling myself is...", which puts the assumption into words so it can be looked at.

Steve's story was different. ##Later he said he had been fighting off a panic attack during the whole swim.## His short answer had nothing to do with her swimsuit and everything to do with what was going on in his chest.

Humans are wired for story, Brown says, and what she calls the rumble is the stage that deals with it. It begins with turning up curiosity and she describes it as the messiest stage of the process: get honest about the story you are telling, test it, and separate what is true from what is only protecting you. She tells this swim both in the book and from the stage.

Brown tells the swim as a story of what could happen if our best selves showed up when we are angry, frustrated or hurt.

[[lessons]]
$$**Say the guess out loud as a guess.** When an answer comes back short, the mind writes the missing part at once, and what it writes is usually about us. A colleague who replies to your message with a single word can easily seem annoyed, though that colleague may simply be buried in something else. Saying "the story I'm telling myself is that you're annoyed with me" turns a verdict into a question and leaves room for the other person to give their side before you act on yours. Once the reply comes, you can tell which part was fact.$$

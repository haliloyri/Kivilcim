"No, ma'am. Three tours." The young soldier said it into a silent room, after several hundred of his fellow special forces soldiers had said nothing at all. The question in front of them had sounded simple. Not one of them could answer it the way the speaker had dared them to.

[[story]]
Brené Brown tells the scene in *Dare to Lead* (2018). It was 2014, on a military base in the Midwest, and she was standing across from several hundred special forces soldiers. For years she had been arguing that courage depends on vulnerability. That day she decided to stop arguing, stop trying to persuade the room, and ask one question instead.

First she gave them her definition: vulnerability is the emotion we experience during times of uncertainty, risk, and emotional exposure. Then she asked: "Can you give me a single example of courage that you've witnessed in another soldier or experienced in your own life that did not require experiencing vulnerability?"

Complete silence. "Crickets," as Brown puts it, in a room full of people whose job is courage. Finally the young soldier spoke: "No, ma'am. Three tours. I can't think of a single act of courage that doesn't require managing massive vulnerability." Brown writes that she has since asked the same question a couple of hundred times, in meeting rooms in many countries.

The rest of the book looks for what that answer means at work. Brown sets two styles of leadership against each other. The armored leader drives perfectionism and a fear of failure, needs to be the knower who is always right, uses power over people, and collects gold stars.

The daring leader models healthy striving instead. They try to be a learner and get it right rather than be right. They use power with people, not over them. And they hand out gold stars rather than hoard them.

For Brown, vulnerability isn't about winning or losing. It is having the courage to show up when you can't control the outcome. Armor is how we avoid those moments, and in the short run it feels like protection.

Her warning about where it leads is blunt: ##"When we get to the place that the armor is so thick that we no longer feel anything, we experience a real death."## The soldier's answer is the counterweight. If people trained for combat describe their own courage in terms of managing vulnerability, the armor the rest of us wear to meetings is protecting us from less than we think.

[[lessons]]
$$**Notice the armor you put on when you're unsure.** Needing to look like the person who already knows, even in a situation that is new to everyone, is one of the most common ways people protect themselves, and it quietly teaches everyone around them to stop raising doubts. A new restaurant manager who answers every supplier question on the spot, even when she is guessing, soon finds her cooks keeping problems and doubts to themselves until they become expensive. Saying "I don't know yet, I'll find out" gets you better information and makes it easier for others to tell you the truth.$$

A surgical resident working about 110 hours a week sat down at night and on weekends to write. Atul Gawande's notes describe the uncertainty behind medicine's flawless face, and they carry the weight of tiredness as they do.

[[story]]
Atul Gawande wrote the essays of *Complications* while he was a general surgery resident at Brigham and Women's Hospital. He worked about 110 hours a week and found time to write at night and on weekends. That is close to two thirds of the hours in a week. The book describes the uncertainty behind medicine's polished surface from inside that long-hours system.

The first essay, "Education of a Knife," shows how a surgeon is trained. Gawande recounts his difficulty inserting a central venous catheter. The same piece includes an emergency tracheotomy that nearly killed a patient. The reader sees a real body under inexperienced hands.

The essay's central question is ethical. Every surgeon was once a beginner, and beginners learn on real patients. The patient pays for the learner's inexperience. That tension, between the wish to gain experience and the patient's wellbeing, grows heavier when it meets exhaustion.

In a hard case, the wish to practise collides with the responsibility to hand the patient to someone fresher and more experienced. The book's third part is devoted to uncertainty. Its essay "The Case of the Red Leg" shows another face of it: is this common cellulitis or rare, deadly necrotizing fasciitis? Having recently seen the second, Gawande decides to do a biopsy.

The two accounts show the same thing: in medicine a decision is often made by accepting risk, not by certainty. What fatigue does to a decision, the book leaves as a question raised by the system it describes.

##What Gawande shows is not flawless medicine but how people who carry the risk of error manage that risk.## The book sets the profession's polished front beside the uncertainty of those who work there.

[[lessons]]
$$**Decide in advance when you will ask for help.** An accountant finishing the last file after a very long shift cannot measure their own tiredness objectively, and is closest to a mistake at exactly that moment. Saying before you start "after this hour I'll have a friend check it" settles the risk while you are still strong. You then hold a rule set beforehand instead of a judgment left to a tired mind.$$

[[reflect]]
&&In your own work, what is one situation where you would want a clear rule, set in advance, for handing something to a person fresher than you?&&

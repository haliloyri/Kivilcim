A small hamburger business in Southern California had everything its owners could ask for: an efficient kitchen, steady customers and a comfortable living. The two brothers who ran it never turned it into more. A partner who arrived later did, under the same name.

[[story]]
John Maxwell opens his first law in *The 21 Irrefutable Laws of Leadership* (1998) with a ceiling. He calls it the Law of the Lid: the lower a person's ability to lead, the lower the lid on everything else they can do. He puts numbers on it, using a scale from 1 to 10. A leader at 8 can reach an effectiveness of 7 at most, and a leader at 4 tops out around 3.

The scale makes the claim uncomfortable. Effort sits on one side and leadership on the other, and Maxwell says the second sets the limit for the first. Hard work under a 4 still ends near a 3.

His main case is two brothers, Dick and Maurice McDonald, who ran a small hamburger business in Southern California. Maxwell credits them with a real gift for kitchen organization and for customer service. The kitchen was built for speed and the hamburgers were popular. The brothers made a comfortable living and, in his account, never grew the business into what it later became. Maxwell calls them efficient managers, and says their thinking set the limit. Their lid was low.

Then Ray Kroc partnered with them and later bought the exclusive rights to the business. Between 1955 and 1959 about a hundred restaurants opened. Four years after that there were five hundred.

Maxwell reads the difference in the leader, and he says the lid in Kroc's life was sky high. ##The kitchen and the hamburgers already existed; the lid decided how far they would travel.##

He does not leave the lid as a verdict. A person can raise it by studying and developing how they lead, and Maxwell says that pays back far more than simply working harder. Organizations in a hard stretch sometimes take the other route and replace the leader.

[[lessons]]
$$**Measure your lid before you add hours.** A repair shop owner who works every evening can still be the reason the shop stays small, because every quote, refund and schedule change waits for her. Her effort is not the limit, her way of leading is. If she spends part of a week teaching one employee to make those calls, she will see whether the same hours now go further. You can run the same test in your own work: pick one decision that waits on you and hand it over with a clear rule.$$

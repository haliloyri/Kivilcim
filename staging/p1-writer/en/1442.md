[[open]]
A small light on a nuclear plant's control panel went dark, and the operators took it to mean a valve had closed. It meant only that a command to close had been sent.

[[story]]
Don Norman has said that after the Three Mile Island accident of 1979 he was called in, with other psychologists, to help retrain the plant's operators. The accident record shows what the light was. The indicator for the pilot-operated relief valve was wired to the valve's solenoid. It reported that a close command had been given, not that the valve had shut. A temperature indicator downstream could have hinted that the valve was stuck, but the operators had not been trained to look at it.

Norman's book defines feedback as full and continuous information about the results of actions, and measured against that definition the light fell short. It showed the command and not the result. The inquiry did not put all the blame on the panel: the President's Commission listed deficiencies in control-room design, procedures, training, management and regulation.

The book breaks action into seven stages. Four belong to execution: forming the goal, forming the intention, specifying the action and carrying it out. Three belong to evaluation: perceiving the outcome, interpreting it and comparing it with the goal. The gulf of evaluation is the effort needed to read a system's state and judge whether the goal was met. In that control room, operators had to work out the state of the valve from an indicator that did not show it.

One of the book's everyday examples is a video recorder whose recording needs several steps beyond pressing Record. The principle of visibility asks that users can see a device's state and their options. The gulf of execution is its mirror: the gap between what a person wants to do and the actions the system demands. The book uses even a reading lamp and its switch to walk through the stages.

[[lessons]]
$$**Show that the job is done, not only that it was ordered.** The light reported a command, not what the valve did, and that gap was all the operators had to work with. A cook who turns a kitchen timer's dial and hears nothing cannot tell whether it is running. A timer that shows the time left lets her see at a glance that it is working, which spares her from guessing. Any signal that confirms only the order leaves people to guess about the result.$$

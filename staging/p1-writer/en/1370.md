The instructor read the results off the board and burst out laughing. Then she turned to the one non-academic in the room and asked whether all he did was say no and stare at people until they caved. He had not said the word once in two days.

[[story]]
The man was Chris Voss, and the room was Harvard Law School's winter negotiation course in 2006, which he describes in *Never Split the Difference* (2016). There were 144 students on the first day, drawn from Harvard and other Boston schools. The first exercise was a plain sale: one buyer, one seller, each with a price in mind.

His partner, whom Voss calls Andy, was a confident student who laid out an offer and a watertight argument for it. Voss kept answering with some version of one question: how am I supposed to do that? After a few rounds they settled on a price. Andy had been told to keep a quarter of his budget in reserve for later tasks. Voss walked away with all of it, reserve included. The next day it happened again with someone else.

##That was when the instructor, Sheila Heen, asked whether he simply said no and stared until people gave in.##

He never used the word. His questions only sounded like it. They suggested that the offer on the table was not quite fair, and the other side started negotiating against themselves.

The book gives a whole chapter to that word. For Voss, "no" is rarely a final rejection. More often it means "I'm not ready to agree yet." Saying it makes people feel safe and in control. That is why skilled negotiators go looking for a no, because the real negotiation often begins there.

"Yes" comes in three kinds. A counterfeit yes is a polite escape from an uncomfortable conversation. A confirmation yes is a reflex with no commitment behind it. A commitment yes is the real agreement, the one that leads to action. When you push someone toward yes, you mostly collect the first two.

His practical advice works on the next phone call you make. Instead of "Do you have a few minutes to talk?", ask "Is now a bad time to talk?" The second question gives the other person an easy no. Either they tell you it is fine to talk, or they tell you it is a bad time and you have lost nothing. The same logic sits behind openers like "Would it be ridiculous to...?"

[[lessons]]
$$**Leave the other person an easy no.** People tend to speak more honestly when they know they are allowed to refuse, because the refusal itself gives them a sense of control. An employee who wants to leave early one afternoon, instead of asking for permission outright, can start by asking whether it would be a ridiculous request this week. The manager can say no without feeling cornered, and usually follows it with the real concern or the condition that would make it work. That way the conversation opens up instead of closing on a polite yes that nobody meant.$$

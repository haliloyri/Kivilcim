Seventeen times. On a silent meditation retreat, a teacher told Dan Harris that worrying seventeen times about one missed flight was pointless. He didn't tell him to stop worrying altogether. He gave him a question to ask instead, and it's the most practical thing in a book with a deliberately small promise on its cover.

[[story]]
Dan Harris called his book *10% Happier* on purpose. He had written about meditation, but he wasn't claiming enlightenment, a transformed life or the end of his problems. His verdict was that it had made him about ten percent happier. It was not a cure-all, and he said so on the front cover.

The figure started half as a joke. It was his answer to skeptics who heard that a TV news correspondent had taken up meditation and assumed he had joined a cult. A small number sounded like something a reporter could stand behind.

What did the ten percent consist of? In his telling, the main gain was "the ability to respond rather than react": a short gap between an urge and acting on it. ##He compares it to the seven-second delay on live award shows, the pause that lets a producer catch something before it reaches viewers.##

The place he needed that gap most was worry. His father had a mantra, "The price of security is insecurity," and for years Harris had used it to justify his own anxiety. Worrying felt like part of being alert and doing the job well.

On a ten-day silent retreat, a teacher took that apart with very little. Worrying seventeen times about a missed flight, he pointed out, was pointless. When a worry shows up, the question to ask is simply: "Is this useful?"

What makes the question work is that it doesn't ban worry. The worry that makes you prepare, pack, book the next flight, can stay. The seventeenth replay of the same thought adds nothing to the first one, however urgent it feels at the time, and can be let go.

His new calm got tested in public too. The book recounts a tense interview with Paris Hilton that put his new steadiness under pressure, on camera, in the middle of his day job.

That is the whole size of Harris's claim: a seven-second delay, a question about usefulness, and a modest share of his days back. The anxieties didn't vanish and the work stayed hard.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask whether a worry is useful.** Part of worrying genuinely helps you prepare, but repeating the same thought usually adds nothing and only wears you down. A student lying awake before an exam, going over the same question for the fifth time, can write the answer down on a card and leave it for the morning. Asking this turns the useful part of a worry into a concrete task and lets you drop the rest, so anxiety keeps doing its one helpful job, getting you ready, without taking over the whole evening or the night that follows.$$

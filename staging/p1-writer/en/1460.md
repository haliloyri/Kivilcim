[[open]]
Twenty-five competencies, five groups, and one big claim about which of them counts most at work. Daniel Goleman's model asks you to name each group before you decide how good you are with people, and it says that, unlike IQ, these skills can be developed.

[[story]]
In *Working with Emotional Intelligence* (1998), Daniel Goleman follows his bestseller *Emotional Intelligence* with a question aimed at the workplace: what do people who excel at work have that others lack? His answer is a set of emotional competencies. Unlike IQ, he says, they can be developed, partly by replacing bad habits with good ones and by growing self-awareness. He is explicit that this is not the same as being nice or giving free rein to feelings.

The model sorts 25 competencies into five dimensions: self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy and social skills. The first three are personal, about how you handle yourself, and the last two are social, about how you handle others. Each group has its own list, and the guide to the book adds that good stress and bad stress produce different biological responses, adrenaline in one case and cortisol in the other.

Goleman's headline claim is that for star performance in every field, emotional intelligence matters twice as much as IQ or technical expertise. That claim has critics. Meta-analytic data, they say, does not support the "twice as important" figure, so it is safest to read it as Goleman's emphasis and not as a measured ratio. The book also argues that among people smart enough to enter a field, IQ has the least power to predict who succeeds.

His picture of star performers is about teams as much as individuals. Stars excel on their own, he writes, and they are the people best able to maximize a team's potential. The data he follows over several decades shows a shift: team building and adapting to change hardly mattered in the 1970s and were decisive by the 1990s, and change catalyst and leveraging diversity have joined the list of star traits. The guide to the book also points to team capabilities, where a group's collective emotional intelligence drives its success.

He also draws on McClelland's finding of a tipping point. People who excelled in six or more competencies stood out as stars, and they typically had strengths in at least one competency from each group. Strength in one corner was not enough.

[[lessons]]
$$**Rate yourself on each group, not on "people skills" as a whole.** A site manager who thinks of herself as good with people may be strong at sensing how a crew feels and weak at keeping her own temper when a delivery arrives late. Naming the five groups lets her see which of the two she is actually talking about, and she can practise the weaker one on purpose instead of working harder at the stronger one. Pick the group you rate lowest and watch for one situation this week where it matters, so that the rating becomes something you can check.$$

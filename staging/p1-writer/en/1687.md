Anyone who falls into quicksand feels the pull to thrash. One therapist's book builds its whole argument about happiness on that pull, and then looks for a way out that starts with stillness.

[[story]]
Russ Harris describes the happiness trap in a single sentence in *The Happiness Trap* (2007): to find happiness we try to avoid or get rid of bad feelings, but the harder we try, the more bad feelings we create. Harris is a physician and therapist, and the book brings acceptance and commitment therapy, ACT, to general readers.

Part of the reason, Harris says, is a myth: that humans are naturally happy. Society keeps telling us that everyone can and should be happy, and media images make us envy people who seem beautiful, intelligent and wealthy. Three traits that once helped survival, detecting threats, fitting into a group and a drive to accumulate and improve, now produce imaginary threats and constant comparison.

He sorts the ways of managing bad feelings into two kinds. One is to fight: to overpower negative thoughts and feelings, for example by suppression. The other is to flee: to avoid or ignore them. Both belong to the same trap.

He illustrates it with quicksand. When you fall in, your emotions tell you to struggle, and struggling makes it worse. His struggle switch works on the same principle: an imaginary switch in the back of the brain.

With the switch on, we amplify a negative feeling by focusing on it and trying to get rid of it. With the switch off, we notice the feeling and let it be there. The thinking self, he says, is like a radio playing constantly in the background, and most of the time it is Radio Doom and Gloom, broadcasting negative stories 24 hours a day.

A full life, in Harris's account, brings a full range of human emotions. Happiness here means a meaningful life lived by your values, not constant good feelings. As he puts it, feelings are not values.

The book's short formula is accept your thoughts and feelings and be present, connect with your values, take effective action. Notes on the book compare emotions to weather and the observing self to the sky: the weather keeps changing, and the sky has room for all of it.

[[lessons]]
$$**Take the step your values point to while the feeling is still there.** Someone dreading a phone call to the bank waits for the dread to pass, and the dread grows while they wait. If they make the call with the dread along for the ride, the call is short and the pile of delay never forms. They have stopped treating feeling ready as a condition and put it next to the task instead.$$

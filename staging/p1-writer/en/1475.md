In most farming societies, at least one child in three died before turning twenty, according to Yuval Noah Harari. Those societies still outnumbered the foragers around them, and in the end they fed far more people. He calls the whole arrangement a fraud, and the first suspect is a grass.

[[story]]
Yuval Noah Harari titles chapter five of *Sapiens* (2011) "History's Biggest Fraud." The fraud is the Agricultural Revolution, usually told as humanity's clever leap forward. His reading is blunter. The average farmer worked harder than the average forager, and ate worse for it.

The one that came out ahead was wheat. Around 10,000 years ago it was a wild grass in a small corner of the Middle East, and a few thousand years later it was growing across the world. Harari says humans did not domesticate wheat. Wheat domesticated humans. Farmers cleared stones from the field, pulled weeds, watched for worms and blight, built fences, dug irrigation canals and spread animal dung. Bodies built for walking and foraging took the strain, and Harari links the work to slipped discs, arthritis and hernias.

The plate shrank too. Humans are omnivores, and before farming, grains were a small part of what they ate. A diet built on cereals is poor in minerals and vitamins. Security did not improve either: a village living off a few crops could lose thousands, even millions of people to a single failed harvest.

So why did nobody walk back? Harari describes a trap. Parents reasoned that more work in the field would secure their children's food, and the reasoning was sound. Then families grew, food had to be shared, disease moved through crowded settlements, and thieves made walls and guards necessary. The change took generations, so nobody remembered the earlier life well enough to compare. ##Harari calls it the luxury trap: luxuries become necessities, and then obligations.##

The numbers show who the trap worked for. Land that fed 100 healthy foragers could carry a crowded village of 1,000, at the price of disease and malnutrition. Births still outran deaths, even with a third of the children gone before twenty.

Harari reaches for a company comparison. A species succeeds by the number of copies of its DNA, the way a company succeeds by the money it earns, not by how happy its staff are. Wheat's copies multiplied. The people who tended it each lived worse. Not everyone accepts his case, and at least one reviewer finds his evidence questionable.

[[lessons]]
$$**Write down what a convenience will cost before you adopt it.** A shop owner installs an ordering app so she can check sales after closing, and at first it saves her a walk to the till. A few months on, supper happens with the phone beside the plate, and checking has become part of the job. Once a new habit is normal, giving it up feels like losing something you never needed. If you list what a convenience asks in return while it is still optional, you can decide with the price in view, not after.$$

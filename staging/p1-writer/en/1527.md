The voices that interrupt a writer's morning turn out to be small enough to carry by the tail. One mason jar holds all of them, and the jar has a volume knob. Each complainer, in this exercise, becomes a mouse.

[[story]]
Anne Lamott admits in *Bird by Bird* (1994) that, left to its own devices, her mind spends much of its time having conversations with people who are not there. While she drafts, the voices are specific: an imagined reader who is not impressed, a menacing critic, her own parents shaking their heads. Quieting them, she says, is at least half the battle she fights daily.

A hypnotist once taught her a visualization for this. She gets quiet and lets the chatter come, then isolates one critical voice. She imagines the person speaking as a mouse, picks it up by the tail and drops it into a mason jar.

The others go in the same way: the high-maintenance parents, the contractors, the lawyers, the colleagues, the children, anyone who is whining in her head. She puts the lid on and watches the mouse people clawing at the glass. The voices have not gone anywhere. They are in a jar, scrabbling.

Then she gives the jar a volume-control button. She turns it all the way up for a minute, no longer, and listens to the stream of angry, neglected, guilt-mongering voices. All of it comes through, one after another and all at once.

After that she turns it all the way down. The mice are still there, frantic, lunging at the glass to get to her.

##Leave it down, and get back to your shitty first draft.## The exercise does not wait for the voices to leave. The draft waiting for her is a rough one, and that is fine, because it only has to exist. She leaves the mice lunging behind the glass with the sound off and turns back to the rough page in front of her.

[[lessons]]
$$**If you cannot silence a voice, give it a size.** A baker about to open her own stall may hear a voice that lists every way the first week could fail. If she pictures that voice as a small figure behind glass, listens to it once on purpose and then turns it down, she can go back to mixing the first batch. The voice does not have to leave for her to get on with the work, only to stop being the one who sets the pace.$$

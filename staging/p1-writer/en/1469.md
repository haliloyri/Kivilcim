The idea behind the World Wide Web took Tim Berners-Lee about ten years, and one root of it was a Victorian household guide he met as a child. Darwin's famous breakthrough looks different when you open his notebooks. In both cases the flash is a story told afterward.

[[story]]
Tim Berners-Lee's web did not arrive in a flash. Steven Johnson traces it across about a decade in *Where Good Ideas Come From*, starting with a childhood encounter with a Victorian how-to book, *Enquire Within Upon Everything*. Later, at CERN in 1980, Berners-Lee wrote a personal program of his own, called Enquire, a small ancestor of the tool he would eventually propose to the world. The childhood guide and the adult program are separated by years of work.

During those years the idea kept coming back to him in different forms. People often credit their ideas to a Eureka moment, Johnson says, and history tells a different story. He calls this kind of idea a slow hunch: a half-formed thought that stays around for a long time before it turns into something.

Darwin is his other example. The usual telling has Darwin reading Malthus on population in 1838 and suddenly seeing natural selection, a single book on a single day. Johnson, drawing on historian Howard Gruber's study of Darwin's notebooks, says the pieces were already on the pages before that moment.

The two stories look alike at the start. Darwin's notebooks hold no finished theory, and Berners-Lee's program is not yet a web. The pieces accumulate and meet, and the moment that looks like a flash is the visible end of that process. The Malthus story is attractive for the same reason it is tidy: one book, one date, one result.

##Johnson's conclusion is practical: slow hunches need somewhere to survive.## A notebook can do it, or a group of people willing to listen. The half-idea has to stay alive until it meets another piece, and a notebook carries it to that meeting without losing it along the way.

[[lessons]]
$$**Write the half-formed idea down and return to it.** An unfinished idea tends to vanish unless it has a place to live. A baker who jots a half-formed thought about a new bread in a kitchen notebook may find, a month later, that a different experiment sits beside it and completes the recipe. That makes it easier to stop demanding that an idea pay off immediately, and to treat going back to the notebook as part of the work itself.$$

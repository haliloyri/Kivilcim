In a classroom in Soweto, Shawn Achor asked who liked doing schoolwork. Almost every hand went up, and the children were smiling. His escort explained why. That answer, and a bag of candy given to a group of doctors, sit at the centre of Achor's case against a formula most of us learned early.

[[story]]
The formula goes like this: work hard, become successful, then you will be happy. In *The Happiness Advantage* (2010), Shawn Achor argues that it has a built-in flaw. Every success moves the goalpost. A good grade makes way for the next grade, a promotion for the next promotion. Happiness keeps getting pushed one step further out.

Achor's claim is that the order runs the other way. Happiness comes first, and success is built on it. To back this he leans on a large review by Sonja Lyubomirsky, Laura King and Ed Diener, who examined 225 studies covering more than 275,000 people. Happiness was linked to success at work, in relationships and in health, and it often came before that success. Happy people were also more inclined to seek out new goals. The authors add that the influence runs in both directions.

One of his clearest examples involves doctors. Forty-four internists were asked to read a patient file and reach a diagnosis. Before starting, half were handed a small bag of candy as a thank-you, and asked not to eat it during the task. ##The doctors in a good mood reached the correct diagnosis earlier.## Achor describes the gap as nearly twice as fast. His explanation is chemical: positive emotions release dopamine and serotonin, which he says turn up the brain's learning centres, so people organise and retrieve information faster.

In another study, students asked to think about the happiest day of their lives right before a maths test outperformed peers who went in without that moment.

Then there is Soweto. The escort told Achor that the children saw schoolwork as a privilege their parents had never had. Achor says he saw the same split at Harvard: students who treated being there as a privilege did better, while those who saw only the workload and the stress did worse.

These scenes make one argument. Held back as a reward for when the work is done, happiness may never arrive. Placed at the start, it works more like a resource the brain draws on to do the work well.

[[lessons]]
$$**Move a small good moment to the start of hard work.** Someone facing a difficult report often saves their favourite coffee and playlist as a treat for when it's finished, and as the report drags on, the treat slides to the evening and then to tomorrow. Having that coffee as they sit down means they begin the first page in a better frame of mind. Moving the reward doesn't make the task itself any easier, but it changes the mind you bring to it and breaks the cycle of waiting for a finish line that keeps moving.$$

In front of a hotel in Los Angeles, a young valet parked other people's luxury cars all day, and everyone assumed the drivers were rich. Years later, Morgan Housel wrote about exactly what that assumption could not see, and about what the cars really showed.

[[story]]
In *The Psychology of Money*, Morgan Housel builds a chapter on the days he worked as a valet in Los Angeles. All day he parked luxury cars, and everyone around assumed the people who drove them were rich. Each set of keys he took seemed to confirm the same guess one more time.

What Housel points out is what an expensive car actually tells you about its owner. It shows that someone spent a large sum. What is left afterwards stays out of view. Plenty of money may remain, or none at all.

That is why he says spending money to show how much you have is a fast way to end up with less of it. Display makes money visible, and in the same act makes it unavailable for anything else. What came with the keys was a car, and nothing in the handover said what the owner had left.

In the book he separates two ideas. Being rich means having a high income. Being wealthy means having money you have not spent, and the two can come apart: a high earner who spends it all can end up with no wealth.

Wealth, as he defines it, is the assets that have not yet been turned into things you can see. It is the car not bought and the renovation not done. Because it is made of choices not to spend, it cannot be photographed, and it cannot be read from someone's possessions.

A related observation in another chapter fits here. When people look at a beautiful car, he says, they picture themselves inside it instead of admiring the driver. The person looking is really seeing their own wish, and that wish tells you nothing about the owner's bank balance.

From the valet's stand, everything visible was a photograph of money already spent, and the part that mattered most stayed out of frame.

[[lessons]]
$$**Read a purchase as money spent.** What is left stays out of sight. The owner of a small café might ask herself whether she wants a new espresso machine because it will serve her customers or because it will make the shop look grander. If she does not buy it, that money stays available for a future rent payment or a bad month. So each time you spend, you can look at the object and also at the option you would be keeping. The second question tells you more about how much freedom a spending choice costs you.$$

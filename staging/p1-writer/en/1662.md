A commencement speaker with a spectacular career tells the graduates that career success is not that important and that failure can be wonderful. One writer calls the whole genre a secular sermon, and he lists what it actually hands the graduates on their way out.

[[story]]
David Brooks opens the second chapter of *The Second Mountain* with the commencement address. Every society, he says, has a way of passing its values to the young. Ours is a secular sermon. Colleges usually ask someone distinguished by fantastic career success to give a speech claiming that career success is not that important, and these speakers then tell the audience not to be afraid to fail.

From this, Brooks says, young people learn that failure can be wonderful if you happen to be J. K. Rowling, Denzel Washington or Steve Jobs. But the speeches hand over more than that. They pass along the dominant values of the age, and he calls the presents great big boxes of nothing.

One box holds freedom: the purpose of life is to be free. Another holds possibility: your future is limitless. A third holds authenticity: look inside yourself, find your true passion, you do you. And the emptiest holds autonomy: it is up to you to define your own values, so do what you love.

##The graduates, Brooks says, are drowning in freedom.## At every box they put it down and ask the next question: what is freedom for, how do I know which path is mine, what can I devote myself to? He points out that the "you" they are told to consult is the very thing that has not yet formed. He quotes Kierkegaard on needing to find the idea for which one is willing to live and die, and then asks how it can be that on this biggest question we have nothing to say.

He ties this to a larger shift. In the middle of the twentieth century a group-oriented moral ecology took hold, summed up as "We're All in This Together": duty, conformity, humility. It also tolerated racism and trapped many people in conformity. It gave way to "I'm Free to Be Myself": freedom, autonomy, authenticity. His central argument is that we have overdone the individualist worldview.

He cites one survey. The Harvard Graduate School of Education asked ten thousand middle and high school students whether their parents cared more about personal achievement or about kindness. Eighty percent said achievement.

[[lessons]]
$$**Give advice about freedom a direction.** A manager who answers a young colleague's question about what to do next with "follow your gut" has handed the question straight back, unanswered, and the colleague leaves with nothing to try on Monday. She could ask instead which work or which people the colleague would like to be useful to, and then propose one concrete task. That turns freedom into a chance to join something, and the young colleague no longer has to judge every option by looking only inward, which is where the answer has not yet formed.$$

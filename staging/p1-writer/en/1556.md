[[open]]
A search box with no price on it, no cap on how many questions go in, and a company behind it that earns money anyway. The arrangement looks like a riddle until you ask who else is in the picture besides the person typing.

[[story]]
Google's search box asks its users for nothing: no fee, and no limit on how many searches they run. Free is a pattern of its own. In *Business Model Generation* (2010), Alexander Osterwalder and Yves Pigneur give it a chapter beside unbundling, the long tail, multi-sided platforms and open business models.

The rule is simple. At least one customer group always receives the value at no cost, and something else pays for it, either another part of the business model or another customer segment. The authors sort the pattern into three kinds: advertising, premium services and continuing purchases.

Google belongs to the first kind. Advertisers pay, and their money is what lets people search without limit and without a bill. ##Two groups sit in this business, and only one of them ever pays.## The chapter on multi-sided platforms describes the same shape, with two or more customer groups that depend on each other, where the more active users a platform draws in, the more it is worth to every side.

The other two kinds work differently. Premium services keep a basic version free and charge for an upgrade, and one summary names Evernote, with three plans: free, personal and professional. Continuing purchases start with a cheap or free first item that creates demand for the follow-up, such as coffee machines that need capsules or printers that need ink cartridges.

On the canvas, the pieces are easy to find. A multi-sided platform is one of the listed kinds of customer segment, and advertising is one of seven listed ways to earn revenue, next to asset sales, usage fees, subscriptions, lending or renting, licensing and brokerage.

Facebook turns up in the same discussion as another free service paid for by advertisers, and one summary says it auctions advertising space based on categories of its users. The same summary adds that the logic can run in reverse, with a high-priced premium product paired with a cheap complement that people keep buying.

[[lessons]]
$$**Name who pays for what you give away.** A neighbourhood paper handed out free at the station has two groups around it: the commuters who read it and the shopkeepers who buy the advertising space. As the readership grows, the paper becomes worth more to the shopkeepers, and their money pays for the printing. When you plan to offer something for nothing, write down who covers the cost and what that person gets in return. Then you can tell whether the free offer will carry itself, or whether you are quietly paying for it out of your own pocket.$$

"I would definitely buy that." A founder who hears this after describing a product feels the warm glow of a sale. Rob Fitzpatrick calls it the most dangerous kind of sentence a customer can say, and he explains why it should make you reach for a different question.

[[story]]
In *The Mom Test* (2013), Fitzpatrick sorts the bad answers customers give into three kinds: compliments, fluff and ideas. They have one trait in common. Each feels like information while it arrives, and each leaves you with nothing to build on once the meeting is over.

Fitzpatrick's explanation for why such answers appear is not cynical. People often do not want to hurt you, do not know their own preferences, or have nothing at stake, so they hand over what is easiest: an agreeable line, said with a smile.

Compliments come first. He calls them the fool's gold of customer learning: shiny, distracting and worthless. When someone tells you your idea is great, he suggests you do not accept it and keep the conversation going. Ask why they like it. Ask what else they have tried. Ask what it cost them. Real interest has answers to those questions, and polite interest does not.

##Fluff comes in three shapes, and "I would definitely buy that" belongs to the second.## The first shape is the generic claim: "I usually", "I always", "I never". The second is the future promise: "I will", "I would". The third is the hypothetical: "I might", "I could". None of them describes something that happened. They describe a person's mood, a plan for a future they have not lived, or a habit that they may or may not have.

His way out is to anchor the person to a real moment. Bring the conversation back to the last time they actually ran into the problem, and let them walk you through it. A concrete episode cannot be generic, and it is hard to promise.

Then there are ideas and feature requests. When someone says "you should add X", it is tempting to write it down and build it. Fitzpatrick wants you to look for the problem underneath. Ask "Why do you want that?", find out how they handle things today, and learn what they hope the feature will do for them.

Each fix is a small redirect, away from what the person says about the future and back toward something that already happened in their week.

[[lessons]]
$$**Treat praise and promises as noise until a past example backs them.** A friend who says she would love your recipe box has told you about her mood, but a friend who tells you when she last cooked from scratch and what got in the way has told you about her life. The second answer hands you a date, an obstacle and a habit you can check against reality. It lets you decide how much of the first answer deserves belief, without having to doubt your friend's kindness, and it tells you what to ask next time.$$

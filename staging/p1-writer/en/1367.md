Most business plans have a floor: the least a company must earn, sell or sign each year. Very few have a ceiling. One airline worked with a ceiling anyway, while more than a hundred cities were asking for its planes and only four of them got a yes.

[[story]]
Paul Jarvis tells that airline's story in *Company of One* (2019). He spent years designing websites for corporations like Microsoft and Mercedes-Benz, with clients that included Shaquille O'Neal and Steve Nash. Then he moved his work home, to an island off Canada's west coast, and ran it from there. The book defends that small scale as a deliberate choice, made with open eyes.

His "company of one" can be one person at a kitchen table or a small team, and headcount barely matters. What sets it apart is a habit of questioning growth before treating it as the default goal. Every chance to expand gets two questions. Is growth truly needed here? And if it is, can it be done with the same resources or fewer?

Jarvis sees four traits in companies like this: resilience, autonomy, speed and simplicity. A new office lease or a longer payroll can quietly wear down one of them. So the book refuses to treat growth as free, and asks for the bill before each step is taken.

Southwest Airlines in 1996 is his large-scale example. More than a hundred cities wanted the airline to serve their airports. Southwest added four new locations. As Jarvis tells it, that restraint helped the airline stay profitable while most of its competitors were losing money.

From cases like this he draws a working rule. Goals need an upper bound as well as a lower one. The upper bound sits at the point where returns start to shrink, where one more client or one more hour adds more strain than value.

To find that ceiling, the book also sets a floor, which Jarvis calls minimum viable profit. It is the profit that covers a full-time salary for the owner, and it counts as viable when it can keep that person going over time. The space between floor and ceiling is the zone where the business is enough.

The question that runs through the book shifts once you see it that way. Owners usually ask how to make the business larger. Jarvis keeps asking how to make it better. ##"Not all growth is beneficial, and some growth can actually reduce your resilience and your autonomy."## That year Southwest left most of the cities on its list waiting at the gate.

[[lessons]]
$$**Write a ceiling next to every goal.** A floor tells you how much you need to earn, and a ceiling tells you where more stops helping. A home caterer whose orders keep climbing could rent a second kitchen right away, or she could decide the most orders she can cook well in a week and politely turn the rest down. Once that number is written, a new opportunity gets weighed against the room left in her week. You can do the same with clients, projects or commitments, and the hardest no becomes a simple check against a line you already drew.$$

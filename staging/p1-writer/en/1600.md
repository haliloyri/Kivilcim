Two silicone plugs go into the nostrils, and surgical tape goes over them so that no air can pass. For about ten days, two men at Stanford will breathe through their mouths only. Both are healthy volunteers, and one of them is the writer James Nestor.

[[story]]
Nestor tells this experiment in *Breath* (2020). He and Anders Olsson, a 49-year-old breathing therapist from Stockholm, signed up for a study run by Dr. Jayakar Nayak, Stanford's chief of rhinology research. Phase one was about ten days of mouth-only breathing. Phase two was about ten days of nose-only breathing. Between phases they went back to the clinic for tests.

Before the plugs went in, Nayak sampled about 200,000 sinus cells and did endoscopic exams. Nestor's X-ray showed a severely deviated septum with a badly obstructed left nostril, so his nose was not an easy one to breathe through to begin with. During the mouth phase, both men ate, exercised and slept as usual.

The numbers from Nestor's mouth-breathing phase read like a medical chart. By his account, his snoring rose almost 5,000 percent and he averaged about 25 sleep apnea episodes a night. His systolic blood pressure climbed to 142, which is stage 2 hypertension, and one report says his blood oxygen dipped below 85 percent. Both men showed higher stress hormones and lower heart rate variability, and Nestor picked up a bacterial infection in his nose.

##Then the tape came off, and the reversal was quick.## In the nose phase, the sleep apnea disappeared. Snoring fell by about 85 percent and stopped after two nights for both men. Nestor's blood pressure returned to a healthy range, and his nasal infection cleared up without treatment.
There is a limit to what this proves. Two men, one experiment, and figures that differ between press accounts. Most of the figures come from Nestor's own account and press coverage, and no peer-reviewed paper accompanies them. What it does show is how much happened in ten days to someone who changed only the door the air went through.

[[lessons]]
$$**Notice which way you breathe when you are not thinking about it.** Someone who wakes with a dry mouth or snores on a stuffy night could check, over a few days, whether the lips are closed while working, reading or watching a screen, and jot down what they find each evening. The point is not to force anything but to see how often the mouth is doing a job the nose could do. Once you see the habit, you can choose to close your lips and watch what changes in your sleep and your energy.$$

[[story]]
In *A Whole New Mind* (2005), Daniel Pink counts design as the first of his six senses. His argument is that it is no longer enough to make something merely functional. Today, he says, making something that is also beautiful, whimsical or emotionally engaging pays off in the market and is rewarding for the person who does it.

The reason is abundance. When material wants are satisfied, products that work are everywhere, and function stops being what sets one apart from another. The iPod is the example that summaries of the chapter pick out: it took over the market from MP3 players that had more functionality. What buyers chose in that case was not the longer list of features.

Take an ordinary example of our own, not Pink's. Two kettles boil water equally well. One has a handle that sits well in the hand and a shape you do not mind leaving on the counter. Most people reach for that one, and they could not always tell you why. That gap between "it works" and "I like using it" is where design now does its job.

Pink does not leave design to professionals. He suggests keeping a design notebook in which you record good and bad design from everyday life. When a badly designed appliance frustrates you, he says, treat the frustration as a prompt to sketch a better one. The notebook turns a passing irritation into something you have looked at closely enough to draw. He also recommends following design magazines and museums.

The chapter gives four basic principles of graphic design: contrast, repetition, alignment and proximity. Anyone can check a page or a slide against them, asking of each one whether it is there and whether it helps the reader.

So the first step is small. You look at ordinary things, notice which ones help you and which ones fight you, and write it down. The sketch can be rough. The habit is the point.

[[lessons]]
$$**Write down what annoys you in everyday things.** Someone who jots down each time a form, an app screen or a kettle handle gets in the way will have, after a few weeks, a list of real design problems and some ideas for fixing them. Pink's notebook works like this: the irritation is the raw material. Say what went wrong and sketch the fix, even badly. At work, the same list gives you something concrete to bring to a meeting, and you will have seen each problem more than once instead of guessing at it.$$

The barrier in the box was low enough for any dog to jump, and getting over it ended the shock. In a University of Pennsylvania lab in the mid-1960s, many of the dogs never tried. They had been through something the others had not, and it was not more pain.

[[story]]
The dogs had first been held in a hammock and given shocks that followed a tone. A day later they went into a shuttlebox, a box split by a low barrier. One jump and the shock stopped. Many simply lay down and waited it out.

Martin Seligman and Steven Maier built a test to find out why, and published it in 1967. Dogs were wired in pairs. One dog could stop the shock by pressing a panel with its nose, and the shock then stopped for both. The second dog, yoked to the first, got exactly the same shocks for exactly as long, but nothing it did changed anything. A third group got no shock at all.

Then all of them went into the shuttlebox. About 90 percent of the panel dogs and the unshocked dogs learned to jump the barrier with ease. ##About two-thirds of the yoked dogs never learned to clear it.## They had felt no more pain than their partners. What they had learned was that their own actions made no difference.

Seligman called it learned helplessness and began tracing its link to depression. Donald Hiroto ran a human version with loud noise instead of shocks. One group could silence it by pressing buttons, a second heard the same noise with no way to stop it, and a third heard nothing. In a new setup, moving a hand was enough to turn the noise off. Most of the no-control group did not even try, and some explained that nothing had worked, so why bother.

Yet in every study a minority refused to give up. Seligman later said that about one in three people in his lab could never be made helpless. *Learned Optimism* (1990) grew out of that puzzle: what those people were doing differently.

One correction belongs here. In 2016 Maier and Seligman wrote that their original theory "got it backwards." Passivity in the face of long, inescapable shock is not learned; it is the brain's default. What gets learned is control. The dog that pressed the panel had its brain record that its action worked, and that record shaped how it met the next ordeal.

[[lessons]]
$$**Test the barrier before you accept it.** A stretch where effort changed nothing can leave you treating a new situation as if it were the old one. Someone whose idea was turned down twice by a previous manager may never pitch to the new one, and may not even notice that staying quiet has become a choice. When you catch yourself saying "there's no point," ask what is actually different now and run one small, cheap test. You then learn what is in your control from a fresh result instead of from an old memory of failing.$$

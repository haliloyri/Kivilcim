"My first mountain was an insanely lucky one." The writer says it about his own career, a career that went far better than planned, then spends pages on what the climb did to the way he treated the people closest to him.

[[story]]
David Brooks says in *The Second Mountain* that many lives have a two-mountain shape. On the first mountain, people establish an identity, cultivate their talents, build a secure ego and try to make a mark. They are always keeping score: how do I measure up, where do I rank? The goals are the ones the culture endorses, which means success, being well thought of, the right social circles and personal happiness.

Some reach the top and find it unsatisfying. Others get knocked off by a failure, a death, a cancer scare or an addiction. Brooks says they end up in the valley.

His own first mountain, he writes, achieved far more professional success than he ever expected. The climb also made him aloof, invulnerable and uncommunicative. He sidestepped the responsibilities of relationship, and he lists his failures as failures of omission: evasion, workaholism, conflict avoidance. Two old friends live 250 miles away, and for them he was too busy and too distant.

##Then came the summer of 2013, and his divorce.## He describes himself as lonely, humiliated and adrift, with a constant burning in his stomach and gut. He and his ex-wife agreed not to discuss the marriage or the divorce in public. In seasons of suffering, he writes, you grip the steering wheel tighter, and sometimes you are defeated and let go of the wheel. Strange things begin happening.

He describes the second mountain as the journey after the first, one that does not require rejecting it. The test is where your ultimate appeal lies: to yourself, or to something outside yourself.

The first mountain is about building the ego, acquiring things, and being ambitious, strategic and independent. The second is about shedding the ego and contributing, and it is relational and intimate. You conquer the first mountain. The second conquers you.

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask where your ultimate appeal goes.** A shopkeeper proud of her business and busy all week may realise one evening that she has not visited a friend in years, and she writes a single visit into her calendar. She keeps the success and adds to it a direction that comes from outside herself, so she holds on to what the first climb gave her. For someone used to keeping score, that is a small, concrete step toward the second mountain, and it asks for nothing to be given up first, not even the pride in the shop itself.$$

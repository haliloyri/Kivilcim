[[open]]
A grain of rice on the first square of a chessboard, two on the second, four on the third. The inventor who asked for this reward wanted nothing more. Two economists use his request to explain why computers keep surprising us, and why the surprise gets bigger every year.

[[story]]
In *The Second Machine Age* (2014), Erik Brynjolfsson and Andrew McAfee retell an old tale as a measuring stick. An inventor asks for a modest reward: one grain of rice on the first of a chessboard's 64 squares, two on the next, and so on, doubling each time. It sounds like a few handfuls, a price any emperor could pay.

By the 32nd square the total is about 4 billion grains, roughly the yield of one large rice field. By the 64th it is more than 18 quintillion, a pile taller than Mount Everest. Some versions of the story end with the emperor having the inventor executed.

The futurist Ray Kurzweil called the stretch after the 32nd square the second half of the chessboard, and the authors take up the phrase for their book. Counting from 1958, when the first silicon transistors were put on the market, they argue that digital technology has entered that second half, where the numbers outrun our ability to picture them.

The doubling has a name. In 1965 Gordon Moore noticed that the number of transistors on a chip was doubling on a regular schedule, later put at about every two years. Moore's Law is an observation, not a law of physics, and it has held because engineers keep finding a way around each barrier.

The authors make the doubling visible with hardware. A 1982 Osborne Executive was 500 times larger, 100 times heavier and ten times more expensive than a 2007 iPhone, and one hundredth as fast. The 2011 iPad 2, a tablet small enough to hold in one hand, matched the processor speed of the 1985 Cray-2, a supercomputer that cost an inflation-adjusted $33 million.

The clearest case is the driverless car. In 2002 DARPA offered US$1 million for a robot vehicle that could cross a 150-mile course in the Mojave Desert. In the 2004 race no entrant finished, and the farthest went 7.4 miles, less than a twentieth of the course. Less than six years later, Google's autonomous car had logged hundreds of thousands of accident-free miles. Our brains, the authors say plainly, are not equipped to grasp sustained exponential growth, which is why that jump still surprises.

[[lessons]]
$$**Project a doubling trend step by step instead of by feel.** A bakery owner sees her online orders double every month for a few months and still plans next month's staff as if it will look like this one. Intuition draws a straight line through a curve and underestimates what comes next, so write down how long the doubling takes and extend it a few steps before you decide anything. Then you can see how soon today's kitchen, stock or staffing runs out, and you can decide calmly before that day arrives, not on it.$$

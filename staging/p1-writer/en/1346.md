[[open]]
"I never settle for second best." It sounds like a motto for a successful life. In Barry Schwartz's research it is one of thirteen survey statements, and the more strongly people agreed with lines like it, the more likely they were to report being unhappy.

[[story]]
Schwartz puts that survey at the heart of *The Paradox of Choice* (2004), but the clearest picture he gives of the idea comes from a friend's family. His friend and his wife kept fighting with their older daughter about clothes. She was style conscious, had expensive taste, and disagreed with them about what she absolutely "needed."

So they negotiated a clothing allowance: one lump sum, enough for a reasonable number of reasonably priced items, and she would decide how to spend it. It worked like a charm. The arguments about clothes stopped, and the parents could spend the rest of her adolescence fighting about more important things.

Pleased, they gave the younger daughter the same deal. ##The two girls turned out to be very different people.## The older one bought things she liked, often on impulse, and never worried about what she had passed up. The younger one agonized on every shopping trip. Was this item really the best use of her money? Would she regret it two months later, when the seasons and styles changed? Schwartz writes that this was too much to ask of a twelve-year-old, and that her "clothing liberation" brought her much worry and little joy.

He names the two styles. The older girl is a satisficer, someone with standards who searches until something meets them and then stops. Her sister is a maximizer, at least with clothes. A maximizer can only be sure she has the best sweater by looking at all the sweaters and checking all the prices, which is why the search never quite ends. Schwartz insists the satisficer is not settling for mediocrity. She is content with the excellent rather than the absolute best.

The word comes from Herbert Simon, the Nobel Prize-winning economist and psychologist, who introduced "satisficing" in the 1950s. Once you count the time, money and anguish of gathering information, Simon argued, satisficing is in fact the best strategy available.

Schwartz and his colleagues built the thirteen-item scale to measure the tendency and gave it to several thousand people. High scorers were less satisfied with life, less happy, less optimistic and more depressed; those scoring 65 or more out of 91 fell in the borderline range for clinical depression. He is careful to call this a correlation, not proof of cause, though he believes maximizing plays a part. Nobody maximizes in every area of life, he adds, so most people already know how to satisfice somewhere.

[[lessons]]
$$**Set your "good enough" before you start looking.** When the standard is fixed in advance, the search has a natural end and you keep some energy for enjoying what you chose. A person hunting for a flat who writes down "under half an hour from work, two bedrooms" and takes the first place that fits can stop opening new listings. Someone who starts without that line tends to reopen the decision every time a new advert appears. Noticing where you need a stopping point lets you save your searching for the few choices that deserve it.$$

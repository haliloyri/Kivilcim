Vasari says the painter Paolo Uccello stayed up through the night working out perspective, delighted with it. Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi uses that scene to show a trait that sounds like a contradiction, and he finds it in many of the creative people he met.

[[story]]
*Creativity* (1996) rests on in-depth interviews with about 91 exceptional people from many fields. Csikszentmihalyi went looking for what they shared, and the answer was pairs of opposites held in one person. He lists ten of them.

Uccello illustrates the first kind: playful and disciplined. Perspective was a hard technical problem, and Uccello spent the dark hours working it out, yet Vasari's account has him delighted with it. The discipline kept him at the table until morning, and the pleasure of the puzzle made the night pass.

The pairs run through the whole list. They are energetic yet restful: long hours of intense concentration, a fresh look, and quiet when at rest. They are smart yet naive, imaginative yet grounded in everyday facts, extroverted and introverted at once, humble and proud.

Flexibility about gender roles is on the list as well. In his tests, creative girls were more dominant than their peers, and creative boys were more sensitive and less aggressive. Rebellious and conservative is another pair. A creative person first absorbs an area of culture, then pushes against it. Csikszentmihalyi holds that without the first step the second does not happen.

Passionate and objective is a pair too. He quotes the historian Natalie Davis on detaching from her own writing so she can take criticism of it. Passion keeps the work going and distance keeps it honest.

The trait he found most consistent stands outside the pairs: the ability to enjoy the process of creating for its own sake, whether or not anyone is watching. He also draws a line on intelligence. Studies he cites suggest a cutoff around an IQ of 120, above which more intelligence does not necessarily bring more creativity.

[[lessons]]
$$**Use the half of a pair that you usually neglect.** A cabinetmaker who plays with shapes on Monday and checks every joint on Tuesday gets two kinds of work into one week. If you think of yourself as a planner or as a dreamer, give the other half one small job, and notice which half feels harder. That tells you what to balance in the next piece of work, and you learn it by doing rather than by deciding who you are.$$

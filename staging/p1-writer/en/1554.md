At the top of one team model sits a dysfunction with no villain in it: people who work hard and keep score of their own results before the group's. Nobody breaks a rule, and still the shared goal slips away.

[[story]]
The fifth dysfunction in Patrick Lencioni's *The Five Dysfunctions of a Team* is inattention to results, and he defines it plainly. It happens when team members put something ahead of the team's collective results: their own ego, their career development, their recognition.

Nobody has to act badly for this to happen. A person can put in long days and hit every personal target while the team's actual goal barely moves. By results, Lencioni means the team's results, not the sum of each member's.

It sits at the top of the pyramid because the other four layers feed it: trust, healthy conflict, commitment and accountability. If the lower layers are weak, he argues, people fall back on protecting their own standing.

He offers two practical remedies. The first is to state the team's results publicly, written where everyone can see them, so that falling short is visible too. The second is to tie rewards to the team's results instead of only to individual output, so the incentive points at the goal the team shares.

A team that gets this right, in his account, keeps its achievement-minded people, feels shared wins and shared failures sharply, and gains members who set their own interests aside for the group's.

His picture of the payoff is a boat. If all the people in an organization were rowing in the same direction, he says, a company could beat any competitor. ##The difficulty is that each rower can have a perfectly good reason to row a little differently.##

That is why this layer is easy to miss. In the book, the team's results are the thing everyone agrees matters, and the competing pull is a quiet one: a name on a title, a standing in the company, a personal record.

[[lessons]]
$$**Check what your measurements reward.** A shop manager who pays staff for their own sales should expect them to leave a customer to a colleague whenever the sale would not count for them. If the bonus also depends on how the whole shop does that month, passing the customer to whoever can serve them best becomes a sensible move. Before you ask people to cooperate, look at what the scoreboard pays them for, and change the scoreboard first, because people follow what is paid and counted.$$

A round pea and a wrinkled pea look like a small difference. For a friar in Brno they were the start of an answer to how traits pass from parent to child. He explained it to a local society and then waited for anyone to care.

[[story]]
Gregor Mendel was an Augustinian friar in Brno who grew pea plants and watched individual traits, such as whether a seed was round or wrinkled. In *The Gene*, Siddhartha Mukherjee tells of this garden as the place where a first clear clue about the gene was found.

The traits of a pea split cleanly into two kinds, round or wrinkled, tall or short, and that made counting possible. Mendel crossed a tall plant with a short one and counted the offspring trait by trait. In the first generation only one of the two traits showed. The other came back in the next generation, in roughly one plant out of four.

What Mendel saw was that traits do not blend away. They pass on in separate units. A trait could vanish in one generation and come back in the next.

He told this to the natural history society of Brno in two lectures, in February and March of 1865. In 1866 his paper, "Research on Plant Hybrids", was printed in the society's Proceedings.

It was largely overlooked. For decades it was cited only a few times. Mendel's results sat in a journal whose readers built nothing on them.

##The idea that heredity travels in separate units sat in a small journal for thirty-five years.##

In 1900, Hugo de Vries, Carl Correns and Erich von Tschermak each reached the same results independently and found Mendel's paper. William Bateson came to the 1865 work later, through a reprint of de Vries's paper.

Not everyone accepts the usual version of this story. Some historians argue that the claim that Mendel was entirely ignored is overstated. Even so, about thirty-five years passed between the 1865 lectures and the rediscovery, and that was long before his work became the foundation of the science of heredity.

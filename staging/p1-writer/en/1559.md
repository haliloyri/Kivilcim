A woman had spent many hours at her dying mother's bedside, reading to her, holding her hand, telling her again and again that she loved her. Her mother was mostly unconscious. Then, one morning before dawn, she opened her eyes and spoke a single sentence.

[[story]]
Tara Brach opens *Radical Acceptance* (2003) with a weekend in the mountains during her college years. She went hiking with an older, wiser friend of twenty-two, and after setting up the tent they sat by a stream, watching the water swirl around the rocks. The friend mentioned that she was learning to be "her own best friend." Brach broke down sobbing.

She was nowhere near her own best friend. An inner judge harassed her, merciless, relentless and nit-picking, often invisible but always on the job, and she would never have treated a friend that way. In her early teens she had imagined herself living inside a transparent orb that kept her apart from everyone, its walls thickening whenever she felt bad about herself. In the eyes of the world she was highly functional. Inside she was anxious and often depressed.

Under it all lay one assumption: something is fundamentally wrong with me. Brach calls living through that lens the trance of unworthiness. In twenty years as a psychologist and Buddhist teacher she has worked with thousands of clients and students, and whether the conversation happens at a ten-day retreat or in a weekly therapy session, the fear of being flawed is basically the same. It takes little to set it off. Someone else's accomplishments will do it, or a criticism, an argument, a mistake at work. A friend of hers put it this way: "Feeling that something is wrong with me is the invisible and toxic gas I am always breathing."

The woman at that bedside was Marilyn, one of Brach's meditation students. Late at night she would meditate beside her mother, who could no longer answer. Most of the time her mother lay unconscious, her breath labored and erratic. One morning before dawn she suddenly opened her eyes and looked at her daughter clearly and intently. ##"You know," she whispered, "all my life I thought something was wrong with me."## She shook her head slightly, as if to say what a waste, closed her eyes and drifted back into a coma. Several hours later she died.

Marilyn called it her mother's parting gift. Out of love for her mother and for life, she resolved to hold herself with more acceptance and kindness, because she did not have to lose her own life that way.

Brach adds a case that looks nothing like shame. A successful writer and teacher among her friends told her, "My main challenge isn't shame, it's pride," and described how easily she rides surges of feeling special and better than other people. Brach's point is that the trance wears more than one face.

[[lessons]]
$$**Listen for the verdict running in the background.** Most of us carry a steady sentence in the back of our heads, some version of "I'm not enough," and it switches on at perfectly ordinary moments. A person who goes quiet when a colleague announces a promotion, or who sinks after a curt email from the boss, often notices only the heaviness and never the sentence that causes it. If you catch it just as it starts, you can treat it as a thought you are having and decide what to do next with a little more room.$$

Three pages, by hand, before anything else in the day. No topic, no polish, no wrong way to do it. And one strange condition attached: for at least eight weeks, not even the writer gets to read them back. This is the first thing a teacher of blocked artists asks her students to do.

[[story]]
Julia Cameron began working with blocked artists in 1978. It started as one-on-one teaching, grew into workshops and then a course, and the course became *The Artist's Way*. The first and most basic tool in the book is called morning pages.

The instructions fit on an index card. Every morning, as soon as you wake, you write three pages of longhand, strictly stream of consciousness. Whatever is in your head goes down: last night's dream, the plans for the day, whatever is worrying or exciting you. Cameron insists there's no wrong way to do them. They are not high art, and nothing is too trivial to go on the page.

She is just as firm about skipping. Morning pages are nonnegotiable, she writes. Never skip or skimp on them.

The reason for all this looseness has a name. Cameron calls the inner critic the Censor, the voice that weighs every sentence and finds it lacking before you've finished it. ##By spilling out of bed and straight onto the page every morning, she says, you learn to evade the Censor.## Because there is no wrong way to write the pages, its opinion simply doesn't count. Beyond the reach of its babble, she writes, we find our own quiet center.

The second rule looks even odder at first. You don't reread your pages, and you don't let anyone else read them either, at least for the first eight weeks. The notebook fills up, and nobody opens it. Writing that has no reader has no grade.

Cameron describes her own writing the same way. She learned, she says, to just show up at the page and write down what she heard. "I didn't have to be in the mood." She doesn't wait for inspiration before she sits down. In her students, she writes, she sees a certain amount of defiance and giddiness in the first few weeks of the process.

The book doesn't pretend morning pages are good writing. It offers them as a daily habit that clears the noise before the real work starts. A blocked writer, painter or musician who does them has, at the very least, put three pages down that day without the pressure of making anything beautiful.

[[lessons]]
$$**Empty the noise onto paper before you start the work that matters.** A page with no standard at all lets you begin without waiting for the right mood, and it leaves your inner critic with nothing to grade. A manager who has been avoiding the quarterly report for weeks could first scribble three messy lines about whatever is bothering her, by hand, on any scrap of paper. With that clutter out of her head she opens the report with less resistance, because the hardest sentence of the morning has already been written somewhere that didn't count.$$

[[open]]
Before orbiting the Earth, an astronaut was not content with the computer's figures. He asked that one particular person check them by hand. She was a mathematician whose name often stayed out of the reports.

[[story]]
The Soviets had sent Yuri Gagarin into orbit a year earlier, in 1961. The Mercury programme was racing to put Americans in space. In early 1962 NASA was preparing to launch John Glenn into orbit around the Earth. The trajectory figures were being calculated by a new electronic computer, and many on the team did not yet trust the machine, because a man's life depended on it.

The flight was set for the morning of 20 February 1962. The capsule, named Friendship 7, would carry one man, and the numbers were meant to bring him back down.

According to Margot Lee Shetterly's *Hidden Figures*, before flying Glenn asked that Katherine Johnson check the numbers by hand. At a critical moment he wanted to turn from the machine to a human's proven work. Johnson had already worked out the trajectory for Alan Shepard's 1961 flight.

Johnson was a mathematician at NASA's Langley research centre, and she had come from the separate group of Black women who did the calculating. These "human computers" worked with mechanical calculating machines. Her group, West Area Computing, was run by Dorothy Vaughan. Johnson was forty-three and had come to Langley from teaching mathematics. Her name was often missing from the reports.

She checked the figures against the computer's output, line by line, and Glenn flew. In February 1962 the launch and three orbits were completed successfully. The flight lasted about five hours, and the numbers held.

Mary Jackson worked in the same unit. The group did its calculating at desks, one column after another, before most people trusted a machine to do it.

##What Glenn trusted was not the speed of the machine but a calculator who had proved right again and again.## One of the country's most visible moments rested on numbers verified by someone most people never saw. Shetterly's book gives that unseen work a name.

[[lessons]]
$$**Trust is built by work that keeps turning out right.** In an accounting office there is usually one person everyone ends up asking to look over the numbers before they go out. That person became easy to verify because they always showed how they got their results. You can do the same by presenting your own work in a form others can check. Over time that builds a record that people reach for when the stakes are high.$$

[[open]]
One large printed sheet, nine boxes and a stack of sticky notes. A whole business model has to fit on it, and a team standing in front of it has to be able to argue about each part, one box at a time.

[[story]]
Alexander Osterwalder began with a doctoral thesis. In 2004 he wrote about how to describe a business model precisely, supervised by Yves Pigneur, and by 2005 he was proposing nine building blocks for it. In 2010 the two published *Business Model Generation*, which its publisher says was co-created with 470 practitioners from 45 countries.

The tool itself is a single printed sheet, big enough for a group to gather around. Everyone looks at the same page. The nine boxes are called customer segments, value propositions, channels, customer relationships, revenue streams, key resources, key activities, key partners and cost structure. People write on sticky notes and press them into the boxes, or draw with board markers. Notes can be peeled off and pressed back into another box.

A layout organizes the sheet. Costs and revenues run along the bottom. The right side holds the market: whom you serve, how you reach them, what the relationship looks like and what they pay. The left side holds what it takes to serve that market, meaning resources, activities and partners. The value proposition sits in the middle, between the two sides.

Boxes depend on one another. Each box leans on the next. ##Move one sticky note and the notes around it start to shift.## Swap the customer segment, and the channels, the activities and the costs of reaching that segment may all need another look. The authors treat a model as a set of connected choices, so a change in one block can limit or strengthen the others.

The canvas has critics. The most common complaint is that it is static: the sheet shows one moment, says little about how a model changes over time or how its parts interact, and leaves the world around the organization almost out of the picture. A photo of a finished sheet shows today's model, not next year's.

Still, the sheet earns its place. It gives a group standing at a wall a pile of notes and one box to argue about at a time.

[[lessons]]
$$**Put the whole plan on one visible page.** A woman who wants to open a small bakery and keeps the idea in her head tells every friend a slightly different version. When she writes down who the customers are, what they will pay and what the oven and the rent cost, she and her friends can finally disagree about the same thing. She sees which part of the plan depends on which, and she can change one part before the rest is fixed. Someone else can also point at the spot she keeps missing, and say so out loud.$$

[[reflect]]
&&Which of your projects lives only in your head, and what would you notice if you wrote who it serves and what it costs on one page?&&

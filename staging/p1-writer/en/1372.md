[[open]]
A gift you never liked has, in one tidying consultant's view, already done its job. Its work was finished the moment it was handed to you. A shirt bought and never worn finished its work at the till. What is left is a small ritual for letting go, and a single test for everything that stays.

[[story]]
Marie Kondo sets out that test in *The Life-Changing Magic of Tidying Up*. She had been thinking about how homes store things since she was five, discovered the relief of discarding as a teenager, and started treating her belongings as if they were alive in high school. Later she turned the obsession into work, tidying other people's homes.

The rule she gives clients fits in one line. Pick up each item and ask whether it sparks joy. ##"The criterion is, of course, whether or not it gives you a thrill of pleasure when you touch it."## Looking at a shelf is not enough. The sweater has to be in your hands, the book held, the scarf lifted out of the drawer. She asks the same thing in other words too: does it speak to your heart, does it move you? In each version the decision belongs to the person holding the object.

That question cuts through the reasons we invent for keeping things. Kondo wants you to keep things because you love them, not "just because". When people cannot let something go, she writes, there are only two causes: an attachment to the past or a fear for the future.

For whatever fails the test, she offers a ritual instead of a guilty bin bag. The shirt you never wore gave you a thrill when you bought it, and in her view that was its role. A present's role was to be received. So she suggests thanking the item for the joy it gave when it arrived, and then letting it go.

She also boils our options down. We can face our possessions now, face them sometime, or avoid them until the day we die. The joy test is her way of making the first option bearable. In the book, discarding always comes first, and only afterwards does anyone decide where the remaining things should live. Everything from one category goes into a pile, each piece is lifted from the pile, and the decision takes as long as the item sits in your hand.

The result she reports reaches beyond the closet. After tidying, she writes, many clients tell her that their worldly desires have decreased. Even so, she insists that tidying is just a tool, not the final destination.

[[lessons]]
$$**Judge an object with it in your hand.** When we ask ourselves why we keep something, the mind always finds a reason, while the feeling that comes from actually holding it tends to be more honest. Someone clearing out a kitchen cupboard can pick up a mug she never uses and notice that she feels nothing at all. That lets her set it aside without guilt, because the small pleasure of buying it has already happened. The same test works for books, cables and clothes, and it turns a long argument with yourself into a few seconds of attention.$$

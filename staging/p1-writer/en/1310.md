A cup of hot coffee left on the table cools toward the temperature of the room. It cools fastest at first, when the gap is biggest, and slows as it gets close. That ordinary cup shows one of the two loops that run almost every system.

[[story]]
In *Thinking in Systems*, Donella Meadows describes two basic kinds of feedback loop. The first is a balancing loop. It pulls something toward a goal. Hot coffee cools toward room temperature, and its rate of cooling depends on the difference between the coffee and the room. Iced coffee does the same in reverse and slowly warms.

A thermostat works the same way. When the room falls below the setting, the furnace switches on, and when the room reaches it, the furnace switches off. A balancing loop notices a gap and works to close it.

The second is a reinforcing loop, which Meadows calls amplifying, self-multiplying, snowballing, a vicious or virtuous circle. It strengthens whatever direction of change it is given. Her first example comes from her childhood: the more her brother pushed her, the more she pushed him back, so the more he pushed her back.

The others follow the same shape. The more soil erodes, the fewer plants can grow, so there are fewer roots to hold the soil, and more of it erodes. The more she practiced piano, the more pleasure she got from the sound, so the more she played. In an economy, more machines and factories mean more output, and more output means more to invest in machines and factories.

##A bank account is the cleanest case: the more money in it, the more interest it earns, and that interest is added to the account to earn interest of its own.## Meadows gives a shortcut for any reinforcing loop. The time it takes to double is roughly 70 divided by the growth rate in percent. Put $100 in the bank at 7 percent and it doubles in about 10 years. At 5 percent it takes about 14.

Whether this is good news, she writes, depends on what is growing: money in the bank, or pests in a cornfield.

She sometimes challenges her students to name any decision that is made without feedback. Then she adds a warning. If you start to see feedback loops everywhere, you are already in danger of becoming a systems thinker.

[[lessons]]
$$**When something keeps growing or shrinking on its own, find the loop that feeds it.** If more of something leads to even more, or less leads to even less, you are inside a reinforcing loop, and you can choose whether to add a brake or more fuel. A runner who skips a week finds running harder, so she skips again, and she can break that loop with one very short run instead of waiting until she feels ready. Seeing the loop moves your attention from single bad days to the link that keeps connecting them.$$

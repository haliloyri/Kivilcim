At a rundown tennis club in Moscow, a player ranked in the world's top ten starts practice the same way as a five-year-old: bouncing and catching a ball. The club has one indoor court and a reputation far larger than that.

[[story]]
Daniel Coyle came across Spartak Tennis Club in Moscow while writing about places that produce outsized talent, and what he describes in *The Talent Code* looks poor on paper. The club has one indoor court, and by his account it is rundown and sits in a freezing climate.

In recent years, he reports, that single court has produced more players in the women's top 20 than the entire United States. That count covers only a few recent years, so it is a snapshot of a strong stretch, not a permanent record.

Coyle calls places like this talent hotbeds: small, often short of money, and turning out far more top performers than their size would suggest.

What happens on the court is plain. Every player, from five-year-olds to a world top-ten player, begins with the same warm-up: simple eye-hand drills of bouncing and catching the ball.

Then come slow-motion swings, and swings with no ball at all. The coaches call this *imitatsiya*, imitation, and everyone does it. A swing that is slow enough can be watched, corrected and repeated until the body keeps it. With no ball to chase, nothing remains but the motion, so any error belongs to the swing.

Players may not compete until they have practiced for three years, a long wait for a child who wants to play real matches. A Spartak coach puts the reasoning in one line: "Technique is everything." Starting to play without it, he says, is a "Big, big mistake!"

##Coyle treats this as deep practice: work broken into pieces small enough to be repeated slowly and gotten right.##

The place has no secret equipment to photograph. The distinguishing detail is what the best player in the building still does in front of the beginners.

[[lessons]]
$$**Rehearse the basic part slowly, away from the real thing.** A carpenter who keeps splitting the wood at one joint can cut that single joint again and again in scrap, slowly, before touching the good board. Repeating a small piece at a pace where every mistake is visible lets the hands learn what the eyes caught. You can use the same approach for the first minute of a presentation or the one tricky step in a recipe, and speed up only once it comes out clean, because a slow mistake is much easier to spot.$$

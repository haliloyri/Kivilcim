A needle is lost in a haystack, and a whole industry is paid to look for it. The research, the buying and selling and the bills that come with them all land in one place: the returns of the people who hoped the search would work.

[[story]]
John Bogle borrows the picture from Cervantes. In *The Little Book of Common Sense Investing* (2007) he says that choosing a mutual fund that will beat the stock market over the long run is, in Cervantes' observation, like looking for a needle in a haystack.

Every fund manager is a searcher in that picture. They research companies, they buy and they sell, and each of those steps costs money. The bill does not go to the manager. It comes out of the returns of the people who own the fund.

Bogle's arithmetic explains why the search is harder than it looks. Before costs, investors as a group hold the whole market, so together they earn exactly what the market earns. For one investor to beat it, another has to fall behind. After costs, the group earns less than the market by exactly what it pays.

He states the rule plainly and repeats it through the book: gross return minus costs equals net return. It does not matter how clever the searcher is. The subtraction happens either way, every year.

His corollary fits in one line: "Don't look for the needle in the haystack. Just buy the haystack!"

A broad index fund is the haystack. It owns the whole market instead of betting on which few stocks will come out on top, and it charges little because there is no search to pay for. The owner takes what the market returns, less a small fee.

The book's own subtitle makes the promise: the only way to guarantee your fair share of stock market returns. Bogle does not say that a particular searcher will fail. He says the one who owns the haystack cannot lose the needle, because the needle is already in the pile.

[[lessons]]
$$**Hold the whole pile when you cannot tell which piece will win.** A shopper who cannot tell which apple in a crate is the best can buy the whole crate at the normal price rather than hire someone to taste each one. When picking is charged to you and nobody can show they pick better ahead of time, owning broadly is the cheaper way to an ordinary result. Before you pay anyone to find the standout, ask what they can show about finding it in advance.$$

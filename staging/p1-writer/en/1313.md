[[open]]
A fifty-two-year-old salesman with a milkshake machine to sell walks into a hamburger stand in San Bernardino, California. The two brothers who run it might buy one. Behind the counter, though, he sees something that makes the machine the least interesting thing in the room.

[[story]]
Michael E. Gerber gives this salesman a chapter in *The E-Myth Revisited*. His name was Ray Kroc, and on that day he was simply there to sell the brothers a milkshake machine.

What he saw stopped him. In Gerber's words the stand worked "like a Swiss watch." Hamburgers came out quickly, efficiently, inexpensively and identically. Best of all, anyone could do it. High school kids were working with precision under the owners' supervision, while a long line of customers waited outside.

What Kroc saw, Gerber says, was a money machine, and he felt a passion for it he had never quite felt before. Soon after that first visit he convinced the brothers to let him franchise their method, and years later he bought them out.

Franchising itself was old by then, more than a hundred years old in Gerber's telling, used by companies like Coca-Cola and General Motors. What Kroc built was different: a Business Format Franchise. The franchisee got more than a famous name. He got an entire system for running the business. ##"What Ray Kroc understood at McDonald's was that the hamburger wasn't his product. McDonald's was."##

That shift came from a practical problem. Kroc had a huge dream and very little money, and the franchisee became the way to pay for it. So the franchisee was his first customer, and that customer didn't care much about burgers or fries. He wanted to know one thing: "Does it work?"

Gerber adds a second reason. Given how often small businesses fail, Kroc must have realized that a franchisee left to his own devices would struggle. So he set out to build a business that depended on systems, not on people, one that could work without him. Gerber's phrase is that Kroc "went to work on his business, not in it," treating McDonald's the way an engineer treats a prototype before mass production, much as Henry Ford must have thought about the Model T.

In the book, Sarah the pie baker pushes back. Her aunt, who taught her to bake, would have seen McDonald's as the opposite of a model. Gerber answers that McDonald's keeps its promise every single time. Her aunt had one kitchen, where she loved to work with Sarah by her side. Kroc had thousands.

[[lessons]]
$$**Write down how the work is done so someone else can do it the same way.** Quality you can count on usually comes from a method anyone can follow, rather than from the one person who happens to know the trick. A café owner who writes the morning opening routine on a single page, from the grinder settings to the order of the chairs, lets a new barista open the shop the way she would. When she is ill or away the shop still opens on time, and when something goes wrong she can look at the page instead of guessing who forgot what.$$

[[open]]
Developers at a software company were handed a day to build anything outside their usual work, on one condition: show it to everyone when the time ran out. The clock was set at 24 hours, and the clock was the only rule that mattered.

[[story]]
Atlassian, a software company, held its first FedEx Day in 2005. The name borrows FedEx's promise to ship overnight, and that is the rule: participants get 24 hours to come up with something, build it and deliver it. Daniel Pink's *Drive* (2009) uses the practice as an example of autonomy at work.

The brief is loose on purpose. One account of the 2005 version says developers had about a day and a half to build something out of the ordinary, and that they showed the result to their colleagues at the end. The aim was to break daily routines, try unfamiliar technology and take on a small project, often with a coworker.

##The topic belonged to the developers. The deadline did not.## Nobody had to justify the choice in advance, and the only thing everyone owed was something to show when the 24 hours were up.

Pink breaks autonomy into four parts: what you work on, when you work, how you do it and who you do it with. A FedEx Day hands over the first of these, the choice of task, and in practice often the last, a coworker to build with, while the clock stays fixed. The one fixed edge is what makes the rest of the freedom easy to use.

Pink's larger argument sits behind the day. He holds that motivation rests on autonomy, mastery and purpose, and that the desire to be self-directed raises engagement over mere compliance. A FedEx Day is a small experiment in the first of the three, run in an office, over a single day, with something to deliver at the end.

The format was modeled on Google's 20 percent time, in which engineers spend a share of their week on projects of their own choosing. Atlassian squeezed the same idea into a single sprint with a hard stop.

Companies that run these days report expanded skills, new collaborations and renewed excitement about projects, though the accounts give no measured figures. Atlassian now calls the event ShipIt Day, and other companies, a few of them crediting *Drive*, run their own.

[[lessons]]
$$**Give people a small piece of time they own, with one fixed edge.** A restaurant owner lets the kitchen team cook anything off the menu on one Tuesday and asks only that every dish reach the counter before closing. The cooks choose the dish, the partner and the method, and the deadline keeps the day from drifting into nothing. After a few Tuesdays she can see what her team cares about and where they are stronger than the printed menu allows them to be. She does not need a meeting to learn any of that, only a clear end time and a place to put the plates.$$

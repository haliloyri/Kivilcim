A pager went off at a random moment, and whoever carried it had to stop and write down what they were doing and how they felt. Some beeps caught people at their desks, others in front of the television. The entries held a result that hardly anyone filling them in would have predicted.

[[story]]
In *Finding Flow*, Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi describes how his team gathered the evidence. People wore pagers that signalled them at random times, several times a day, and each time they stopped to record what they were doing and how they felt. The signal arrived without warning, so the entries caught people in the middle of whatever they happened to be doing, whether that was a shift or a sofa.

At work, people more often reported the state he calls flow: a task that stretched their skills and held their whole attention. And yet, asked what they wanted, they said they would rather be doing something else. He calls this the paradox of work. The job felt better than it sounded, and the pager entries showed it clearly.

Free time told a different story. In front of the television, or simply resting, people described weaker concentration and a flatter mood than they had on the job, and many of them still wanted more of those hours.

##The hours people chose were not the hours that felt best.##

Csikszentmihalyi's reading is that open time asks more of a person than it seems to. With no goal and no feedback, attention has nothing to hold on to, and in his view it tends to drift toward worries. A finished piece of work, or a task with a visible result, gives it somewhere to land. Rest alone does not do that, however long it lasts.

His conclusion is that a good moment depends on an activity that offers a challenge and a sign of progress, and in his account more rest and fewer responsibilities do not supply those. His pager data pointed that way too, since flow showed up more often at work.

[[lessons]]
$$**Give free time a small challenge.** An empty Sunday afternoon often slides into scrolling on the sofa, and by evening the person feels less rested than he expected. If he picks one thing with a clear end, such as fixing a loose cupboard hinge or learning a new recipe, the afternoon has a goal and a moment when he can see it is done. That lets him notice which kinds of free time actually leave him better off, and he can plan more of those the following weekend. Choosing the activity ahead of time matters, because deciding in the middle of the afternoon usually means deciding on the sofa.$$

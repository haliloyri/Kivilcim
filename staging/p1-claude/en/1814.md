A company's best specialists put a problem online that they had failed to solve for years. The answer came from someone who did not work anywhere near that field, and nobody had expected it from there.

[[story]]
In *Range* (2019), David Epstein describes a platform called InnoCentive. Companies posted the research problems they could not solve, and people from all over the world tried to send in solutions, from chemistry and engineering to biology and physical science.

The results were surprising. A chemistry problem was often solved not by a chemist but by a physicist or a biologist looking at the subject from outside.

##The farther the solver's expertise lay from the field of the problem, the likelier the solver was to crack it.##

This was the finding of a study. At first glance it seems irrational, because someone who gave their life to one field should do best in that field.

Epstein explains it in two steps. The expert knows the familiar solution routes of the field very well. When those routes are blocked, nothing else comes to mind. The outsider brings an idea that is ordinary in his own field. In this new field it has never been tried, and it makes the difference.

A solution known in one distant field can be the key to an unsolvable problem in another, as long as someone places the two fields side by side. Epstein's emphasis is this: deep expertise is needed to see a problem correctly, but what solves it is often a viewpoint carried in from far away.

So the person standing in the very middle of a problem can be the person farthest from its solution. Whoever knows the field best also carries its habits best. The stranger is tied to none of them.

The logic of the finding is simple. Those who know the problem best are also the most attached to its definition. Someone working at a chemical company has heard "this is how the problem is solved" for years. An outsider has never heard that sentence, and so can look in another direction.

[[lessons]]
$$**Explain the problem you are stuck on to someone who knows nothing about that work.** If a kitchen team cannot work out why it is always late on the dishes, a friend who knows nothing about restaurants may ask, "Who takes the dishes, and in what order?" That question can open something the team never asked. The naive question makes the expert's habitual assumption visible. Even if it does not give the answer at once, it shows which side of the problem you have not been looking at.$$

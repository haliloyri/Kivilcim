Every morning the same partner knocks on your door and names a price for the same share of your business. Yesterday's price was high. Today's is close to nothing. The business itself has not moved.

[[story]]
Benjamin Graham asks the reader of *The Intelligent Investor* (1949) to imagine being a partner in a small, private business. The other partner is called Mr. Market. Every day he comes by with a price. Either he will buy your share at that price, or he will sell you his own.

The price swings because Mr. Market's mood swings. On a euphoric morning he sees a bright future and asks a high figure. On a gloomy morning he sees only trouble and will let the same share go for very little. Between those two mornings the business's balance sheet, its earnings and its day-to-day operation have stayed where they were. The only thing that changed is the mood of the partner at the door.

##You do not have to follow Mr. Market's offer.## You can hear the figure and let it pass, because he will be back tomorrow with another one. In Graham's words, Mr. Market is there to serve you, not to guide you.

What guides you is the business itself. You look at its balance sheet, its earnings and how it operates, and you form your own sense of what the share is worth. Then you compare each morning's quote with that value. When the price is far above it, which means Mr. Market is euphoric, you can consider selling him your share. When the price is far below it, which means he is gloomy, you can consider buying his share cheaply. On the days in between you close the door and go back to your own work.

Graham also writes that the investor's chief problem, even his worst enemy, is likely to be himself. The price does not push anyone around. The person who reacts to Mr. Market's euphoria or gloom is the investor. Warren Buffett is known to value this chapter.

[[lessons]]
$$**Read a price as an offer.** A used-book seller whose shelf copy gets a very low bid from one customer does not conclude that the book is worthless. She checks the edition, the binding and how many copies are left, and then answers by her own reckoning, saying no if she has to. When you read a figure on a screen as someone else's offer of the day, you give yourself time to work out the value with your own knowledge. Your answer then comes from that work, not from the number in front of you. The number remains useful, but only as one side's opening bid.$$

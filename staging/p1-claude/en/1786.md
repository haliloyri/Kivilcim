[[story]]
Oliver Burkeman spent years as a journalist writing about productivity and tried most of the techniques he recommended on himself. In *Four Thousand Weeks* (2021) he concludes that they never made him feel relieved. An average life lasts about eighty years. That works out to roughly four thousand weeks. Counted in weeks, a life stops being endless and becomes something you can number.

He calls the problem the productivity trap. Answer your email faster and more email arrives. Clear your to-do list and a new list takes its place. Becoming more efficient does not bring relief; it pulls in more work. For someone who had tried the techniques day after day, this was no abstract warning but something that kept happening.

##Burkeman's way out is to drop the dream of getting on top of everything and to accept that your life is finite.##

After that acceptance, the next step is clear. You make room for a few things that matter and you consciously neglect the rest. Neglect here is a choice, not a failure. In a finite life, every yes is a no to something else.

So missing things is the normal result of a finite life. The invitation you skip, the book you never open and the message you leave unanswered are all parts of the same price. Nobody gets to everything, and the reason is not that you are slow but that there are not enough weeks.

For that reason, Burkeman does not treat time management as a question of speed. However long your list grows, the number of your weeks stays the same. If you do not decide where your weeks go, your inbox and other people's requests decide for you.

[[lessons]]
$$**Treat time management as deciding what you will leave undone.** A tailor in a small workshop who accepts every order may end up neglecting the work he cares about most. If he decides at the start of the month which jobs he will take, he pays the price of the ones he turns down knowingly. What you miss then stops being a surprise, because you chose to leave it.$$

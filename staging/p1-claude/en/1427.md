[[open]]
A physician with twenty years of experience and one with five see the same patient. Most people would say, without thinking, which of them gives better care. A large review of how doctors actually practise did not agree.

[[story]]
Geoff Colvin's *Talent Is Overrated* (Yetenek Yanılgısı, 2008) tests that instinct with a study. In 2005 Choudhry, Fletcher and Soumerai published a systematic review in the *Annals of Internal Medicine* that compared physicians' years in practice with the quality of care they gave. On most measures the relationship ran the wrong way. The more years a doctor had been practising, the further the care drifted from current evidence.

Medicine suits this kind of test because the quality of care can be measured by how closely it matches current evidence. The yardstick is not seniority or reputation. It is whether the patient gets what the evidence supports.

The review shows that experience does not automatically produce competence. One plausible reason is that medical knowledge renews quickly while routine reinforces old habits. Year after year the doctor sees more patients, but often with the same method.

##Colvin separates routine practice from deliberate practice.## Routine practice is repetition. Deliberate practice is work aimed at a weak point, guided by feedback, and pushing past the current limit. The calendar cannot tell them apart. The same twenty years can mean twenty years of learning for one person and one year lived twenty times for another.

Deliberate practice is uncomfortable because it happens outside the comfort zone. Where it feels easy, nothing may be changing.

A caveat belongs here. Colvin's book appeared in 2008 and its central thesis has weakened since. A 2014 meta-analysis by Macnamara, Hambrick and Oswald found that deliberate practice explained about 26 percent of performance differences in games, 21 percent in music, 18 percent in sports, 4 percent in education and under 1 percent in professions. That does not mean practice is useless. It means practice alone is not enough, and in professions it explains almost nothing about who does better.

What survives of Colvin is a modest observation: counting years measures time, not progress. The review of physicians supports that much.

[[lessons]]
$$**Counting years does not measure progress.** A manager who has given the same kind of presentation for a decade does it with the same ease every time, and that ease is exactly why nothing new is learned. Instead of saying how long you have done something, ask what was hard in it lately. The answer shows far more clearly whether you are actually improving, and it points to the one part worth working on next. A year of practice is worth something only if some of it was uncomfortable.$$

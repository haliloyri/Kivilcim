A computer beat the world chess champion Garry Kasparov. Most people read it as the end of human intelligence, but David Epstein drew a completely different lesson from the same event.

[[story]]
In *Range* (2019), David Epstein reads that 1997 defeat from another angle and shows that supremacy at the chess table does not carry over to every job. Chess is a kind world: fixed rules, the board before your eyes, calculable possibilities. In such a closed and clear system, enormous computing power can beat a human. What is surprising is not that the machine won but that it won so late.

Real life is not chess. Rules are blurry, information is missing, the future is uncertain. Machines struggle precisely in these wicked, open worlds.

After his defeat Kasparov proposed a format called advanced chess: human and computer on the same team. In those tournaments, the winner against even the strongest computer was neither the strongest human nor the strongest machine.

##Even the strongest computer was beaten by teams of broad-minded humans working with ordinary computers.##

The human contribution was not raw computing power. It was context the machine could not see, strategy, and a sense of which question mattered. The narrow, deep calculation belonged to the machine; the broad, flexible, context-building mind belonged to the human.

Epstein's lesson is plain. In closed and clear systems, expertise and automation win. But most of the world is not closed. There, broad and adaptable thinking is still indispensable.

This does not belittle the chess defeat. It only shows which job suits which kind of intelligence. A machine often does better a job with clear rules and fast, honest feedback. Deciding which question should be asked is still a human's job.

The format did not mean that the human merely suggested moves. The machine ranked possibilities and the human decided which fit the context. Each side filled in what the other lacked.

[[lessons]]
$$**Spend your time on uncertain decisions, not on jobs with clear rules.** An accountant sorting and classifying receipts does a job a computer can do, but deciding which investment to recommend to which client is an uncertain job. If you work at mastering what a calculator can do, you may lose your value. Build your real value where you read context and find the right question.$$

The moment danger is sensed, the body stops digestion and puts growth and reproduction on hold. According to Robert Sapolsky, these sudden postponements become the costliest line in your health once they last too long.

[[story]]
In *Why Zebras Don't Get Ulcers* (1994), Robert Sapolsky describes the stress response as resource management. When the brain senses danger, adrenaline and cortisol are released. The heart speeds up, sugar and fat are sent to the muscles and blood pressure rises. The body gets ready to run or fight. Energy is limited here, so it is spent on the present first and the future waits.

The preparation has a price. Long-term projects are postponed: digestion slows, growth and reproduction are put on hold, and parts of immune function are pushed back. The body suspends everything that is not needed to survive right now. That is no malfunction. It is an unconscious ranking, with the heart and muscles first and tomorrow's jobs last.

The two hormones work at different speeds. Adrenaline acts within seconds. Cortisol arrives a little later and lasts longer. For a short flight the arrangement is efficient, because once the danger passes the system switches off and the postponed jobs resume. A few minutes of chase is a manageable expense for the body.

##The problem is when the system stays on for weeks.##

According to Sapolsky, prolonged high levels of stress hormones are linked to strained blood vessels and blood pressure, weaker immunity, effects on memory-related areas of the brain, and effects on digestion. Each of these is the bill for a job that was put off during the emergency.

That is why Sapolsky ties many stress-related illnesses to the length of the response, not the response itself. The same mechanism that saves a life in a chase leaves postponed repairs waiting in line when it never switches off.

The book's view does not blame the alarm. The alarm does its job. The real question is when it shuts off. A short alarm is an ordinary event for the body. During one that lasts weeks, every postponed job piles up.

[[lessons]]
$$**Postponed repairs eventually come due.** An accountant who has worked late for months is not doing the maintenance his body skipped meanwhile, and it can come back one morning as exhaustion or constant colds. A system that lives in permanent emergency always pushes sleep and rest to next week. Write recovery into the week itself, not into some day after the work is finished.$$

Tiger Woods hit a golf ball on television at the age of two. Around the same years, Roger Federer was skiing, wrestling, swimming and chasing any ball he saw. Both reached the top, but their roads looked nothing alike.

[[story]]
In *Range* (2019), David Epstein sets these two childhoods side by side. Everyone knows Tiger Woods's story: a club was put in his hands almost as soon as he could walk, and his whole childhood was built around one sport. Over time that story hardened into a rule. Pick early, specialise early, bury your hours in a single thing.

Roger Federer skied, wrestled and swam, and he played basketball and football. When one sport bored him, he moved on to the next. He locked onto tennis much later. His mother was a tennis coach, yet she refused to coach him.

##Most elite athletes had tried several different sports before they found their own.##

Epstein calls this stretch the sampling period: getting to know yourself before you commit to a field. Early specialisers start fast and look impressive, while the benefit of a broad base shows up years later. That is why everyone tells Tiger's story and very few tell Federer's childhood.

According to the research Epstein cites, early starters lead at first, and late samplers often catch them and pass them. He explains it through match quality, the fit between a person's abilities and the work. Someone who tries many things has a better chance of finding that fit. Even if they fall a year or two behind, they close the gap once they land in the right place.

Epstein also suggests that Federer's varied childhood may have fed his flexibility on court. Movement patterns borrowed from other sports can open solutions that rivals, locked into a single mould, never see.

His point in this comparison is that speed can be an illusion. While other children were settling into a single pattern, Federer was still experimenting. Going fast down the wrong road usually costs more than starting late on the right one.

[[lessons]]
$$**Do not count a late branching-out as being late.** A student who changed majors four times may feel behind his classmates at graduation. What those four attempts left him is knowing which kind of work makes him feel good. The time you think you lost while changing direction can also be read as an investment in finding the work that fits you, and the research Epstein cites supports that reading.$$

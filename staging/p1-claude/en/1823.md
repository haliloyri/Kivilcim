A salesperson told a customer, "You do not need this product." On the day, that cost him a sale. Years later it was the reason he sold more than anyone.

[[story]]
In *Give and Take* (2013), Adam Grant explains that the most successful names in sales are often the most generous, the givers. Whatever comes to mind when we hear the word salesperson, it is a pushy, thick-skinned manner. Grant's data say something else.

These salespeople recommended to the customer not the most expensive product but the one that really suited the customer. Sometimes they even said, "You do not need this product," or "The cheaper one is enough for you." In the short run, that meant turning away a sale they could have had.

##The customer felt that nothing was being sold to them, that they were being helped.##

What happened in the long run was striking. Those customers came back. They sent their friends and relatives. They stayed loyal for years. Knowing that they had not been fooled even once turned them into long-term customers.

A taker salesperson wins once and often never sees the customer again. A giver salesperson may lose a sale the first time, but gains trust. That trust returns many times over, as recommendations and repeat purchases. The short-term loss turns into a far bigger gain in the long term.

This story is not limited to salespeople. Anyone trying to get something accepted should ask whether they can put the other person's interest first. Persuading is often not tricking or cornering the other side but bringing their real need together with what you offer. When that meeting happens, the sale comes by itself, and next time the customer looks for the same person.

The question these salespeople asked themselves was not how to sell but what would help this customer most. Changing the question changed the results. They turned the relationship with the customer from a single sale into a long bond of trust, and that bond became their real capital.

[[lessons]]
$$**Put the customer's real interest first.** A salesperson in a furniture shop who recommends an affordable option instead of an expensive sofa set for a small flat earns less that day. But after the customer is settled at home, she recommends the shop to people she knows, and next time she comes to him again. Trust returns more than a one-off gain.$$

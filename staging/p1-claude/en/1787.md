Nothing in the world is softer than water, yet nothing is better at wearing down what is hard and strong. An old Chinese text builds a whole way of living on that contradiction.

[[story]]
The *Tao Te Ching*, traditionally attributed to Lao Tzu, is made up of eighty-one short chapters. Who wrote it and when is disputed, and the wording differs from one translation to the next. The image of water, though, appears in nearly every version, and it works as a key to the rest of the book.

Water takes the shape of whatever vessel it is poured into. It resists no mold and, left alone, flows toward low places. The text says nothing is softer than water, yet nothing surpasses it in wearing down the hard and the strong.

##The text likens the highest good to water: it benefits all things and does not contend with them.##

The same book looks for strength in what is soft in other places too. A clay pot is made of clay, but what makes it useful is the empty space inside. However thick the clay, grain or water is poured into the emptiness. A room works because of the space between its walls. The solid parts only surround the emptiness that does the work.

Plants are another example. A living plant is soft and moist, a dead one dry and hard. In the text, stiffness belongs with death and gentleness with life. What hardens breaks, and what bends goes on. Placed side by side, these images say that strength is found in flexibility and not in rigidity.

That outlook joins the idea known in Taoist thought as *wu wei*. It is often understood as acting without forcing. The point is not to do nothing but to read the flow and move with it instead of pushing against it. Water does not keep hitting the stone. It goes around it and, over time, wears it down.

[[lessons]]
$$**Read the flow before you force.** Someone talking with a colleague who rejects every proposal and pushing on the same point again and again will meet only a wall that gets harder. If he listens for what the colleague is protecting and offers another route to the same goal, a way through can open without anyone giving ground. This approach may not pay off at once, but it keeps you from spending your force in the wrong place.$$

Tiger Woods's father, Earl, wrote a whole book about raising his son as a golfer. In it he says how much he loved teaching. When Geoff Colvin looked at top performers in other fields, he did not find this to be an exception.

[[story]]
Colvin's *Talent Is Overrated* (Yetenek Yanılgısı, 2008) studies the lives of people who reached the top in different fields. The pattern he finds is that none of them got there alone. Each worked for many years with someone who guided them. In Woods's case that person was his father, who wrote *Training a Tiger*.

##For Colvin, deliberate practice needs feedback, and feedback by definition comes from outside.## Judging your own performance objectively is nearly impossible. A musician may not hear a wrong note because the right one is playing in their head. An athlete cannot see a technical flaw from inside the movement. A writer knows what they meant and misses what the page actually says. The blind spot is the same in all three: your own intention hides your own output.

A coach does more than find mistakes. According to Colvin, the coach also designs the work: what to practise, in what order, at what intensity, and with how much rest. That plan is what makes deliberate practice possible at all.

Missing feedback does more than slow progress. Someone who rehearses a flawed technique for years reinforces the flaw, and fixing it later can be harder than learning from scratch. Choosing what to work on matters before counting how many hours were worked.

A caveat belongs here. Everyone Colvin looked at had already succeeded, and the book does not measure whether people who failed also had coaches. What we do have is this: nearly everyone at the very top had someone beside them.

What if you have no coach? The book offers no ready recipe, but the mechanism points the way. What you need is not necessarily a coach but a measure that sits outside your own intention. A recording, a video, a score or an honest colleague fills the same gap to a degree.

[[lessons]]
$$**You cannot see your own blind spot.** A manager who has given the same presentation for years only notices that half the room checks their phones in the second section when she watches a recording. An outside pair of eyes, or a measurement that does not depend on your intention, shows whether what you practise really works. Your intention hides your output from you, and that is exactly the gap an outside view closes. The sooner you invite one, the fewer years you spend rehearsing a flaw you cannot see.$$

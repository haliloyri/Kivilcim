When the best connector in Silicon Valley was named, the winner was not a flashy boss. He was a shy and modest man.

[[story]]
In *Give and Take* (2013), Adam Grant tells of an entrepreneur named Adam Rifkin. A magazine had named Rifkin the best connected person in Silicon Valley. Rifkin was not an aggressive networker.

His secret was a single habit: the five-minute favour. His principle was simple. For everyone, whether he knew them or not, he would do a help that took under five minutes, without expecting anything back. An introduction, a tip, a short piece of feedback, a connection that worked.

These small favours looked unimportant one by one. Nobody says, "A five-minute favour changed my life." But over the years thousands of them piled up.

##Rifkin did not build his network to use it; he built it to be useful to people.##

Rifkin never asked anyone for anything up front. But the thousands of people he helped did not forget him when the time came. Around him a wide network formed on its own, of people who felt grateful and ran to him when an opportunity appeared.

This is the point Grant underlines, and the paradox of it lies here: takers and matchers, who tried to build their network for gain, could not build one as strong as Rifkin's. People sense a calculation, but they do not forget a favour with no strings.

A five-minute favour is easy. Passing on a message, introducing one person to another or looking over a document can take that long. Rifkin's method was to see a favour not as a big sacrifice but as a small job that could be done every day. Because it was small, it was sustainable.

According to Grant, favours of this kind cost the giver very little time but can make the receiver's work much easier. Someone introduced to the right person may find, with one message, a door he would have searched months for. Rifkin noticed this and made the small favour a habit.

[[lessons]]
$$**Hand out small favours without expecting anything back.** Introducing a new friend at work to someone you know, or looking over a presentation and writing a suggestion, costs you five minutes. When you do it without expecting a return, the person you helped remembers it, and when the time comes may come back to you. That is why, in building a network, thinking about what you can give works out more soundly than thinking about what you can get.$$

[[open]]
Picture two young people. One locks onto a field the moment she leaves university. The other tries a few jobs, changes direction a few times, and looks from outside like someone wasting time.

[[story]]
In *Range* (2019), David Epstein compares these two roads. Our instinct praises the first: focused, determined, fast. We assume the second is scattered and late. The data say something else.

People who try different jobs and specialise late really do fall behind at first. While their peers run ahead, they are still searching. Even so, in the long run they often catch up with those peers and pass them.

##Early specialisers start fast but may lock onto the wrong work; late specialisers start slow but find the work that fits them.##

Epstein explains this through match quality: the fit between a person's work and that person's abilities, interests and personality. You cannot calculate this fit at a desk; you discover it by trying. Every trial makes clearer which job suits you.

Epstein also describes this as short-term planning. Many successful people do not lock onto a giant decades-long goal in their youth. Instead they ask "what suits me right now?", move in small steps and get to know themselves along the way.

In the right job, motivation and growth come more easily. In the wrong one, every step is a load dragged along by willpower. So a few years counted as lost may be the price paid for a lifelong fit.

Seeing a change of direction as failure is therefore a big mistake. Often we pay later for binding ourselves too early to a road we chose before trying enough and knowing ourselves well enough.

The key difference is this: to find a good match you have to try, and trying takes time. That is why the first years of a late specialiser are not lost years but measuring years. What tires you in a job, and what makes you work, you learn only by doing the job.

[[lessons]]
$$**Do not count a change of direction as failure.** Someone who spent five years in banking and then moved into training may earn less in the first year and feel behind his peers. If he knows himself better in that work and works with more drive, the real gain of the move shows up in time. How long you have spent on a road is not, on its own, a reason to stay on it.$$

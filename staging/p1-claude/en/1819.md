A researcher who studied engineers, salespeople and medical students found the group that was exploited and burned out the most. Then he looked at the very top of the success ranking and saw the same group there too.

[[story]]
In *Give and Take* (2013), Adam Grant divides people into three types. Takers think of themselves first. Matchers give as much as they get. Givers help without expecting anything back. Then he asks a simple question: where on the ladder of success does each type stand?

The most surprising answer concerns the givers. When Grant studied engineers, salespeople and medical students, looking at engineers' productivity, salespeople's revenue and students' grades, he found givers always at the bottom. They are the group most exploited, most burned out and most behind on their own work. They run to others so much that no time is left for their own tasks.

But when he looked at the very top of the ladder, he saw givers again.

##The lowest performers and the highest performers are both givers.##

Takers and matchers stay in the middle. Both ends belong to givers. How does the same generosity carry one to the bottom and another to the summit?

The difference lies in the way of giving, and Grant spells it out in the rest of the book. The giver at the bottom sacrifices everything, cannot say no and forgets his own needs. The giver at the top is generous too, but not naive. He knows how to protect himself, set limits and spot takers. What he gives is unconditional, but it is not unlimited.

So the question is not giving or not giving. Being a taker does not carry you to the top, but unconscious giving drags you to the bottom. What Grant describes throughout the book is learning to give without exhausting yourself. Done well, generosity can be one of the most sustainable forms of success.

Givers fell to the bottom because they neglected their own work. The givers at the top helped as well, but they also finished their own. Both groups had the same generosity, and how and how much they helped made the difference.

[[lessons]]
$$**Leave room for your own work when you give.** An office worker who runs to his colleagues' tasks every day may leave his own report to the evenings and start working at night. Telling them he will help at set hours each week protects his own work and also keeps the help going. For generosity to continue without wearing you out, you have to draw a frame around it.$$

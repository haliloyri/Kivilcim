Most people try to look strong: flawless, confident, free of mistakes. For a lawyer, showing weakness in court sounds like a disaster. One lawyer did the opposite.

[[story]]
In *Give and Take* (2013), Adam Grant tells the story of Dave Walton. For a lawyer, the real question is whether the jury trusts him.

Walton stuttered. When he spoke, words sometimes stuck in his throat. For a speaker, this is the greatest weakness one can imagine.

But Walton did not try to hide it. He told the jury openly that he stuttered, and spoke calmly and sincerely, at peace with his flaw. He did not pretend to be strong.

##The jury listened to him more and trusted him more.##

According to Grant, the reason is that someone who does not hide his weakness seems sincere. You become sure that he is not selling something but telling the truth. A person who looks flawless often sets off a defensive reflex.

Grant calls this powerless communication: admitting a flaw, asking for advice, showing hesitation, and being able to say "I don't know" when you don't. Takers take this for weakness and try to look strong. But what persuades people most is often sincerity and humility.

Showing weakness therefore becomes a kind of strength. The person in front of you sees you not as someone trying to control them but as someone listening to them. You win trust not with a show of power but with an open manner.

Walton's example shows that a flaw does not always have to be hidden. Some flaws grow the more they are concealed. Spoken aloud, they turn into an ordinary detail. The jury could now read the stutter not as a flaw but as a sign that the lawyer was at peace with himself.

Walton's manner let the jury see him not as a salesman but as a person with flaws. People keep their distance from those who look flawless, and move closer to those who speak openly.

[[lessons]]
$$**Name your weakness instead of hiding it.** Someone about to give a presentation for the first time who says, "I'm a little nervous, but I know the subject," may earn more trust from the audience. Hiding the weakness and trying to look perfect often makes the tension bigger. Speaking sincerely does not make you weak; it makes you trustworthy.$$

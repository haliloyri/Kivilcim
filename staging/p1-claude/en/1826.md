[[open]]
In business, promises are often tied to paper; people say "no paper, no promise". Jon Huntsman thought differently, and it cost him millions.

[[story]]
In *Give and Take* (2013), Adam Grant tells of the businessman Jon Huntsman. For a large deal, Huntsman had shaken hands with the other side and agreed verbally on a price. Months passed before the contract was signed, and in that time the value of the business rose sharply.

That decision was truly costly at the time, because the same business could now find a buyer at a much higher price. He could have proposed talking to the other side about a new and higher price. Legally he had the right; nothing was written down.

##Huntsman insisted on the price he had shaken hands on months earlier.##

This decision earned him millions of dollars less. For him a given word came before the signed paper. Once hands were shaken, a deal was a deal.

In the short run he lost serious money. But what he gained was far more valuable: an unshakeable reputation. In business everyone now knew Huntsman's word was more reliable than the tightest contract. That reputation opened doors for him. People were willing to do business with him on a handshake instead of an army of lawyers.

Grant ties this example to the givers' long game. For Huntsman the real gain was not the value of one deal but the number of deals that followed. A man who keeps his word at his own expense gets trust even from people he is meeting for the first time. That trust makes every new deal less dependent on bargaining and lawyers.

Of course this principle is not easy. Huntsman's cost was heavy, and not everyone may be ready to pay it. But what Grant describes is a short-term loss met by a long-term trust.

[[lessons]]
$$**Keep your word even when nobody forces you.** A craftsman who told a customer "I'll do it this week" and now wants to back out because prices changed could easily pull away, since the customer has no written record. If he keeps his word, that customer recommends him to everyone in the neighbourhood. Reputation is earned slowly and lost quickly, and in the long run it stays worth more than most gains.$$

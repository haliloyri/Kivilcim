[[open]]
A teacher was told that a few of her students would make an extraordinary leap that year. Those students had not been chosen by any test. Their names had been drawn by lot.

[[story]]
In *Give and Take* (2013), Adam Grant relays an experiment that shows the Pygmalion effect. In a classroom, the teacher is told that a special test has identified some students who will make an extraordinary jump this year. She begins to look at these future high-flyers with a different eye.

But there was no test. Those students were chosen completely at random; the names were set by lot.

##At the end of the year, the children chosen by lot had truly developed more than the others.##

The lot had given these children no advantage; what made the difference was the teacher's expectation. The reason is that she behaved differently without noticing. She gave more time to the students she thought talented, looked at them more warmly, asked harder and more nourishing questions, and when they made mistakes she did not give up but kept supporting them.

This is called the Pygmalion effect: someone's expectation of us truly changes our performance. A high expectation turns into a self-fulfilling prophecy.

According to Grant, this is the hidden power of giving. Givers tend to see potential in people, and that view actually grows the potential. Expectation works like a gift. The one who receives it rises to deserve it.

Takers, by contrast, sort people into useful and useless. This sorting often creates a self-fulfilling negative prophecy. A student expected to do little receives little effort. A student who receives little effort develops little. In the end the first judgement seems confirmed.

The delicate side of the experiment is that the teacher wronged nobody on purpose. Her expectation leaked into her behaviour, and her behaviour into the child's result. That is why Grant stresses that our expectations do not stay in our thoughts; they show in our actions.

So the result shows not that the children were born bright but that expectation turns into behaviour and behaviour into outcome. A child who shone in the teacher's eyes had found more chances to truly shine in class.

[[lessons]]
$$**Choose your expectations deliberately.** A master who thinks of a newly arrived apprentice, "this work is too much for him," gives him simple jobs, and in time the apprentice stays at simple work. A master who looks at the same apprentice and thinks, "he can do it," gives him hard jobs too and sees what he is capable of. What you expect of someone often shapes who they become.$$

In chess you see the result of a move within minutes. The result of a doctor's or an investor's decision arrives months later, and sometimes never.

[[story]]
The psychologist Robin Hogarth divided learning environments into two kinds, kind and wicked. In *Range* (2019), David Epstein uses this split to shake many beliefs about experience.

Chess is a kind environment. The rules are clear, the board sits in front of you, and patterns repeat. You make a move and see the result quickly. A person who trains for years in such a place even educates their intuition, because the world gives honest feedback every time.

Most of real life is not like that. In the world of a doctor, a manager or an investor, feedback comes months or years later. The same decision is rewarded one time and goes badly another time.

##Years of work in a kind environment make you a master; in a wicked one, the same repetition can lock in the wrong patterns even harder.##

That is why Epstein points out that in some fields the forecasts of very experienced experts turn out hardly better than those of beginners. Over the years, what grows is not accuracy but confidence. Confidence and accuracy are not the same thing.

As a remedy he proposes desirable difficulty. Learning that feels easy and smooth is comforting but often deceptive. Learning in which you face your mistakes and compare different situations lasts longer. In a wicked world the safe harbour is not clinging to one pattern but recalling different examples and saying "this looks like that, but it is not exactly the same".

So Epstein advises experts to step outside their own field now and then. Gathering examples from other disciplines gives a person a mirror in which to see their own habits from outside. The question worth asking is this: do I see the results of my decisions clearly and on time, or do I think I am always right because I never measure the results?

[[lessons]]
$$**Ask whether your experience gives you honest feedback.** A sales rep who has sold the same way for years, in a job where results arrive months later, can easily believe the method works. Yet other factors were at work during those months. Trust your experience in a field where you can see the result clearly and on time. In a field where you cannot, collecting examples from other disciplines lets you see your own habits from outside.$$

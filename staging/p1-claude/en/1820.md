Picture two teachers. One spends time only on students who look bright to him. The other makes time even for the student nobody else bothers about.

[[story]]
In *Give and Take* (2013), Adam Grant tells how some teachers choose talent in advance. These teachers look at a part of the children as stars and at the rest as mediocre, and they make the needed investment only in the stars. The others they quietly ignore.

Grant introduces a teacher who does the opposite. This teacher believes every student holds a potential that has not yet appeared. She gives time to students who look ordinary and whom nobody takes up, pushes them, deals with them one by one and does not give up easily.

##Unexpected successes came out of children who were thought mediocre.##

Grant's emphasis is that talent is not fixed. It grows with attention, trust and opportunity. A teacher who truly believes in a student gives more than knowledge; she gives a new belief about himself. After a while the child begins to look at himself the way the teacher looks at him.

This approach has a price. Giver teachers spend more effort in the short run, because believing in everyone is tiring. Giving each child separate time slows the flow of a class. In the long run, though, extraordinary results come from students nobody expected.

These teachers create talent instead of filtering it out. A star chosen early would have shone anyway. Making a student shine whom nobody expected makes a real difference.

Grant's distinction in this chapter is that the teacher looks at the class without leaving anyone out. Looking for talent means putting in work to grow it. And the reward for that effort often comes from the student himself.

The teacher's attitude works for the child as a kind of proof: if someone believes in me, maybe I can do it too. That belief keeps the child trying. A child who keeps trying keeps learning.

[[lessons]]
$$**Do not filter talent out in advance; put in work on it.** A manager who labels a new intern "mediocre" does not give him hard tasks, and over time the intern stays at the expected level. A manager who gives the same person hard, meaningful work and regular feedback often sees what he can do. Believing in someone is often a way not of discovering potential but of creating it.$$

[[reflect]]
&&If there is someone around you whose potential you have not yet seen, what do you think might change if you truly believed in them and gave them time?&&

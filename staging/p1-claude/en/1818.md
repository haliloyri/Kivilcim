In the twentieth century, intelligence test scores rose from generation to generation. Grandchildren scored clearly higher than their grandparents. Were people really being born smarter?

[[story]]
In *Range* (2019), David Epstein describes this rise as the Flynn effect. The answer is no: what changed was not inborn intelligence but the way of thinking. Tests no longer measured only knowledge, but also applying an abstract pattern to a new question. The scientist James Flynn noticed that modern people had learned to look at the world through abstract and scientific lenses.

Older studies show this. People who had never seen a school in remote villages were asked questions. Asked what two living things had in common, instead of answering with an abstract category, they listed concrete uses. To hypothetical questions they said, "I have not seen it, I cannot know."

##They were not stupid; they looked at the world through everyday, concrete experience, not through abstract classes.##

In their world, the meaning of a thing was what it was used for. They had no need of a category, a hypothesis or an "what if" question, because their lives did not work that way.

Modern education has taught us to think abstractly all the time: categories, hypotheses, rules. Once you put on these lenses, you see the world differently. And this way of thinking can be carried from one field to another.

Concrete knowledge is useful only where it was learned. An abstract pattern can travel anywhere. You can take the logic of one field and apply it to a totally different problem.

Epstein's inference is that the most valuable skill is not memorising one subject. Having abstract, flexible patterns of thought that apply across fields takes you further than knowledge itself. What Flynn's data show is that part of intelligence is about learning this point of view.

So the rise in test scores reflects less that people know more than a change in how they read the question. The villagers' answers are not a deficiency but the product of a different logic. Modern people have learned to approach a question with an abstract category, and that view can be carried everywhere.

[[lessons]]
$$**Keep what you learn as a pattern.** A restaurant owner who has learned the logic of stock-keeping can use the same logic in a library or a workshop. Someone who knows only "this is how it is done in our restaurant" does not know what to do in a new setting. Each time you learn something new, ask in which other job it could work.$$

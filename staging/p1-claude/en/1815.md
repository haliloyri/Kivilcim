When Nintendo released the Game Boy, its rivals laughed. The screen had no colour, only a faded grey-green. The technology inside had been known for years, and it was cheap and old.

[[story]]
In *Range* (2019), David Epstein tells of the man behind the Game Boy, Gunpei Yokoi. His rivals' devices had bright colour screens and more powerful hardware. Yokoi was not someone chasing the latest technology. Nor did he describe himself as a master of technology.

His philosophy was lateral thinking with withered technology: instead of chasing the newest and most expensive part, take old technology that had become cheap and reliable and combine it creatively.

This choice, which looked backward, gave the Game Boy what its rivals could not offer: near-indestructible toughness, far longer battery life and a low price.

##The colourful rivals whose batteries drained quickly were forgotten; the cheap, sturdy Game Boy that lasted for hours sold tens of millions.##

Children wanted a colour screen, but what they really wanted was to carry the device everywhere, not to fear it would break when dropped, and to play for a long time. Yokoi had seen this. The feature rivals prized mattered less in children's daily use.

The interesting part is that his biggest failure came when he did the opposite. The Virtual Boy, which pushed the newest technology, was a commercial fiasco. Yokoi's strength was not chasing the latest but combining what was at hand in ways nobody had thought of.

From here Epstein moves to the value of a wide view. Yokoi was not the deepest expert in any single field. He was an inventor who knew different technologies and could connect one to another. That left him an option that specialists miss: choose not what is new but what works.

For Yokoi the question was not how new a technology was but what could be done with it. The old parts had fallen in price and proven themselves reliable. A colour screen made a device expensive and drained its battery fast. The Game Boy did not pay that price, and that is why it became a device you could take anywhere.

[[lessons]]
$$**Combine the ordinary tools you already have in a new way.** A neighbourhood baker can, instead of buying an expensive machine, join the old oven he owns with a worktable his neighbour does not use, and turn out a new product. That keeps the investment small and keeps his attention on what the customer actually wants. What pays off is often not the newest thing but whatever fits the user best, and finding it is the real work.$$

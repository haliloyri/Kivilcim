Johannes Kepler was stuck on one question: why do the planets circle the Sun? He had no formula and no theory to copy. He looked for the answer far from his own field.

[[story]]
In *Range* (2019), David Epstein presents Kepler as a master of analogical thinking. In his age nobody imagined that an invisible force governed the sky. When Kepler could not find the answer inside astronomy, he borrowed comparisons from other fields.

He thought about candlelight filling a room: could the Sun be sending out something unseen too? He thought about the magnet, newly debated in those years: could a force that pulls at a distance, without touching, exist in the heavens as well? None of these comparisons came from astronomy, yet each let him look at a question that seemed unsolvable from a new side.

##Kepler did not cling to a single analogy; he set several side by side and compared them.##

His road was not straight. Epstein writes that he calculated for years, built wrong models and hit dead ends again and again. Each analogy lights up only one face of reality. Kepler's working move was to see which of those faces held together. In the end he saw that the planets move on elliptical orbits, not circular ones. It was an idea nobody had dared to propose.

From here Epstein turns to the limit of narrow expertise. A narrow expert thinks only with the tools of one field. Give someone a hammer and everything looks like a nail. Kepler, by contrast, used curiosities that ran from optics to music like a many-field toolbox within reach. In Epstein's account, the most creative solutions in teams working on hard problems often come from analogies that say "this is like that".

Epstein says it would be wrong to read Kepler's scattered interests as lack of focus. Optics, physics, music and seafaring were tools waiting at hand. He could not know in advance which would matter when, but each stood ready for a later leap. The farther an analogy comes from, the fresher the window it opens.

[[lessons]]
$$**Liken the problem that has you stuck to another field.** A manager who cannot work out why her staff produce no new ideas can think of a kitchen: not everyone works at the stove at the same moment, because there is order and rhythm. That comparison may lead her to look at who speaks, and when, in her meetings. The farther a comparison comes from, the more of the problem's overlooked sides it can light up.$$

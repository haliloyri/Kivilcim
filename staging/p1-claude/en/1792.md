The most aggressive males of a baboon troop died of the same disease. How the survivors chose to live was still remembered years later.

[[story]]
In *Why Zebras Don't Get Ulcers* (1994), Robert Sapolsky describes following a baboon group he calls the Forest Troop for years in East Africa. Because he watched for so long, he could compare the time before and after what happened. Like most troops, this one was run by the most aggressive males, who made life hard for those below them and set the general mood.

Then the troop's most aggressive males ate tainted meat from the garbage dump of a tourist lodge and died of tuberculosis. The remaining males and the females were more mellow. The members who pressed hardest on the others had disappeared at once.

##The troop's style changed, and it stayed changed.##

There was less bullying, more grooming, less tension. This calmer culture continued after the aggressive males were gone. New males who joined later adopted the same style over time. Sapolsky writes that the measured stress hormones of the subordinate animals fell as well. So the change showed up in the bodies of the baboons, not only in their behaviour.

This was not an experiment. It was an accident of circumstance, and Sapolsky presents it as a possibility, not as a rule. It does not mean the same thing will happen in every troop. It does show that a troop's level of tension is not a fixed law of nature and can change as a learned style.

What stands out in the account is that the change lasted. That newcomers also fit into the calm style suggests that a group's mood comes not only from the temperament of its members but also from habits that have settled in.

Applying this story directly to human groups needs care. Still, it is a reminder that tension does not depend only on the personalities of individual members. The order they live in plays a part too.

[[lessons]]
$$**A group's tension is a learned style and it can change.** In a team where every meeting is tense, everyone may assume it comes from certain people's temperaments. A small habit, such as each speaker first thanking someone, can change the mood over time. Starting it often depends on one person taking the first step.$$
